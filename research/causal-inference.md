# Causal inference in the research companion

The project uses natural experiments where historical institutions or events create a defensible comparison. The evidence records specify the exposure, outcome, counterfactual, and limits of each analysis. [Results by chapter](evidence.md) · [Data and scripts](../scripts/README.md)

## What a historical comparison can show

The 2021 economics prize recognized David Card's empirical work and Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens's methodological contributions to causal analysis. Natural experiments exploit variation arising outside a researcher's controlled intervention, including changes generated by institutions and policies. The event's being historical does not make it a natural experiment. Its assignment process must justify the comparison being made. [S103](sources.md#s103)

A causal model states which variables influence which others and what assumptions permit an effect to be identified. Pearl's framework makes these commitments explicit; a diagram records assumptions rather than certifying that its arrows are true. Identification asks whether the desired effect follows from those assumptions and the available observations. Estimation then concerns its magnitude and uncertainty. [S104](sources.md#s104)

For each argument, specify the unit of comparison, the change being investigated, the outcome, and the interval. A mine, a workshop, a person, and a research program are different units. The effect of cheaper delivered coal on engine adoption is different from its effect on net extraction, and both differ from the effect of additional resources committed to trials while fuel prices remain unchanged. Defining the question prevents one successful comparison from silently answering several others.

Then identify why some units encountered the change and others did not. If mines expecting rapid expansion obtained better fuel access, comparing them with slower-growing mines may confuse the effect of fuel with the prospects that prompted its provision. Historical correspondence, engineering records, administrative rules, and accounts of failed proposals become evidence about this assignment process. They help establish whether a comparison is defensible before a statistical method is chosen.

## Three approaches to identification

One possible design compares changes over time in affected and comparison groups. Difference-in-differences requires a defensible account of how their outcomes would have changed without treatment, often expressed as parallel untreated trends. Similar earlier trends are informative but cannot prove that counterfactual. Treatment timing and differing effects must be handled explicitly rather than hidden in one indiscriminate before-and-after regression. [S106](sources.md#s106)

Another design studies an assignment threshold. If a rule changes eligibility at a cutoff while other relevant conditions vary smoothly there, observations just to either side may identify a local effect. Precise sorting around the cutoff or another simultaneous change can undermine that interpretation. The result concerns the comparison supported near the threshold, not automatically every institution in the economy. [S107](sources.md#s107)

An instrumental-variable design uses a source of variation that changes exposure to a proposed cause. Its interpretation requires that the instrument have an effect on exposure, be appropriately independent of other causes of the outcome, and affect the outcome through the specified exposure rather than a separate route. Further assumptions determine which effect is recovered; the familiar local-average-effect interpretation requires that the instrument not move some units toward exposure and others away from it. Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin show why the resulting effect may concern the subgroup whose exposure changes with the instrument. A causal estimate is not automatically a universal law. [S105](sources.md#s105)

These are routes to investigate, not labels to attach to any convenient episode. An archive may support a causal model through other justified comparisons, or only partial bounds on an effect. If the relevant causes cannot be separated, the claim remains an association or an unresolved hypothesis. A detailed narrative can establish timing and mechanism without identifying the magnitude of a counterfactual effect. Its evidentiary contribution should be stated on those terms.

## An example from the history of steam

Consider a proposed study of the first engines. Suppose records revealed a change in delivered fuel costs whose timing and allocation were not responses to expected engine adoption. Investigators could examine whether exposed mines subsequently adopted fuel-hungry engines more often than defensible comparison mines. They would need evidence about earlier trajectories, ore quality, flooding, labor costs, finance, engineering access, and other concurrent changes. The causal model must distinguish prior common causes from consequences of cheaper fuel; adjusting away the latter could remove the very pathway being investigated. This is a proposed design for first adoption. The modern coal comparison measures a different outcome.

Even that design would answer only part of the chapter's question. Cheaper fuel could enable operation without explaining where the design came from. Adoption could rise without increasing the net energy obtained after construction and operation are counted. To test the proposed support for costly intermediates, examine committed resources, unsuccessful trials, the interval before useful operation, and the resulting capacity. Identify each link and investigate alternative routes by which the initiating change could have affected the outcome.

This separates a demonstrated local mechanism from the book's larger physical explanation. Evidence that energy access caused adoption would support one connection in the synthesis. Evidence that adoption increased extraction would support another. Neither alone establishes a maximum of entropy production. That stronger claim must predict the choice, rate, or development of pathways in a way that rival accounts do not already predict. If the physical quantities cannot be reconstructed well enough, the energetic claim remains unresolved even when an institutional or technological effect is well identified.

## Forecasting a technology from earlier records

A historical forecast asks what someone could have inferred *before* the outcome. Three levels of prediction can earn separate scores. An invention forecast specifies a design feature. An adoption forecast says who will build or use it, where and when. A thermodynamic forecast ranks the resulting rates of entropy production over a stated territory and period. Fix the date, the service to be delivered and the thresholds of success before opening the later records.

1. **Find the contradiction and put units on it.** Follow one stroke, one ton or one order through the existing system. An engine needs the same cylinder hot and cold in succession. A mine can raise coal faster than it can get coal to buyers. An office can add columns faster than it can enter and check the figures. Measure each operation at the volume the system must handle. Improving a stage with spare capacity can leave the output unchanged.
2. **Generate rival designs from components then available.** Give each required function a short list of ways to perform it. Try separating two incompatible functions in space or time, sharing a costly component, or adding a missing connection. Eliminate combinations that fail the geometry, energy balance, manufacturing tolerances or institutional requirements of the period. Include the incumbent, postponement and combinations of old and new equipment. Publish this list before admitting the later invention as evidence.
3. **Compare routes through assembly space.** For every feasible sequence, estimate the date it could first deliver the specified service reliably. Include the slowest chain of prerequisites, activities that can proceed together, scarce workers, finance and failed trials. Rank whole sequences. A step that takes time and supplies no immediate output can open a faster route to the target. Dynamic programming formalizes the comparison by valuing a decision partly for the options it leaves at the next stage. [Bellman’s original formulation](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.38.8.716)
4. **Calculate the energetic prediction independently.** Use the same initial resources, minimum service, system boundary and horizon for every route. Average the rate of entropy production over construction and operation, accounting for displaced uses, gains in demand and physical flows across the boundary. Record net useful power, the owner’s return and time to working capacity separately. A fuel bill gives a cost and a boiler rating gives a power. Neither records all the entropy produced in the district. If different paths win on speed and dissipation, report both. The strong claim that one route wins on both criteria can fail. [Thermodynamic proposals and limits](sources.md#s07)

There is a short calculation an engineer could make for any two candidate machines. Let a route take *b* years to build and then deliver steady net useful power *P*. Over *H* years from the investment decision, its output is `P × max(0, H − b)` in power-years. A rival can deliver more power once built and still supply less energy by the chosen date. Use a time-dependent rate and subtract construction inputs when comparing net deliveries. For the entropy claim use the independently estimated entropy production rate, in watts per kelvin, over that same interval. The speed and power of a prototype also need a route to adoption. Ask who receives enough of the gain to finance the next installation.

For a test beyond these illustrations, pick a population of mines from a directory written before the adoption period. Include mines that closed or kept their existing pumps. Use dated drainage, freight and engineering records to rank the mutually feasible investments at each site. Record the predicted first installation, its time window, and the ranking by district-wide dissipation wherever the physical data permit one. Freeze those predictions, then open the installation and output records. Score the share of sites whose first adoption and timing window were predicted correctly. Use the same sites to score a simple forecast based on profitability alone. The most informative cases are the ones where the forecasts disagree. If the energetic ranking loses repeatedly despite adequate records of alternatives, that evidence bears directly on the book’s claim.

## How to read the results

The research design should state its main comparison before selecting favorable results from the records. Historical research cannot erase the investigator's knowledge of famous outcomes. It can use contemporaneous descriptions of constraints, define case selection without requiring success, distinguish exploratory work from confirmation, and test implications in further places or periods. Failed attempts, missing records, and systems that persisted without the predicted transition belong in the sample where the question requires them.

For every estimate, investigate competing events, changes in who enters the records, and effects spilling into the proposed comparison group. Report uncertainty and sensitivity to plausible violations of the assumptions. Tests using earlier dates or outcomes the mechanism should not affect can expose a bad design; passing them does not prove every assumption. A small, imprecise estimate cannot establish the absence of a mechanism. A precise result incompatible with a specified prediction is much more informative. These are the book's requirements for assessing its proposed comparisons, not analyses already completed.
