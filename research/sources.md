# Working source ledger

This ledger accompanies the [fifteen-chapter manuscript](../README.md#contents). Each chapter’s notes point to the observations, documents and models on which its argument rests. The [outline](../editorial/outline.md) and [expanded ideas](../editorial/ideas.md) trace the argument. The [methods guide](causal-inference.md) and [methodological afterword](../manuscript/afterword.md) accompany the research. Earlier dated entries retain the numbering of the [twelve-chapter draft](../editorial/archive/twelve-chapter-outline.md) or the [eighteen-chapter plan](../editorial/archive/eighteen-chapter-outline.md). S170–S178 document additional primary material for the current edition.

## S01

**Constraint satisfaction.** Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, [constraint-satisfaction chapter](https://aima.cs.berkeley.edu/4th-ed/pdfs/newchap05.pdf); UC Berkeley CS188, [variable ordering](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/csp/ordering.html).

Supports the minimum-remaining-values heuristic and early detection of impossible branches. It does not establish a universal algorithm used by nature. Chapter 1's question about the creative role of bottlenecks is the book's extension from this limited example; the computational sources do not establish a general physical mechanism of creation. The opening Sudoku experience comes from the author's notes; no benchmark results or reconstructed dialogue are claimed.

## S02

**Assembly theory.** Abhishek Sharma et al., [“Assembly theory explains and quantifies selection and evolution”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06600-9), *Nature* 622 (2023), 321–328.

Starting point for assembly index, copy number, and the authors' account of selection. The index depends on construction rules and reusable components; it is not a direct transcript of actual history. Claims of universal applicability and biosignature specificity require independent scrutiny. Chapter 3 now presents a bounded account alongside the independent criticism in S32; broader validation and the controversy remain open.

The author preprint abstract was rechecked November 17. I75 makes a separate necessity claim linking recurring assembly to autopoiesis; copy number alone does not establish that mechanism.

## S03

**Metabolic innovation and historical contingency.** Zachary D. Blount, Christina Z. Borland, and Richard E. Lenski, [“Historical contingency and the evolution of a key innovation in an experimental population of Escherichia coli”](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0803151105), *PNAS* (2008).

Supports the example of aerobic citrate use in the long-term evolution experiment. Related mechanistic research: Erik M. Quandt et al., [“Recursive genomewide recombination and sequencing reveals a key refinement step…”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3926077/), *PNAS* (2014). Distinguish the initial innovation, its historical preconditions, and later refinement.

Blount identifies Tim Cooper as the person who noticed the cloudy flask. Lenski recalls suspecting contamination. The frozen samples showed that weak citrate use preceded the conspicuous increase in density. Caroline Turner and colleagues later traced how compounds released by the citrate users fed a coexisting lineage. [Blount, *Replaying Evolution*](https://www.blount-lab.org/uploads/2/3/0/3/23037222/blount_2017_-_replaying_evolution.pdf) · [Lenski interview](https://telliamedrevisited.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/interview-with-richard-lenski-in-microbiology-textbook.pdf) · [Turner et al. on cross-feeding](https://doi.org/10.1099/mic.0.001390)

## S05

**Stationary principles.** Richard Feynman, Robert Leighton, and Matthew Sands, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, [I.26, optics](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_26.html) and [II.19, action](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_19.html).

Supports the distinction between a variational characterization of physical motion and an intentional search for a goal. Stationarity is not a theorem that history takes the fastest route to maximum energy extraction.

The action discussion was rechecked for I66 and chapter 6's combined fine-tuning hypothesis, including its explicit distinction between stationarity and a minimum. The source supports the component principle; it does not derive cosmic parameter values or the universe's necessity.

## S06

**Physical asymmetries.** ATLAS Collaboration, [“Probing fundamental symmetries of nature with the Higgs boson”](https://atlas.cern/Updates/Briefing/CP-Symmetry-Higgs); CERN, [future Higgs research](https://home.web.cern.ch/science/physics/higgs-boson/future/).

Orientation to symmetry questions and open research. Distinguish parity violation, CP violation, electroweak symmetry breaking, and the unexplained cosmic matter excess. These sources do not establish assembled constants or dimensions.

## S07

**Thermodynamic proposals.** L. M. Martyushev and V. D. Seleznev, [“The restrictions of the Maximum Entropy Production Principle”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.2068) (2013 preprint). Roderick C. Dewar and Amos Maritan, [“The second law, maximum entropy production and Liouville's theorem”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.1088) (2011 preprint).

Relevant full-text passages were read: Dewar–Maritan's stationary-state argument under forcing constraints and its heuristic status, and Martyushev–Seleznev's local force–flux formulation and restrictions. Chapter 2 uses them to motivate and delimit the broader assembly conjecture, not to claim an established universal law of discovery or evolution. Minimum-production results likewise require their particular assumptions.

The current book advances a universal rate conjecture and an earliest-formation conjecture. Dewar and Maritan give a heuristic argument for stationary states of open systems. Martyushev and Seleznev discuss restrictions on the maximum principle. The two-reservoir balance yields a local rate for a specified heat flow. The forcing, time interval, feasible routes and historical generalization require separate arguments. [MIT, entropy generation by heat transfer](https://web.mit.edu/course/16/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node41.html)

## S08

**Energy-rate density.** Eric J. Chaisson, [“Energy Rate Density as a Complexity Metric”](https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~ejchaisson/reprints/EnergyRateDensity_I_FINAL_2011.pdf) (2011).

The paper compares energy flow per unit mass across systems. Section 5 was read for its emphasis on optimal ranges and its explicit refusal to infer a universal power- or entropy-production extremum from the data. It supports an energy–complexity research connection, not an independent proof of the manuscript's maximizing hypothesis. Wider evaluation of the metric and system boundaries remains open.

## S10

## S11

## S12

## S13

**Early Homo sapiens.** Jean-Jacques Hublin et al., [“New fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco and the pan-African origin of Homo sapiens”](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22336), *Nature* (2017).

Supports the deep history of our species, roughly 300,000 years. It does not support biological stasis, an idle early humanity, or one uniquely dated departure from Africa. ## S14

**Cooperation beyond humans.** Alicia P. Melis, Brian Hare, and Michael Tomasello, [“Chimpanzees Recruit the Best Collaborators”](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/AAAS/Melis_Chimpanzees_Science_2006_1555100.pdf), *Science* 311 (2006), 1297–1300, DOI 10.1126/science.1123007.

**Kaiping coalfield.** An 1878 dispatch includes a contemporary summary of Tang Tingshu’s survey of the district. Sections 3 and 7 describe water and mud in shallow pits and estimate how many carts the proposed higher output would require. The survey accompanied a proposal to finance new works. The forwarding minister described an earlier provincial scheme that failed when estimated outlays rose. Consul James C. Zuck subsequently described the restrictions on the railway, the bridges built for future locomotives, and the choice between drills and hand labor as the rock changed. One records an artisan named *ko-ki-da* delivering twenty-two pairs of new wheels and a single wheel. The other assigns a wheeled chariot, a horse and a cuirass to *ti-ri-jo-qa*. The palace’s ability to meet maintenance costs is an inference from such records. Radiocarbon modeling of steppe burial evidence supplies an independent chronology for early horse-drawn transport. ## S174

**Survival through a drought on Daphne Major.** Peter Boag and Peter Grant measured Darwin’s finches before and after the 1977 drought. The survivors disproportionately included large birds, particularly males with large beaks capable of opening the hard seeds that remained. Selection on differences already present can change a population quickly. The study supplies an observed case of selection and no direct estimate of the time required for abiogenesis. ## S175

**Electricity, waste heat and the rate for bare rock.** The International Energy Agency estimates 485 TWh of worldwide data-center electricity use in 2025. Total data-center demand rose 17 percent that year and the AI-focused subset rose 50 percent. Dividing 485 TWh by 8,760 hours gives approximately 55 GW for all data-center workloads. In steady operation nearly all on-site electrical work eventually leaves as heat. The IEA figures give electrical power and changes in demand. Recoverable heat and entropy production have different units and require further measurements or assumptions. [IEA, *Key Questions on Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Laine and colleagues’ table 3 records January 2016 electricity of 3,411 MWh at the Mäntsälä site, 663 MWh of heat supplied from the site, 331 MWh of electricity at the municipal heat pump and 1,042 MWh of heat supplied by the pump. The site’s measured 663 MWh is recovered waste heat. The different meters can be reported separately. The 744-hour month gives a mean site power of about 4.58 MW. These records cover the data center as a whole. [Monthly site study, table 3](https://www.sdewes.org/jsdewes/pid7.0301) · [Operating context](#s169)

The epilogue’s bare-rock comparison is an illustrative local calculation. Diviner measured the lunar ground’s day–night temperature variation. The fixed boundaries of 320 K and 300 K and the conductive current on the hypothetical lifeless planet are assumptions. A steady heat current of (3,411/744) MW produces about 955 W/K as it conducts across the drop. Irreversible conversion of the same amount of electrical work into heat at 300 K adds about 15,300 W/K. The two contributions together are about 16,200 W/K on the powered patch. Supplying a resistor with equal work produces the same contribution. A whole-planet comparison would include absorption and emission of radiation, internal heat sources, the origin of the electricity and displaced uses. [Vasavada and colleagues, Diviner measurements](https://doi.org/10.1029/2011JE003987) · [MIT, entropy generation by heat transfer](https://web.mit.edu/course/16/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node41.html) · [Maximum-rate proposals](#s07)

Langseth, Keihm and Peters used the subsurface temperature record from Apollo 15 and 17 to infer the local heat flow. At Apollo 15 the site value is 21 ± 3 mW/m². One hectare at the central estimate conducts 210 W. To match the Finnish building’s January electrical power would take about 218 km² at that flux. This compares power from the lunar interior with power supplied to a building. It specifies neither the Finnish site’s footprint nor the total radiation balance of a lifeless world. Applying an illustrative 320-to-300 K drop to the hectare’s flux gives 0.04375 W/K. The common final-transfer column throughout the [epilogue](../manuscript/book/epilogue.md) multiplies each listed power in watts by (1/300 − 1/320) = 1/4,800 per kelvin. Each row uses a separate hypothetical heat current of that size. Counting the two columns together would count the same power twice. [Apollo heat-flow investigators](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19770051977) · [Site and regional flux estimates, section 2](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025JE009458)

## S176

**Energy use by microbes beneath the seafloor.** James A. Bradley and colleagues (2020) combined estimates of reaction rates, available Gibbs free energy and cell abundance in global Quaternary-age subseafloor sediments. They report 1.50 × 10⁻²⁰ W per cell as the median for methanogens. Their 37.3 GW total includes the aerobic, sulfate-reducing and methanogenic zones. It is about 8,136 times the Finnish building’s January load. The modern cell illustrates an anaerobic metabolism. Its measured environment and the size of today’s sediment biosphere cannot supply the population or energy budget of the first living Earth. [Study and figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32821818/) · [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7406382/)

For the cell, full dissipation of the available chemical free energy with no storage or exported work at an assumed 300 K gives 1.50 × 10⁻²⁰ / 300 = 5.0 × 10⁻²³ W/K. This estimate uses chemical free energy in the calculation of irreversible loss. The separate heat-transfer benchmark compares a heat current of equal power at two prescribed temperatures. [Bradley and colleagues, thermodynamic and power calculations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7406382/) · [LaRowe and Amend, “Energy Limits for Life in the Subsurface”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A75173D093B9A43C6237C4F61821FDA8/9781108477499c19_585-619.pdf/energy_limits_for_life_in_the_subsurface.pdf)

## S177

**Heat from a breathing animal.** In article II of their *Mémoire sur la chaleur*, Lavoisier and Laplace normalized two trials on the same guinea pig to ten hours and averaged the resulting ice melt. They give 13 French ounces, 1 gros and 13½ grains. At 30.594 g to the ounce, 8 gros and 576 grains to the ounce, the mass is 0.402 kg. Melting that much ice requires about 134 kJ at 333.5 kJ/kg, or 3.73 W over ten hours. Article IV discusses the contribution of cooling extremities and condensed moisture. The epilogue uses gross calorimeter heat. Where it estimates entropy generated during heat transfer, an effective warm temperature of 310 K and melting ice at 273 K give 3.73 × (1/273 − 1/310) ≈ 0.0016 W/K. Neither that estimate nor the observation measures total entropy production in an animal biosphere. [Original memoir, articles II and IV](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/%C5%92uvres_compl%C3%A8tes_de_Laplace/M%C3%A9moire_sur_la_chaleur) · [Getty Museum’s French weights table](https://www.getty.edu/publications/french-silver/reader-note-2/) · [National Bureau of Standards, heat of fusion of ice](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/23/jresv23n6p643_A1b.pdf)

Darren Southwell and colleagues modeled prey consumption by Adélie penguins on Béchervaise Island from thirteen years of data. Their mean breeding population of 1,836 pairs consumed an estimated 16,447 MJ per day in the breeding season. Dividing by 86,400 seconds gives 190 kW in ingested food, about one twenty-fourth of the January site draw. The colony’s total metabolic heat would also depend on absorption, growth, work and the energy carried away in waste. [Study and population estimate](https://www.bas.ac.uk/data/our-data/publication/a-bioenergetics-model-for-estimating-prey-consumption-by-an-adelie-penguin-population-in-east-antarctica/)

## S178

**Energy in the same territory before and after industrialization.** Paul Warde’s *Energy Consumption in England & Wales, 1560–2000* (2007), appendix I, table 1, gives 63.7 PJ for 1560 and 6,115.0 PJ for 1950. The respective coal entries are 6.3 and 5,227.6 PJ. Fodder for working animals accounted for 21.1 PJ in 1560 and firewood for 21.4. Table 3 gives 20.0 and 140.4 GJ per person per year. Using a 365.25-day year, the totals correspond to 2.02 and 193.8 GW, or roughly 440 and 42,000 facilities at the Mäntsälä January rate. Dividing its draw by the 1560 per-person average (634 W) gives about 7,200 people. The IEA’s 55.4 GW for all data centers gives 27.4 and 0.286 times the respective territorial totals. These ratios compare power even though the inputs differ in quality. [Table 1, 1560](https://histecon.fas.harvard.edu/energyhistory/data/Warde_Energy%20Consumption%20England.pdf#page=116) · [Table 1, 1950](https://histecon.fas.harvard.edu/energyhistory/data/Warde_Energy%20Consumption%20England.pdf#page=122) · [Table 3, per-person series](https://histecon.fas.harvard.edu/energyhistory/data/Warde_Energy%20Consumption%20England.pdf#page=132) · [Electricity and site data](#s175)

For a comparison of entropy production with the same fuel and temperatures in both years, take only the coal entries. These give mean thermal powers of 199.6 MW and 165.7 GW. If each entire flow passes as heat from an effective 1,000 K reservoir to a 300 K sink, the entropy generated by that transfer is about 466,000 W/K and 387 million W/K. The ratio of approximately 830 is just the coal ratio because the temperature assumptions are the same. Applying the separate 320-to-300 K benchmark to the two flows gives about 41,600 and 34.5 million W/K. These are two alternative calculations on the historical fuel supply. An actual national entropy budget would require the uses of the fuel, work and products leaving each process, and the temperatures at which heat was released.
