# Does reopening an energy pathway help it sustain itself?

In Washington's Elwha River, salmon feed at sea and carry ocean-grown tissue upriver when they return to spawn. Their offspring can feed in a river enriched by those returns, go to sea, and bring the next delivery upstream. Two dams interrupted that route for roughly a century. Their removal in 2011–2014 gives chapters 2 and 3 a natural experiment with a physical boundary and an outcome observable a generation later. The assembly under study is the local connection between an ocean feeding ground, an upstream spawning ground and its food web. The salmon species already existed. [Dam-removal field study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fevo.2021.765488) · [Food-web study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2015.09.009)

The book makes a stronger prediction than fish passage alone. If the energy the adults deliver helps renew the run, then, among otherwise comparable spawning reaches, more marine food available to juveniles should increase the number of locally born offspring that survive to return **per parent**. A count of adults on spawning grounds can rise even when every generation depends on fish supplied by a hatchery.

## What changed after removal?

I compared the 19 stretches of river that USGS crews surveyed in all four years. The original release contains 22 reaches, three of which were first surveyed after removal. The entries below are counts of adult Chinook seen by snorkelers. Survey distance varied, so the accompanying results also give kilometers surveyed, adults per kilometer, and the number of reaches with sightings. Above both dams, crews covered 59.1 km across the two early surveys without seeing an adult Chinook. They covered 63.0 km across the later surveys and saw adults in six of the eleven reaches in 2018 and four in 2019. Juveniles were also present in three of the eleven upper reaches in 2018 and four in 2019. The earlier surveys did not record juvenile occupancy. The middle section requires particular care. During the 2018 survey managers released 998 adults held at a hatchery into one of those reaches. The downstream section shows that fish were returning to the river in all four years. It shares the same fish, management and sediment disturbance, which limits its use as a control. These are repeated observations of one restoration. ## How far did the feedback go?

**Marine material entered the food web.** Within a year of the lower dam's removal, researchers detected a shift in carbon and nitrogen isotopes in American dippers, birds that feed along the river. A separate experiment in six Elwha side channels helps interpret that finding. Before dam removal, researchers added salmon carcasses to one reach of each pair. Dissolved inorganic nitrogen doubled after a month and algal growth increased after two. Nitrogen from the salmon showed up in animals, yet the researchers detected no corresponding improvement in the juvenile trout measures they tracked. The transfer can be real while its benefit to fish remains small, seasonal or hard to detect. Isotopes trace where material came from. They do not measure net energy extraction or entropy production. [Tonra and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2015.09.009) · [Morley and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1422)

**Did the salmon replace themselves?** The river-wide record pairs naturally spawning parents with their estimated river-born offspring returning as adults. It includes hatchery-born parents that spawned in the river. One returning adult per parent is replacement. For every brood year from 2004 through 2015, both published estimates fall below that threshold. Their numerical estimates differ. Pess and colleagues report a maximum of 0.40. The state's Table 2a gives 0.50 in 2009 and, for example, an estimated 343 returning offspring from 2,552 parents in 2015, or 0.13 per parent. Neither series measures replacement specifically for the fish seen above the dams in 2018 and 2019. [Pess and colleagues, section 4.4](https://doi.org/10.3389/fevo.2024.1241028) · [Public brood-year counts, Table 2a, PDF p. 359](https://wdfw.wa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/02309/wdfw02309.pdf#page=359)

The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe also tracks young fish migrating out to sea. The 2024 recovery plan reports averages of 97 young Chinook reaching that stage per female before removal, 66 during removal and 172 afterward. That is progress toward renewal. Each of those young fish still has an ocean migration to survive before it can return the next delivery of marine food. [Elwha recovery plan, Appendix C](https://restoringolympicpeninsulasalmon.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Elwha_Salmon_Recovery_Plan_Update_Dec2024_web.pdf)

## What would distinguish renewal from passage?

Compare tributaries accessible to the same run in the same brood year. Match them on spawning density, habitat, flow and sediment. Measure marine input independently of adult head counts, using carcass biomass and uptake in the juvenile food supply. Then trace river-born offspring from each tributary through their ocean years and back, accounting for hatchery origin, harvest and shared ocean conditions. Where food limits survival, greater usable marine input per juvenile should yield more returning offspring per parent. Sustained replacement across several generations would be evidence that a local run can renew itself.

If well-matched reaches repeatedly receive and use different amounts of marine food yet produce equally many returning offspring per parent, with enough precision to exclude a biologically meaningful benefit, that would count against the proposed energy feedback here. Opening more spawning habitat could explain upstream occupation on its own. The survey and public brood tables let us test passage and historical replacement now. They lack the linked reach-by-cohort measurements needed to decide between those explanations for the later upstream run. [USGS survey](https://doi.org/10.5066/P9MFJXK1) · [Cohort methods and habitat effects](https://doi.org/10.3389/fevo.2024.1241028)

## Reproduce the survey comparison

From the repository root, with the [research dependencies](../scripts/requirements.txt) installed, run `python scripts/elwha.py --fetch`. The [analysis](../scripts/elwha.py) downloads the [original USGS CSV](https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/file/get/615c9c83d34e7b019242b88f?f=__disk__cc%2Fff%2Fe9%2Fccffe978aaaea7f20c2f9eb59d6f5786e8780c4d), checks its fingerprint, and writes the [annual results](../scripts/results/elwha.csv). For an existing download use `python scripts/elwha.py --input path/to/survey.csv`. The food-web and replacement results above come from the linked studies.
