# Chapter 2 — The Work of Staying the Same

*Continuity is something that happens.*

In February 2021, severe cold disrupted electricity generation across Texas and other parts of the South Central United States. Generating units failed, lost output, or could not start. In Texas, controlled disconnections were ordered to prevent a wider grid collapse. The later investigation by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, and regional entities identified both freezing equipment and interrupted fuel supplies among the principal causes.[1]

The machinery had not all vanished. Much of it stood where it had stood before the weather changed. Yet equipment that existed could no longer provide the capacity on which people depended.

The distinction is the starting point of this book. A capacity is more than an inventory. It is an arrangement that can operate under particular conditions, for a particular interval, with the inputs and responses it requires. Counting machines without those conditions can make a society look more secure than it is.

The investigation also described a damaging connection between natural gas and electricity. Gas supplied generators, while parts of gas production and delivery needed electrical power. Power losses at gas facilities could therefore contribute to fuel shortages affecting electricity generation. Some losses came from weather damage and some from controlled disconnections. The report called for better coordination and identification of critical gas infrastructure.[1]

That connection did not explain every failure. It revealed something more useful than a single culprit: support could run in both directions. What appeared to be an input to one industry could depend on the output of the industry it supplied. Under stress, the relationship that normally sustained operation could transmit interruption.

We often picture infrastructure as a chain beginning at a source and ending at a customer. Fuel arrives, a generator turns, electricity travels, and a home becomes warm. Following the work backward unsettles that picture. The fuel system uses electricity. The electric system requires communications. Repair crews require transport and places to rest. A fuel source is a source only within an organization able to obtain and deliver it.

These are not reasons to draw an arrow between everything and everything else. Most dependencies are irrelevant to a particular interruption, and some are buffered by reserves or substitutes. The work of explanation is to identify the connections that matter on the timescale of the failure. A stock of spare parts may matter over months. An interruption of control power may matter immediately. Calling both dependence is a beginning; distinguishing them makes the account useful.

Imagine a pump that keeps water away from equipment. During ordinary operation, the pump appears to be a small auxiliary device. If it stops and the water disables the larger machine, its apparent importance changes. Nothing about the pump’s horsepower has increased. What has become visible is its position in the conditions of continued operation.

This pump is an illustration, not an additional reported detail of the Texas event. It allows us to separate output from the work that makes output repeatable. The machine doing the advertised job may be only one part of the organization preserving that job.

Maintenance is often described as the opposite of change. The machine remains the same because someone replaces a seal, cleans a filter, adjusts a connection, or notices a new vibration. A service continues because people change what would otherwise fail. The result can look motionless from outside precisely because there is activity within it.

There are several different kinds of persistence here. A rock may retain its form for a long time without repair. A bridge can persist through a combination of durable construction and intermittent work. A living cell continually exchanges material while maintaining an organization. An institution can outlast its members through repeated teaching, decisions, records, and enforcement. No single metabolism belongs to all four. What they share is the need for an explanation appropriate to the interval over which we say they remain.

Even the word same requires care. A repaired machine may contain replacement components. A business may occupy another building and employ entirely different people. Continuity can concern material identity, function, recognized authority, or the ability to perform a task. Before asking what preserves a system, we should say what we are counting as preserved.

In biological theory, Maël Montévil and Matteo Mossio distinguish changing processes from constraints that remain relatively stable at the timescale under study. They describe organization in terms of constraints that contribute to maintaining one another.[2] A structure can direct an activity while that activity helps renew the structure. Their account concerns biological organization; it does not turn a power grid into an organism. It gives us a precise predecessor for the question of how active processes preserve some of their own conditions.

For an engineered system, we must often follow those conditions beyond the machine. The device does not normally train its technician, manufacture its complete supply of replacements, or arrange the income that lets someone attend to it. Describing a machine as self-maintaining can conceal the people maintaining it elsewhere.

A boundary is therefore part of an explanation, not merely a line around an object. Put the boundary around the pump and electricity arrives from outside. Put it around the entire facility and fuel, workers, and spare parts arrive from outside. Enlarge it again and further dependencies appear. The point is not to find a boundary beyond which nothing enters. It is to choose one that makes the proposed mechanism and its important supports visible.

This is also why the energy argument needs more than a rising meter reading. Energy is transferred and transformed; power is a rate of transfer. A system can draw considerable power while failing to deliver its intended service. Another can preserve the service with less power because insulation, coordination, or a better component reduces the burden. The question is which organized conversion sustains which capacity, at what cost.

A household wants warmth, not a maximum rate of fuel consumption. A water service needs dependable delivery, not a pump that works hardest while its pipe leaks. These are differences between a means and an outcome. They prevent a material history from treating energy throughput as a universal measure of achievement.

Now imagine two otherwise comparable workshops. One keeps a spare component and trains two people to replace it. The other uses the same money to expand ordinary output. During an uneventful year, the second may appear more productive. During a supply interruption, the first may continue while the second stops. Neither comparison can be settled without saying which events, costs, and periods matter.

Resilience has an opportunity cost. A spare can remain unused; an extra trained person could do other work; a reserve occupies space. Yet excluding these costs from normal production can move them into the future as a larger loss. What looks idle in a narrow account may be the ability to respond under a changed condition.

The relevant question is not whether every organization should stock every conceivable spare. That would be impossible. It is which disruptions it can absorb, which it can recover from, and which would remove the means of recovery itself. A reserve dependent on the same vulnerable supply as the ordinary system may provide less protection than its separate label suggests.

The Texas investigation’s recommendations included cold-weather preparation and closer gas-electric coordination.[1] Read through the argument developed here, these are proposals to change the conditions under which installed equipment becomes available capacity. They concern the organization of continuation. A nameplate rating cannot answer whether fuel arrives, components remain operable, or a support facility keeps its power.

The human work in that organization is easy to misclassify. An inspection that discovers no fault can look like time spent producing nothing. Training can appear to reduce present output. A worker who stops an operation before damage occurs may seem to have caused the interruption visible in the records. Successful prevention removes the event that would have made its value obvious.

This creates a problem of recognition as well as a problem of engineering. What evidence can justify a continuing expense when its most important result is an absence? Comparisons, inspections, near misses, and explicit failure models can help. So can institutions that give people authority to report deterioration without requiring a disaster to prove that it mattered. None supplies perfect foresight. They make the conditions of confidence inspectable.

Consider an illustrative contract that rewards output delivered this month while assigning the cost of a future breakdown to someone else. The operator can benefit from postponing repair even if all participants know that repair will eventually be required. An apparent failure to understand the system may actually be a division of incentives within it. Better information alone would not remove the conflict.

Conversely, an organization can demand elaborate compliance records while failing to preserve the practical skill needed to interpret a machine’s condition. Documentation is valuable when it connects observation to action. It becomes a substitute for maintenance when producing the record satisfies the institution regardless of what the equipment can do. The physical and social accounts must meet at that point.

Maintenance also raises a political question: what is being preserved? Keeping a clinic available, keeping an expensive asset profitable, and keeping a particular manager in control are different objectives. They can coincide for a time and later conflict. A system’s persistence does not establish the desirability of every relationship through which it persists.

That is the first opening toward the book’s larger thesis. Organizations turn inherited conditions into capabilities. Their operation then helps shape the conditions of further operation. The effects may renew a resource, exhaust it, train successors, concentrate authority, or make an alternative harder to adopt. Staying the same is already a way of changing the future.

The distinction between maintenance and transformation is consequently less absolute than it first appears. Replacing a fragile component changes the system in order to preserve its service. Changing a contract may preserve a useful capability while altering who receives its benefits. Some transformations work by separating what people need to continue from the arrangement through which it happened to be supplied.

We should be able to test this account. Identify the proposed supporting activity before an interruption. Specify what should fail if the activity stops, how long a reserve should delay failure, and what should happen if the support is restored. If the predicted dependence does not appear, the explanation needs correction. A story that discovers a crucial dependency after every possible outcome has learned too little.

A second test concerns the boundary. Does the apparent improvement depend on moving costs or risks outside the measurement? A factory may reduce its own maintenance bill by using components whose repair burdens customers. A service may report greater efficiency by making households perform work previously counted inside it. A wider boundary can reveal a transfer where a narrow account reports a saving.

These are research questions, not a new law claiming that everything survives by the same method. The point is to stop treating a continuing capacity as a thing that requires explanation only once, at its invention. The invention establishes a possibility. Repeated operation has a history of its own.

After an interruption, restoring the previous arrangement can be urgent. People need heat and water before every institutional question has been resolved. But restoration and learning are separate achievements. The capacity to resume can coexist with a failure to change the conditions that made the interruption likely.

The challenge is to preserve the ability to live while changing the way it is supported. That challenge will return in the factory, the mortgage, the laboratory, and the international agreement. Before reaching them, we need to examine a form of organization for which ongoing renewal is part of what it means to exist.

A living cell does more than stand through time. It participates in producing the conditions under which its activities can continue. Across generations, those activities can change the world in which later life must make its way.

# Chapter 9 — The House That Could Not Get Cheaper

*A society can become dependent on the price of what its members cannot afford.*

Imagine a house purchased for $300,000 with $60,000 in savings and a $240,000 mortgage. These figures are invented for the example. Ignore transaction costs, interest already paid, and changes in the remaining loan balance. If the house's market price falls to $250,000, the owner's equity falls from $60,000 to $10,000. If it falls to $220,000, the debt exceeds the estimated sale value by $20,000.

No room has disappeared. The roof may be just as sound. The house can supply the same shelter while the financial position of its owner changes dramatically.

For someone trying to buy a first home, the lower price may be welcome. But even that benefit depends on income, borrowing conditions, and the ability to make a down payment. A price decline during a credit contraction can make a house cheaper and a purchase harder at the same time.

This is the stability trap in a familiar form. Society needs housing to be accessible. Many households and institutions also depend on the value of existing housing claims. A physical improvement in provision and a favorable movement in asset prices are not the same event.

We need to separate several uses. A house provides shelter. An owner may receive rent or occupy it directly. A lender may rely on it as collateral. A household may plan retirement around its sale value. A local government may draw revenue from property taxation under a particular set of rules. Construction firms depend on the demand for new buildings. These relationships can reinforce one another without benefiting everyone equally.

An owner who expects to sell and buy another house in the same market may gain less from a general price rise than the headline value of the existing home suggests. A renter may face rising costs without acquiring an asset. A heavily indebted buyer may be vulnerable to a price fall that an owner without debt can absorb. “Homeowners” and “the housing market” are not single interests.

The relationships can nevertheless form a reinforcing loop. In an illustrative boom, expectations of higher prices encourage borrowing. Credit supports purchases, purchases support prices, and rising collateral values can encourage further lending. Participants can interpret the resulting prices as confirmation that the original expectations were sound. The evidence they observe is partly an effect of the behavior it encourages.

The loop can reverse. Falling prices weaken collateral and equity. Lenders become cautious. Reduced credit limits purchases. Distressed sales can put further pressure on prices. None of these movements requires the housing stock to become physically less useful at the same rate. Claims and the provision they organize can move differently.

The United States financial crisis of 2007–2009 supplies a historical case, though it cannot be reduced to one housing feedback. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's majority report identified failures of regulation and supervision, corporate governance and risk management, excessive borrowing, deteriorating mortgage standards, and weaknesses in the handling of complex financial products. The published report includes dissenting views, which disagree about causal emphasis.[1]

The report matters here because it connects failures across organizations. Mortgages could be originated, pooled into securities, financed, rated, sold, and held by different participants. Risk did not disappear because it had changed form or location. Dependence on continued funding could make institutions vulnerable when confidence in assets and counterparties deteriorated.

A simple hypothetical balance sheet helps explain the amplification. An institution with assets of 100, debt of 95, and equity of 5 has a thin cushion. A loss of 5 in asset value exhausts that equity before any further loss is absorbed elsewhere. If it must sell assets quickly to meet obligations, its own sales may contribute to lower prices. Leverage changes how a modest movement in asset values affects the capacity to continue.

The arithmetic alone does not explain the crisis. We must identify which assets lost value, how they were financed, which obligations came due, and why institutions could not replace funding or absorb losses. The point of the example is to show one mechanism through which an apparently local problem can become a wider constraint.

Finance is not unreal in this account. Payments, credit, and commitments coordinate production across time. Their disruption can prevent useful activity even when many workers, buildings, and machines remain. A manufacturer may lose working capital; a household may lose income; customers may cut purchases; an otherwise viable supplier may fail because its customers cannot pay.

The Great Depression offers a different historical comparison. U.S. banking panics in the early 1930s, monetary contraction, and the constraints of the international gold standard participated in a much wider breakdown. Federal Reserve historical accounts describe both the panics and policy failures that aggravated them.[2] These mechanisms were not identical to those of 2008, and the comparison should not erase the difference in monetary regimes.

What the cases share is a limit on explanations that equate material possibility with actual production. The continued existence of useful resources does not guarantee that institutions will bring them together. Coordination can fail. Loss of income can weaken demand, weakened demand can discourage production, and reduced production can further reduce income. The social organization of claims can become a binding constraint on material activity.

This does not mean every idle resource could instantly be redeployed. Skills may not match available work; equipment may be specialized; supply chains may be broken. A crisis can destroy capabilities as it continues. The argument is that financial and institutional breakdown can be part of that destruction rather than a mere reflection of an independent disappearance of physical capacity.

These conflicts explain why an institution may defend an arrangement its members criticize. A household can favor affordable housing while fearing a loss of equity. A bank can recognize the need for lower entry costs while depending on collateral. A government can seek stability while protecting valuations that make access difficult. The system need not possess a secret mind. Its persistence can emerge from interlocking dependencies.

A humane correction would have to distinguish them. Expanding useful housing supply, changing access to credit, protecting vulnerable occupants, restructuring distressed obligations, or altering ownership can have different effects. None is a universal prescription in this chapter. Each would need a specific jurisdiction, institutional design, and account of transition. The theoretical point is that affordability should be pursued as provision, while the financial consequences of change are managed explicitly.

If every incumbent claim is treated as inviolable, improvement can be blocked indefinitely. If every incumbent loss is dismissed, a transition can impose severe costs on people who organized ordinary lives around existing rules. The problem is to discriminate among claims and share adjustment in ways that preserve necessary capabilities. It cannot be solved by pretending there is no conflict.

Consider three illustrative attempts at correction. A grant to buyers can help some households make a down payment. If the number of homes available does not respond, part of the added purchasing power can support higher prices. The intervention helps an immediate claimant while leaving the dependence on expensive housing intact. Whether that happens, and by how much, requires evidence about the particular market; it is not a universal verdict on assistance.

A second proposal changes the terms on which housing is supplied: for example, homes held under a public or cooperative arrangement with rules governing access and resale. Here the intended mechanism is to provide shelter without requiring every occupant to gain through a rising sale price. The proposal still needs land, construction, maintenance, competent administration, and a fair method of allocating scarce places. Changing the claim on a building does not build it or keep its roof sound.

A third proposal concentrates on the debts attached to existing homes. Restructuring a claim can reduce one household’s burden while imposing a loss elsewhere. That loss cannot be analyzed only as an entry erased from a ledger: whose savings, income, or institutional solvency depended on payment? The answer may justify absorbing some claims collectively, imposing losses on some holders, or choosing another route. It cannot justify treating every claim as sacred merely because somebody holds it.

These are mechanisms to investigate, not a housing program inferred from a philosophical principle. Their differences expose the real choice. One intervention changes purchasing power, another changes the terms of access, and another changes inherited obligations. A serious comparison measures shelter, security, public cost, and the distribution of loss, rather than asking only whether prices recovered.

The transition also has a calendar. New supply may take years while a household needs somewhere to live this week. A plan that is sound at its endpoint can fail during the interval. Temporary provision, finance, and protection against displacement are therefore part of the mechanism, not an apology added after the design is complete.

Entrenchment becomes a testable explanation when these dependencies can be identified before opposition occurs. If an organization’s income or collateral depends on a valuation, changing that dependence should sometimes change its response to reform. If resistance persists unchanged after the dependence is removed, or if a technical objection explains the behavior better, the account needs revision. Calling every disagreement a defense of vested interests would make the thesis impossible to test.

Return to the house. The practical objective is not to make its price rise forever or fall without regard to consequences. It is to organize dependable access to useful shelter while managing the claims and dependencies built around it. That is a harder task than choosing a direction for one number. It is also closer to the need that made the house matter in the first place.

The stability trap can be understood as a failure to distinguish the reproduction of useful life from the reproduction of every existing valuation. The two overlap enough to make change difficult. They differ enough to make change necessary. A society capable of deliberate correction must learn to separate them without destroying the capabilities people need.

Such correction depends on knowledge. Institutions must notice conditions, compare proposals, test consequences, and revise their actions. The required intelligence is distributed across people, records, tools, and organizations. No solitary mind possesses it all.

The next chapter asks how societies preserve the competence to make such comparisons, and why possessing information is different from being able to act on it.

# Chapter 12 — Can a System Change Itself?

*To preserve a capability, we may have to change what keeps it going.*

A chemical can work well in a machine and badly in the world that supports the machine’s users. The two judgments need not concern different substances or different physical laws. They can concern consequences separated by place, time, and the boundary of an account.

In 1974, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland described how chlorofluoromethanes could survive in the lower atmosphere, reach the stratosphere, and release chlorine through photochemical breakdown. Chlorine could then participate in reactions destroying ozone.[1] The stability that allowed the compounds to persist did not make their eventual effects harmless. Following a substance beyond its immediate use changed the problem that needed to be solved.

In 1985, Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner, and Jonathan Shanklin reported large seasonal losses of ozone over Antarctica.[2] The observations made a planetary change visible through a maintained practice of measurement. The atmospheric chemistry required further investigation; a striking observation did not contain the whole mechanism inside it. Yet the object of concern was no longer confined to an industrial process or a laboratory reaction.

The resulting challenge had several parts. People had to establish what was happening, identify activities that could be changed, decide who would change them, and preserve useful services through the transition. More knowledge of atmospheric chemistry could improve the diagnosis without by itself supplying a replacement refrigerant, financing a conversion, or creating an enforceable agreement.

The Montreal Protocol, agreed in 1987 and entering into force in 1989, established controls on the production and consumption of substances that deplete the ozone layer. Its controls developed through subsequent adjustments and amendments.[3] What began as knowledge about an environmental effect entered the rules governing industrial activity.

That is a concrete answer to the question of this chapter. An organized society can learn something about the consequences of its operation and alter some of the operations producing those consequences. It does so through institutions with their own material conditions, conflicts, and limits. The answer is neither automatic adaptation nor a mind outside the system directing it from above.

Consider what a production limit changes. A manufacturer making a long-lived investment cannot evaluate the project solely by assuming that yesterday’s permitted activity will remain available indefinitely. The rule can change the expected usefulness of equipment, the value of developing alternatives, and the relationships among suppliers and customers. This is an inference about the mechanism of a credible limit, not a claim that every company responded identically or only for this reason.

A rule does not manufacture the alternative. Production must change through equipment, materials, skills, and repeated operation. A substitute that performs well in one application may not fit another without redesign. In an illustrative refrigeration workshop, a change in working fluid might also require different components, handling procedures, or training. The service to preserve is cooling; the particular chemical is one historical means of providing it.

That distinction is the practical center of transformation. If preserving a service is identified with preserving every existing component, the old arrangement becomes indispensable by definition. If reform dismisses the service as merely an excuse for the old components, people can lose something they need. A successful transition separates the capability from the specific dependence that has become harmful.

The treaty’s arrangements also recognized that participants did not begin with equal capacities. Article 5 provides differentiated obligations for qualifying developing countries. Article 10 establishes financial and technical cooperation, including support for agreed incremental costs and a Multilateral Fund.[4] The fund began operating in 1991 and supports the implementation of the agreement through investment and technical assistance.[5]

These provisions matter to the causal account. A uniform instruction can confront unequal means of complying. Financial support and technical cooperation can make a formally available alternative practically accessible. That does not establish that every burden was fairly shared or every project well chosen. It shows that changing a dependency requires attention to the means of transition as well as the intended endpoint.

The arrangement also makes a useful distinction between recognizing an obligation and acquiring the ability to fulfill it. A country can accept a goal while lacking the equipment, training, or finance to implement it on a proposed schedule. Treating that gap as a failure of belief would direct effort toward persuasion when the missing link is productive capacity. Conversely, providing equipment does not guarantee that an obligation will be enforced. Both links need evidence.

Assessment was incorporated into the agreement itself. Article 6 calls for recurring review of control measures using scientific, environmental, technical, and economic information.[6] The institution included a way to revisit its decisions as the knowledge and feasibility of action changed. A treaty could preserve a continuing purpose while changing the measures used to pursue it.

The distinction between a rule and its effects remained essential. Reported compliance, atmospheric concentrations, emissions, and ozone recovery are related quantities, not interchangeable measures. A production restriction acts on a flow of new material. Chemicals already present in equipment or in the atmosphere do not disappear when a decision is signed. The material history continues through the institutional change.

The 2022 scientific assessment reported declining atmospheric abundances of controlled ozone-depleting substances and evidence of ozone recovery, with important differences among regions and atmospheric layers.[7] It did not describe a planet whose ozone problem was completely finished. The timing of recovery and the interpretation of individual years still depended on atmospheric processes and variability.

The same assessment discussed unexpected CFC-11 emissions, investigations, policy responses, and subsequent reductions, while noting remaining observational uncertainties.[7] Measurement could expose a gap between the organization’s declared activity and its physical consequences. A system capable of correction needs that possibility. If success is defined only by its own paperwork, evidence of failure has nowhere authoritative to enter.

Even the replacement pathway created another environmental problem. Hydrofluorocarbons used as substitutes do not deplete ozone, but many are powerful greenhouse gases. The 2016 Kigali Amendment added a phase-down of specified HFC production and consumption.[3] A solution to one constraint had to be reconsidered in relation to another effect.

This is not a reason to say that solving problems is futile. It is a reason to avoid identifying a successful intervention with the end of history. The relevant achievement is a capacity to observe consequences and revise action, including consequences created by earlier improvements. A system that can correct a substitute’s harms has learned something different from a system that merely replaces one product once.

Montreal should not be used as proof that any global problem can be solved by copying its institutional form. Different industries, distributions of power, alternatives, and enforcement conditions produce different difficulties. A chemical phaseout and a transformation of an entire energy economy are not equivalent tasks. The case supplies mechanisms to compare, not a promise that every comparison will succeed.

It also does not demonstrate that evidence alone caused agreement or that every participant shared one purpose. Scientific findings identify and constrain a problem; political organization determines how reasons, interests, and capacities enter a decision. To explain the relative importance of particular negotiators, campaigns, firms, and states would require a more detailed political history than this chapter supplies. The narrower claim rests on the documented connection among evidence, controls, support for implementation, and measured consequences.

The question is what this connection teaches us about the argument developed across the book.

Organizations turn inherited conditions into new capabilities. Their success changes those conditions, creating both opportunities for further development and dependencies that can obstruct change. We have followed that sequence through access to resources, the reproduction of productive skills, and claims on the results of production. Montreal adds a case in which some of the connections were deliberately revised.

The sequence is not a law explaining every event. In biological organization, maintenance concerns relations among living processes. In technological development, existing components and skills can be recombined. In institutions, recurring decisions and enforceable claims can preserve authority. These mechanisms have substantial prior literatures, including autopoiesis and closure of constraints, niche construction, technological combination, and attempts to relate Marxian analysis to systems theory.[8]

The book’s proposed contribution is a way to connect three questions that are often separated. What makes a capability possible? What work and claims make it recur? How does dependence on that recurrence affect the possibility of changing it? The third question prevents the first two from becoming an uncomplicated story of cumulative success.

The originality of that connection should be judged by what it helps explain. Merely naming a feedback loop adds little to an explanation that already includes it. A useful synthesis must identify a neglected dependency, distinguish mechanisms previously conflated, or produce a prediction that survives comparison with a simpler account. Breadth is an obligation to demonstrate these gains, not evidence that they have been achieved.

Montreal suggests one such conditional proposition. Where a harmful input supplies a useful service, replacing the input is more feasible when the service can be continued through an accessible alternative. Credible rules, transition resources, and observation of the result can help make that alternative both usable and durable. This is a hypothesis about several connected conditions, not a finding that each was necessary in every phase of this particular history.

A comparison would need cases in which some of those conditions were absent. Did an available alternative fail to spread because finance or authority was missing? Did support fail because the replacement was technically inadequate? Did a rule change behavior before new equipment arrived by changing investment expectations? Which changes followed the agreement, and which were already underway? A successful case alone cannot answer all of these questions.

The same discipline applies to entrenchment. If a firm’s revenue, a household’s security, or an institution’s authority depends on an arrangement, we can investigate whether that dependence affects its response to change. We must identify the dependence independently of the opposition. If the proposed alternative fails on its own technical merits, resistance need not be explained as defense of an incumbent interest.

The housing case makes the distinction vivid. A lower price can improve entry while weakening an existing balance sheet. Explaining that joint effect is more informative than describing an abstract struggle between good intentions and selfishness. It allows proposals to target the dependence: preserve shelter and security while changing the claims that require exclusion. The explanation remains incomplete until it shows who bears the costs and how the transition can occur.

In the language of dialectics, the same relation can enable an activity and obstruct its transformation. The useful claim concerns a coupled effect. It is not that every object possesses a mysterious opposite, or that a conflict must resolve itself into a higher stage. A causal account must show how the enabling and obstructing effects arise from the arrangement.

Quantity can become quality when an identified threshold changes what a system can do. A new connection can make isolated competencies jointly usable; an accumulated stock can outlast a temporary interruption; a sufficiently credible change in expected demand can make investment viable. Increasing a number does not establish such a threshold. The organization and the mechanism have to be specified.

Overcoming while preserving is equally concrete. The aim of a refrigerant transition is to retain useful cooling while changing its material basis. The factory can preserve a productive purpose while reorganizing power delivery. A reform can preserve people’s access to essential services while changing who holds the claims. Preservation requires work during the transition, not simply a declaration about the world after it.

Collapse is no substitute for that work. It can destroy the equipment, trust, and knowledge required for an alternative. A theory that treats every breakdown as a necessary advance cannot distinguish liberation from enduring loss. Deliberate transformation matters precisely because people can sometimes change an arrangement before its failure removes the means of acting.

This returns us to maintenance, but with a different object. At the beginning of the book, we asked what keeps a capacity operating. We can now ask what keeps the capacity to revise it operating. Measurements must continue, records must remain intelligible, critics must be able to present evidence, and people must have enough material security to participate. The ability to correct a system has supports that can themselves be neglected.

That makes institutional learning a material achievement. A committee does not cool a warehouse by agreeing that cooling matters. It can authorize work, change claims, assemble resources, and revise requirements. Those operations can cause machinery to be built and maintained. Meanings and decisions have consequences through organized people and things; they do not need to stand outside material reality to act upon it.

The future role of AI belongs inside this account. Better tools may help compare alternatives, interpret measurements, or preserve lessons. They may also make a defective objective easier to pursue. More capable prediction does not determine which harms count, who may challenge a decision, or who gains from an improvement. Those remain institutional choices even when computation changes their feasible range.

There is an ethical judgment that the historical argument cannot derive for us. Persistence is not goodness. An organization can maintain itself by exhausting people or excluding them from the resources it controls. A more capable system can do more damage. Choosing shared capability, security, and the possibility of correction requires reasons about human lives, not an appeal to maximum energy use or historical inevitability.

That choice need not be immaterial to be real. People can make it, organize around it, build arrangements that express it, and judge those arrangements by their consequences. Values enter the history through action. The difficulty is keeping the declared purpose connected to the conditions under which people actually live.

The ozone case leaves us with an unfinished achievement: institutions that helped redirect industrial activity and must continue to observe and respond to its effects. The work did not end with the discovery, the signature, or the first replacement product. Each became part of the conditions under which further work could happen.

The title of this book names that relation. Products of activity become conditions for further activity. Some organizations renew those conditions; some exhaust them; some acquire the ability to change them deliberately. No consciousness needs to direct the universe for people within it to become responsible for part of its future.

We inherit capabilities we did not create and dependencies we did not choose. The task is to learn which should be sustained, which should be transformed, and how to make the transformation without abandoning the people whose lives continue through it.
