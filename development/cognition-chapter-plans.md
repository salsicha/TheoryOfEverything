# Chapter 10 and 11 plans before prose drafting

Preserved November 15, 2026. These plans belong to the current twelve-chapter sequence. The active chapters are now prose drafts; consult the [current outline](../outline) for their revised argument.

## Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

**Status: chapter outline; full prose pending.** This chapter follows economic self-production, builds on collective intelligence in chapter 8 and the discovery argument in chapter 4, and precedes the dedicated explanation of AI through the thesis in chapter 11. See [the full book outline](../outline), [expanded ideas I57–I60](../ideas), and [the source ledger](../sources.md#s87).

### Central argument

An assembled system can acquire representations that change how it acts, learns, and searches. When those representations include its own capacities and cognitive activity, the system can regulate aspects of its own regulation. Develop the proposal that a self emerges through this recursive modeling, while distinguishing its functional explanation from a settled account of subjective experience.

Cybernetics gives this argument its account of feedback and control. The later history of AI supplies a test of how capabilities become constructible: the logic-first program's expectations were not the route through which trained networks achieved their major advances. The book's proposed driver remains disequilibrium; regulation and learning are mechanisms through which assembled material and formal constraints can change.

### 1. An action inside an undertaking

Open with an explicitly illustrative person carrying a cup across an uneven floor. Small corrections belong to a coordinated movement, itself part of a longer undertaking. Introduce the question of how these processes affect one another. Do not invent experimental findings or make the example stand for a literal map of the brain.

Move from immediate correction to anticipation: the person can change the route after considering their own unsteadiness or distraction. The same body that acts becomes something the organism can take into account. Connect the preceding chapter's account of support for participants to the capacities that support must sustain. Return to chapter 4's question about the organization of search: how can a system assess its own actions, revise a representation, and alter its next attempt?

### 2. Feedback and continued self-production

Explain a loop of action, changed conditions, observation, and revised action. Introduce Wiener's synthesis and Ashby's regulation framework. Specify what is sensed, what can be changed, and which disturbances can be answered. [S87](../sources.md#s87), [S45](../sources.md#s45)

Connect to chapter 3's living organization and chapter 7's account of humans regenerating and changing their developmental conditions. Control can support autopoiesis, but keeping a variable stable does not produce every component needed to continue. Identify the external energy, replenishment, and repair on which the loop depends.

### 3. Hierarchy, recurrence, and recursion

Use the visual-cortex evidence to introduce interacting levels with influences in both directions. Keep its anatomical scope explicit. The larger brain is not a single chain of commands descending from a final executive. [S88](../sources.md#s88)

Explain the differences among organizational levels, recurrent connections, recursive operations, and models of one's own activity. Show what each adds to the account of constrained search. Having a deep hierarchy alone establishes neither recurrence nor a self.

### 4. The model includes the modeler

Develop the proposed emergence of a self through representations of body, action, attention, memory, and limitations. A partial self-model can be useful without a complete internal duplicate or an additional spectator. Its inaccuracies are part of the explanation: a system can act on a mistaken account of its own capacity.

Use attention schema theory as one concrete account to examine. The associated artificial-agent experiment supplies a limited intervention on the usefulness of a self-related model. Keep the broader consciousness claim identified as a hypothesis. [S89](../sources.md#s89), [S94](../sources.md#s94)

Relate this to chapter 8: language and interaction may provide distinctions through which people interpret their own conduct. Preserve the difference between an individual's self-model and collective competence distributed among people.

### 5. Cybernetics and the logic-first ambition

Trace the intellectual development from control and neural modeling to symbolic AI. McCarthy's recollections establish his methodological preference, not its correctness. Explain why explicit deduction appeared promising, then distinguish achievements in specified formal tasks from the unrealized broader ambition. [S90](../sources.md#s90)

Keep the historical question focused on mechanisms of intelligence and how researchers attempted to build them. An institution's research agenda and the methods that eventually prove effective need not coincide.

### 6. Learned organization changes the route

Develop the neural lineage through cybernetics, connectionism, and deep learning. The AI name survives a major shift in the means of producing capabilities. Training acquires useful representations without the designer listing every distinction and inference in advance. [S91](../sources.md#s91)

Use AlexNet as a bounded example of an assembly combining architecture, training methods, data, and computing hardware. Connect the resources committed before a return to chapter 5's account of first viability. Separate this from a claim that spending more resources guarantees success. [S92](../sources.md#s92)

### 7. What the successor preserves

Examine learned search combined with explicit verification through the narrow AlphaProof example. Formal reasoning remains useful within an organization whose search strategies are acquired through learning. Treat the retention and replacement of capabilities as a candidate dialectical transformation, not a universal law of research history. [S93](../sources.md#s93)

This also clarifies the book's proposed inevitability of AI: the emergence of a class of capabilities can depart from influential researchers' expectations about its architecture or developmental route. Chapter 11 develops the causal explanation of AI through assembly, constrained search, and the proposed energetic driver; chapter 12 follows the prediction into data centers and productive use.

### 8. A model changes what can be assembled

Return to the central thesis. Physically realized models preserve relations that alter possible actions. Modeling the modeler's uncertainty or limits can change the search itself. Ask which costs are incurred, which actions become feasible, and which subsequent physical flows change.

Keep a learning objective's mathematical gradient distinct from physical disequilibrium. Feedback and task success alone do not establish entropy-production maximization. The stronger claim requires the coupling from material support, through learned capacity, to changed productive pathways.

### Transition to chapter 11

Move from the account of regulation, self-modeling, and learned representations to the dedicated explanation of AI through the thesis. Chapter 11 connects joint material and Platonic organization, constrained search, costly training, regenerative support, and the proposed inevitability of artificial cognition. Chapter 12 then follows those capabilities into data centers and productive deployment. Keep the historical and cognitive explanation here distinct from that wider causal argument.
## Chapter 11 — Why the World Builds Artificial Intelligence

**Status: chapter outline; full prose pending.** This chapter explains AI through the book's central thesis. It follows the account of cybernetics and self-modeling in [chapter10](../chapter10), then prepares the account of data centers and productive deployment in [chapter12](../chapter12). See [expanded idea I61](../ideas) and [the book outline](../outline).

### Central argument

The book proposes that artificial intelligence is an inevitable outcome of disequilibrium-driven assembly. A productive system can commit existing resources to constructing machinery that acquires representations, concentrates search, and changes what subsequent activity can accomplish. Those capabilities are jointly material and Platonic: their physical organization realizes formal relations, while architecture, objectives, and learned relations constrain the operations the machinery can perform.

Explain the proposed causal sequence directly: physical flows sustain a productive organization; that organization carries costly training and experimentation; learning assembles a usable capability; the capability changes accessible actions and searches; some consequences may support its retention and further development. The claimed tendency toward maximum energy extraction and entropy-production rates is the proposed driver of this wider process. The sequence identifies mechanisms to investigate, not a derivation of that tendency from training success.

### 1. An answer that was never individually programmed

Open with an explicitly illustrative learner that improves at a classification task. A designer supplies an architecture, examples, an objective, and an update procedure without listing a separate answer for every future input. Ask what has been assembled when the resulting system can make a useful distinction on a new example. Keep training performance, generalization, and usefulness in an actual undertaking distinct. [S91](../sources.md#s91)

Carry forward chapter 10's history of learned representations without repeating the whole history. This chapter's question is how that achievement fits the same account as human learning, convergent discovery, and the first steam engine. Define the relevant AI capabilities through prediction, search, representation, and control; do not silently substitute a conscious or universally competent agent for the systems under discussion.

### 2. A system that is jointly material and Platonic

Follow a training update through its formal and material descriptions. The architecture and objective specify relations among permitted operations and evaluated outcomes. A numerical parameter change must be realized through physical computation, memory, and communication. Hardware limits what can be computed; a different representation or method changes what the same hardware can accomplish.

Explain why an equation, a stored parameter set, and a working capability are different achievements. To act, the learned organization needs an implementation, inputs, power, and a context in which its output can be used. Treat the formal relations as part of what the physical system realizes, not as a complete operating system supplied from outside material history. Use the bounded AlexNet example to connect learned organization to its computing requirements. [S92](../sources.md#s92)

### 3. How constraints concentrate the search

Return to the Sudoku argument and chapter 4's discovery problem. Training does not sample every possible program or parameter configuration with equal probability. Architecture, examples, objectives, update rules, and evaluation determine which changes can be attempted and which are retained. Explain how inherited organization makes some capabilities reachable while excluding or neglecting others.

Distinguish finding a useful function from finding the globally best system. Constraints can concentrate search around a misleading shortcut or preserve an error. Different training histories may yield related capabilities without identical internal representations. This supplies a candidate connection to convergent discovery, not proof that one exact architecture or parameter configuration had to appear.

### 4. Existing energy carries the costly intermediate

Use chapter 5's first engine as the physical comparison. A training effort can consume equipment time, electricity, materials, and human work before a usable capability exists. Existing production must carry that interval. A later useful result cannot supply the earlier expenditure backward in time.

Separate improvements that reduce the cost of learning from conditions that let a system commit more resources to a difficult attempt. Both can change what becomes viable. Relate the comparison to the support for human learning in chapter 7. Specify failed runs and unused results as part of the accounting; expenditure alone does not establish successful assembly or energetic benefit.

### 5. Where the proposed disequilibrium driver enters

Trace the wider physical process rather than treating the loss function as a thermodynamic quantity. A derivative used to update parameters is not itself a temperature difference, and lower prediction error does not measure entropy production. Identify the energy supply, the work sustained by it, the organizational choices directing that work, and the actions made possible by the learned system.

Develop the strong claim: under the book's hypothesis, the tendency to develop pathways for greater energy extraction can produce machinery that changes the search for further pathways. Institutions, investment, datasets, and research practices are mechanisms through which the proposed driver acts. They do not establish it merely by being present. Apply chapter 2's accounting discipline before drawing a conclusion about a maximum rate. [S07](../sources.md#s07), [S68](../sources.md#s68)

### 6. Assembly, retention, and regenerative closure

Follow how data, techniques, software, hardware, and trained representations become inputs to later attempts. Distinguish constructing a model, retaining its learned organization, and sustaining the network that makes its continued use possible. Copying weights preserves one component of a capability; it does not reproduce all its operating conditions.

Place autopoiesis at the boundary whose regenerative operations can actually be identified. An isolated trained network does not produce its own electricity, processors, or maintenance. Chapter 9's account of economic self-production supplies the wider question: which returning operations sustain the people, equipment, access, and commitments on which artificial cognition depends? Do not infer closure merely because a service keeps answering requests. [S33](../sources.md#s33)

### 7. A successor can change how search is performed

Use the methodological reversal established in chapter 10 as a specific dialectical case. A learned system can acquire a search strategy that a designer did not supply as an exhaustive rulebook. Formal constraints can remain useful within that changed organization. AlphaProof supplies a bounded example of learned search combined with formal checking. [S93](../sources.md#s93)

Connect this to the joint material and Platonic thesis: the system can change how it reaches admissible answers while retaining constraints on what counts as an answer. Avoid making self-modeling a prerequisite for every AI capability. The chapter explains artificial cognition without requiring a resolution of subjective experience.

### 8. What inevitability would mean

State the author's claim plainly: AI is an inevitable outcome of this process. Define the predicted class of capabilities, the prerequisites, and the relevant alternative histories before testing the claim. A particular company, model architecture, date, or ownership arrangement requires a separate explanation.

Ask whether alternative routes repeatedly become viable when the identified material and formal prerequisites are present. Examine whether removing a proposed enabling condition blocks or delays the predicted transition, and whether a substitute restores it. Specify what outcome would count against the claim. Historical success alone cannot establish necessity, and present prerequisites must not be defined merely as whatever preceded the winner.

### 9. From a learned capacity to a changed physical world

Return to the illustrative learner. It now performs a task, but its wider significance depends on what someone can do with that performance. A useful classification or prediction can still encounter missing evidence, unavailable equipment, or an organization unable to act. Separate measured task success from a demonstrated change in productive pathways.

Set up comparisons that hold stated resources constant while changing representations or methods, and others that vary support for initial training while keeping the task defined. Follow performance into physical consequences and include cases where additional computation yields no useful return. These comparisons belong with the afterword's tests of the stronger energetic claim.

### Transition to chapter 12

The next chapter takes the assembled capability into operation. Explain why data centers become a predicted physical organization of computation, power, cooling, communication, and maintenance, then investigate whether AI-assisted control and design can improve those supports. Chapter 11 explains artificial cognition through the thesis; chapter 12 examines the infrastructure and productive consequences through which that cognition might change the world that sustains it.
