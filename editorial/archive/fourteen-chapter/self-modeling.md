# Chapter 5 — Self-Modeling

*Part II · Humans*

When Charles Darwin came across a fact that went against his theory, he wrote it down at once. In his autobiography he gave the reason. His memory held on to favorable observations and let awkward ones slip. The next time he opened the notebook, the objection would still be there.[1]

Sterile worker ants posed one such difficulty. A worker could have jaws unlike those of her fertile parents and leave no daughters to inherit them. Frederick Smith supplied driver ants from a single West African nest. Darwin dissected workers of different sizes. John Lubbock drew them with a camera lucida, looking at the specimen and the drawing surface together. Between the smallest and largest workers they found intermediate forms.[1]

Darwin traced selection back to the fertile parents. Parents whose workers helped their colony could leave more descendants. The explanation depended on keeping the difficulty in view long enough to change the unit he was thinking about, from the worker to the family.[1]

## Knowing where one is looking

In this account, intelligence requires **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. Darwin observed the ants. At another level he observed what his memory did with evidence about them. When he let that knowledge change how he recorded the next observation, the higher level altered the work of the first.

Walter’s tortoise could steer by the state of its battery. To find evidence of a self-model, look for a system that represents something about its own activity and uses that representation to revise what it does next. One place to look is the control of attention.[2]

Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano gave an artificial player a paddle and a ball falling through a noisy field of view. It could move a clear window across the field to find the ball. A second display told it where it was looking. After training, the researchers removed that display. The player could still move the window and the paddle. It caught fewer balls.[2]

The extra signal mattered when the window missed. It gave the player information about where it had already looked. If the whole field were clear, keeping track of the window should buy less. The score measured the value of the signal in catches.[2]

Nikola Markov’s group traced connections in both directions between levels of the macaque visual cortex. Signals from lower areas reach higher ones, and signals can travel back. The anatomy gives a route for a correction. Discovering what information travels along it takes a test like removing the player’s view of its own attention.[2]

## When the instrument is a person

In Nabenchauk, Mexico, researchers filmed a young weaver learning at a loom. An experienced weaver could put her hands to the threads while the learner held her place. An instructor who spots a misplaced thread has two decisions to make. Can the learner find it herself, and how long should the instructor let her try? Correct it too soon and the cloth advances while the learner misses the error.[3]

In Lyon, J.-L. Moulin used brown to mark black silk on the squared design for Carquillat’s portrait of Jacquard. Black ink would have hidden marks he still needed to see. The loom could select threads for thousands of passes from the cards made to its pattern. It took a draftsman who understood his own vision to keep the distinctions legible on their way from drawing to silk.[3]

Each time a person remembers what she tends to overlook and turns back to check it, her own conduct becomes part of what she can know. The check leaves another memory to draw on. The proposal is that a self can take shape through such repeated acts of modeling the observer. The acts are open to observation. How they feel to the person performing them is a further question.[2]

## When turning away from the wind pays

On a wind farm in northwestern India, the first turbine in a row could take so much speed out of the wind that its neighbors downwind had less to work with. Michael Howland and colleagues gave a group of three turbines two ways to work. Each could face into the wind. Or the upstream machines could turn a little aside on the strength of a model of the wakes they themselves made. A machine that gave up some of its own output could steer its slower-moving air clear of the next rotor. The return would show up on another turbine’s meter.[4]

For most of the wind directions they tested, the model placed the best angle for the upstream turbine within five degrees of the measured one. The team then alternated standard and coordinated operation every two and a half hours. In the tested sector, at wind speeds of six to eight meters per second, the coordinated setting delivered about three percent more electricity. If the wind shifted far enough to carry the wake clear of the next rotor, the reason to turn disappeared. The model could say when to leave the machine facing the wind.[4]

The model included the disturbed air each turbine would send toward the others after it turned. Representing the effect of its own action let the controller change that action. Here the return from a correction appeared in electricity the second and third turbines could deliver.[4]

If the extra electricity runs a load and dissipates there at the same temperature, the entropy produced at that load rises with the watts. Farther downwind the slower air behind the turbines would have mixed and dissipated energy anyway. An account of the whole wind field has to include those wakes and the other electricity the farm’s customers would have used.[4]

Richard Lee had a related difficulty when he bought an ox for a feast. He judged the animal well enough. What he misjudged was how his hosts saw the man who had given it.
