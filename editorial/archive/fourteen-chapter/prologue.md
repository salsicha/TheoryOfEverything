# Prologue — What If Life Is Everywhere?

Heather Graham sent her colleagues material from the Murchison meteorite and a sample of bay sediment. She kept their origins to herself. The team had to decide which samples bore traces of life before Graham would tell them what they had tested.[1]

Both a living cell and a meteorite can contain organic molecules. On another planet the contents of a sample tube might be all an investigator had to go on.[1][2]

Stuart Marshall, Cole Mathis and their colleagues broke the molecules into fragments. From the pattern they estimated the fewest distinct joins needed to build each molecule, allowing a piece made once to be used again. They called that number the molecular *assembly index*. High counts in a substance abundant enough to measure suggested that something kept making it. When Graham disclosed the samples’ origins, the high scores belonged to material of biological origin. Some biological samples scored low. The bay sediment did. So did a sample of Scotch whisky. Distillation had left little trace of the yeast.[1][2]

Graham asked how we might recognize life elsewhere. Suppose life arises wherever a planet can sustain it. What natural law would keep producing it? We have one inhabited planet on which to begin looking for an answer.

When I wrote a Sudoku solver, I started with the first empty square. Six digits might fit. The program could fill half the board before it found the contradiction in its first move. Elsewhere the puzzle had already settled a choice. Here are two rows from one board. In the third square of row seven, the setter has printed a five.[3]

~~~text
Row 1     8 2 ·  |  9 · 4  |  7 1 6
Row 4     7 1 6  |  8 · 3  |  9 · 4
~~~

The first row lacks a three and a five. The five in row seven rules out a five in the third column. Write three beside the two. The gap between nine and four must now take five. Follow that five down the column to row four. Its middle gap must take two, leaving five for the gap at the right. One entry several rows away settled four moves. No one had to try them out.[3]

Two fives cannot share a column. The restriction holds on paper, on a screen and on a copy whose solver has yet to mark a square. That relation belongs to what this book calls *Platonic space*. Counting every digit one could write in each gap gave my program a great many choices. Four of those gaps had their answers before the program began. Other boards still required it to guess.[3]

On the Atlantic seafloor, hydrogen-rich vent fluid meets seawater in the pores of a mineral chimney. Some reaction products wash out. Others cling to the walls long enough to react with the next water that comes through. A pore that retains a catalyst can carry on a reaction its bare neighbor cannot sustain. Fresh fluid supplies the chemical energy. Heat and spent material leave with the current.[4]

This is the *hierarchical entropy rejection funnel*. The Sudoku column rules out moves. The pore can retain an ordered structure while exporting entropy in heat and outgoing fluid. A catalyst left on the wall changes what the next parcel of water can do. A cell inherits whole molecules that already fold and can put them to work together. Each level begins with parts another level has made.[4][5]

At Saltom on the Cumberland coast, miners sank a shaft toward coal while water rose in the workings. Once it covered the working face, a whole seam lay out of reach. Burning some coal under a steam boiler could lift the water and bring far more coal to the surface. The owners even paid to send coal underground a second time.[6]

Once the parts are in place, the book argues, assembly takes the quickest feasible route to the greatest rate of entropy production the system can sustain. At Saltom a mine owner could ask how soon each available pump would draw water. To judge the second claim we would have to follow the coal to the fires where it burned and count the fuel and time it took to build the pump and reach the buyers. The engine that started first might lose that comparison.[5][6]

Earth may have spent ages cooling and acquired life in the first interval when its chemistry could leave descendants. Long before there were oceans, how did distant places come to share the same physical constants, and what made one of those places dense enough to light a star?
