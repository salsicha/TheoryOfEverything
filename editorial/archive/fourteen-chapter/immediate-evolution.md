# Chapter 11 — The Immediate Evolution Paradox

*Part IV · Paradox*

The rock in which the Jack Hills zircon grew had long since eroded. On one grain recovered in Western Australia, Simon Wilde’s team used uranium and the lead left by its decay to date part of the crystal to 4.404 billion years ago, give or take eight million years. Within that grain they found zones with different proportions of oxygen isotopes. Those proportions suggested that some of the material that formed the zircon had interacted with liquid water at low temperature.[1]

An impact could still destroy the products of a young ocean’s chemistry. The claim here is that Earth spent geological ages becoming habitable, then acquired life in the first interval when a lineage could survive. Once water, raw material and chemical energy endured together, the waiting time for life belonged to reactions and generations.[1][2]

Oleg Abramov and his colleagues used an impact history and a thermal model to ask when RNA chemistry could persist between strikes. Their 2026 study placed favorable conditions around 4.33 billion years ago. The date marks a change in the modeled habitat. Finding a molecule made at the time would require another kind of evidence.[2]

Edmund Moody’s team worked backward from genes shared by distant branches of life. They anchored parts of the tree to dated events and estimated rates of change. Their last universal common ancestor lived roughly 4.2 billion years ago, with one reported interval from 4.33 to 4.09 billion years. This was already an evolved population. Earlier lineages that died out have no living descendants to put on the tree. In Western Australia, Tara Djokic and colleagues found evidence for microbes at hot springs about 3.48 billion years ago. Their rocks put organisms at a particular spring. The settlers could have had a long ancestry.[3]

Subtracting the two central estimates gives about 130 million years between a modeled chemical window and a population with ancestors. That interval can contain many generations after the founding. To date the founding itself we need to know how a family of reactions could establish before its habitat disappeared.[1][2][3]

## When reactions begin to leave descendants

At Lost City the rock determines much of what enters a pore. Which bonds can form and which products stay until the next flow restrict the next reaction. Counting all the arrangements of a modern cell ignores those material and Platonic constraints at each step.[4]

Wim Hordijk, Mike Steel and Stuart Kauffman modeled chains made of two kinds of building block. They fixed the chance that a chain could catalyze a given reaction and allowed successively longer chains. Each additional position doubled the number of chains of that length. In one series of simulations, by length thirteen there was a good chance of finding a set whose members could be built from the starting food and whose reactions all had catalysts among that food or the set itself. One new chain might supply a catalyst several incomplete routes lacked.[4]

In running water the catalysts must also form faster than they wash out. Once a network can replace them it can outlast the molecules it began with. Heredity adds a further requirement. A descendant must inherit enough of the arrangement to carry on, along with differences it can pass to its own descendants. When each founding unit leaves fewer than one viable successor on average, families dwindle. Above one, some families can survive and spread. Natural selection could begin while the whole family still fit inside one pore.[4]

At Scripps, Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce studied two RNA catalysts that helped make one another. Their population could double in about an hour. When several pairs competed for the same feed, new combinations arose and some displaced their parents. The team had supplied the starting catalysts. The hour timed their multiplication. On the young Earth the first pair, or other chemistry capable of passing on its organization, also had to form.[4]

Why expect even the first workable combination to turn up promptly? A field of mineral pores gave the same restricted chemistry many starts. Each additional kind of molecule could supply a missing reaction for several sets at once. Each additional pore offered another place where one set might begin operating before a current dispersed it. If attempts are independent and roughly steady, doubling the number of equally well supplied pores halves the expected wait for the first successful start. A common impact can cut all those attempts short. Cooling and the retreat of frequent impacts changed both how many places qualified and how long each one had.[2][4]

Once descendants occupied fresh surfaces, some chemical energy that would have gone out to sea in unreacted compounds could pass through living cells. To argue for an origin in the first habitable interval, the wait for that first surviving family must be short compared with the life of the habitat. To argue for the highest rate of dissipation one must also account for the energy its descendants carried away in cells and products.[2][3][4]

## One season on Daphne Major

Peter Boag and Peter Grant had measured Darwin’s finches on Daphne Major before the drought of 1977. As food grew scarce, large hard seeds remained. Large birds, especially males with large beaks, survived in greater proportion. The useful beaks existed before the soft seeds ran out. A single dry season changed who could eat.[5]

A flask that turned cloudy in 2003 gives a reason to ask what a population already carried before a change. Richard Lenski’s team had supplied twelve populations with glucose and citrate for years. One acquired the ability to grow on citrate in the presence of oxygen. From frozen samples the researchers could give earlier ancestors’ descendants another chance at the same food. Descendants of certain later ancestors acquired the trait more readily. The citrate had been there all along. What it took to reach it had accumulated over tens of thousands of generations.[6]

An observer who needs billions of years to evolve has a better chance of finding herself on a planet where life started early. David Kipping allowed for that bias. In 2025, using the proposed 4.2-billion-year date for the common ancestor and a start to habitability near 4.4 billion years, he obtained odds of roughly thirteen to one for rapid over slow abiogenesis. Here *rapid* is relative to a planet’s habitable lifetime. The first suitable interval is a finer division than those dates resolve.[1][3][7]

A grain like the Jack Hills zircon can date an early condition without relying on a fossil. Establish when water, chemical energy and building material persisted together, then look for independent traces of descendants. Evidence of sterile habitats enduring far longer than the chemistry required would put a second long wait after Earth became habitable. A lineage dating from the first such interval would put the delay in the cooling of the planet.[1][2][3][4][7]
