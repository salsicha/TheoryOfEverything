# Chapter 8 — Self Modeling

At a signal, the volunteers tapped a sensor with their right index fingers. A motor tapped each person’s left fingertip, then tapped it again. They pressed a foot pedal to say which touch felt stronger. Konstantina Kilteni, Christian Houborg and Henrik Ehrsson varied the force of the second tap. The first stayed at two newtons. What they could change was when it arrived.[1]

With the apparatus responding as fast as it could, a weaker second tap could feel as strong as the one the participant had triggered. Then the researchers inserted an extra tenth of a second between the right finger’s movement and the tap on the left. After five hundred repetitions with this delay, they tested both timings. Across the group, the delayed tap now felt fainter than it had before training. When the researchers took the delay away, the prompt tap felt stronger than it had before. The motor delivered the same force. The volunteers had learned a new consequence of moving a finger.[1]

## Knowing what my hand will do

A touch receptor in the left finger can report pressure. On its own it has no way to tell whether the right hand caused it. The nervous system has another source of information. When it sends a command to move, a copy of the command can be used to predict what that movement will feel like. The model must relate an outgoing action to an expected site and time of contact. Change the delay for long enough and the prediction can move with it. An expected tap then feels less intense.[1][2]

In a follow-up experiment, the right finger could make the triggering movement voluntarily or be moved passively. The left finger got its tap in either case. Only when the participant initiated the movement did the tap feel weaker than an externally triggered one. In a separate brain-imaging study, people who showed stronger attenuation also showed closer coordination between activity in the cerebellum and in regions of cortex that process touch. The scan gives a place to look for communication between movement and sensation. The passive finger gives a reason to think the communication carries information about the person’s own command.[2]

What the brain models here is a relation within the person. A particular movement usually causes a sensation at a particular place and time. Getting such a relation into nervous tissue is a physical problem. Five hundred movements occupy five hundred separate moments. What does the last one encounter that the first one did not?

## What one impulse leaves for the next

Guo-qiang Bi and Mu-ming Poo could make either of two connected rat neurons in a dish fire first. When the receiving cell fired within about twenty milliseconds after its neighbor had sent a signal, repeated pairings could strengthen a weak excitatory connection. Reverse the order within a comparable interval and the connection could weaken. After the pairings stopped, a fresh signal across the same connection produced a different response. The number of firings alone would have missed what the tissue retained. Their order mattered.[3]

These firings drew on a chemical supply. A nerve cell stays out of equilibrium by keeping different concentrations of ions on either side of its membrane. During a signal, ions flow down their gradients. Restoring them and recycling the small packets that carry chemical messages across synapses takes work. Vidhya Rangaraju and her colleagues tracked ATP, a molecule that supplies chemical work, in the nerve endings of cultured neurons. Electrical activity raised the demand for ATP and stimulated its production. Releasing and recycling the packets accounted for a large share of that demand. Some of the chemical energy disperses as heat. Some does the work that prepares the terminal to send the next signal.[4]

This gives *dissipative adaptation* a specific job in the account. A driven system can pass through several arrangements while it takes up work and releases heat. In the models studied by Nikolay Perunov, Robert Marsland and Jeremy England, changing the drive changes which transitions are likely. What accumulates depends on which arrangements the drive can produce, how reliably it does so and how readily the changes reverse. In a brain, metabolism supplies the work. Moving a finger and receiving a touch supply a sequence. A connection susceptible to that sequence can change a little on each pass. If the change lasts until the next pass, the same incoming signal encounters a different circuit.[3][4][5]

Bi and Poo measured one way a sequence can alter a connection. Kilteni’s volunteers learned the timing of their own movements. Connecting the two findings requires identifying the changes that hold a timing prediction in a human brain. The twenty milliseconds in the dish measures a condition for changing a synapse. The extra hundred milliseconds at the fingertip measures what the person learned. A physical test would keep the number of signals and the fuel supply fixed, vary their timing, and use measured work and rates of reversal to predict which pattern would persist after the signals stopped. A correct judgment with a foot pedal gives us the prediction the circuit learned. It gives us no reading of its heat.[1][3][5]

## Knowing when to look again

In Oxford, Kobe Desender, Annika Boldt and Nick Yeung gave volunteers a choice that cost one point. Eight colored shapes flashed on a screen. The observer had to decide whether their average color was closer to red or blue. A correct answer earned five points. On some trials a click bought a second, clearer look.[6]

The researchers could make the colors nearly alike but hard to call, or mix redder and bluer shapes whose average lay farther from the dividing line. They adjusted the displays until people answered about as accurately in the two conditions. Yet people expressed less confidence when the shapes disagreed more. Given the choice, they spent the point more often on those trials. Their estimate of how well they had seen guided what they chose to see next.[6]

There is evidence in human brains for a distinction between making a judgment and knowing how far to trust it. Stephen Fleming and his colleagues asked people which of two circles contained more dots. Seven people with damage to the front of the prefrontal cortex chose as accurately as the comparison groups. Their confidence was a poorer guide to which of their own visual answers were right. On a word-recognition task, their confidence still tracked their success. A person could lose some ability to judge the reliability of her vision while retaining the ability to judge her memory.[7]

The book defines intelligence as **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. The timing learned at the fingertip gives the first part a physical foothold. A circuit can anticipate what the body’s own action will do. The Oxford decision adds a level. One judgment concerns the colors on the screen. Another concerns how much to trust one’s view of them. The process becomes recursive when that estimate sends the observer back for evidence and the new evidence changes the judgment being estimated. An account of how a self takes shape can begin with acts like these. A person can learn something about her own way of seeing and use it the next time she looks.[1][6][7]

At the fingertip, the experimenters set the sequence the brain learned. In Oxford the observer could change the sequence herself. Each time doubt prompted a second look, the circuits judging color received another input. If the new evidence changed her answer, the consequences of her own doubt became available for learning too. That is how dissipative adaptation could help build a hierarchy. A pattern sustained by metabolic work at one level helps determine which connections are driven, and which can change, at another.[3][4][5][6]

In Oxford a second look cost one point. The volunteer could judge whether it was worth buying. Someone else had set the terms on which she could act.

## Notes

1. Kilteni, Houborg and Ehrsson report the force-comparison task and the change in perceived intensity at both timings. The 100-millisecond delay was added to the apparatus’s usual response time of about 36 milliseconds. [Exposure and timing, figures 1 and 2](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.42888)

2. Kilteni, Engeler and Ehrsson compared voluntary, passive and externally triggered touch. Kilteni and Ehrsson separately measured functional coupling during self-touch. The imaging result is a correlation. [Active and passive movement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2020.100843) · [Cerebellar and cortical activity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6975290/)

3. Bi and Poo found timing-dependent changes at excitatory connections between cultured hippocampal neurons. The outcome also depended on the initial strength of the connection and the cell receiving the signal. [Spike timing and synaptic strength](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998)

4. Rangaraju, Calloway and Ryan measured ATP at presynaptic terminals and examined the cost of the synaptic vesicle cycle. [Local ATP supply during signaling](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2013.12.042)

5. Perunov, Marsland and England relate transition probabilities in driven systems to accessibility, work histories and fluctuations. Applying that framework to the formation of a self-model is the book’s proposed explanation. [Statistical physics of adaptation](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevX.6.021036) · [Self Assembly](../../../manuscript/book/06-self-assembly.md#which-arrangements-does-a-drive-favor)

6. Desender, Boldt and Yeung varied the mean color and the spread of colors independently. In their first experiment a participant could spend one point for a clearer display. Confidence and requests for more evidence differed between two conditions matched for performance. [Color judgments and the price of a second look](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617744771)

7. Fleming, Ryu, Golfinos and Blackmon compared perceptual and memory judgments after anterior prefrontal lesions. Their measure asked how well a person’s confidence distinguished correct answers from errors at a given level of task performance. [Confidence in perception and memory](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awu221)
