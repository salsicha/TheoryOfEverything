# Drafts before the human-development revision

These complete drafts preserve the chapter 5 and chapter 8 versions from the twelve-chapter sequence, before they were merged into **The Species That Changed Its Own Conditions**. Their chapter numbers and prose cross-references are historical; the current reading sequence is in the [outline](../outline.md). The current chapter 5 uses human self-domestication and development as its main story, retaining a shorter industrial comparison.

## Former chapter 5 — How Systems Reproduce and Transform Their Constraints

*A system's next possibilities depend on what its present activity renews.*

Imagine a workshop that has made a successful pump. Its drawing survives, the parts fit, and a trial has demonstrated the required movement. A year later, the only person who knows a difficult fitting procedure has left. A measuring instrument is damaged, a supplier has disappeared, and the workshop cannot make a replacement. The pump was possible once. The capacity to produce it has not been preserved.

The preceding chapter explained how existing activity can sustain a new pathway before its return becomes available. We now face a second problem: how does an assembled capability become an organization able to renew itself, and how can that renewal change what becomes possible next?

Autopoiesis gives the first part of the question a precise direction. In the biological formulation, processes produce components that regenerate the network and constitute its organization.[1] The social extension investigated here asks which operations renew the capacities and relationships that make further operations possible. A repeated output is one observable consequence. The explanation concerns the work that keeps producing its conditions.

The chapter's argument has two stages. First, an assembly can direct some of its activity toward maintaining and regenerating the organization through which it operates. Second, the regenerated organization can differ in ways that change its material capacities and formal constraints. A system can therefore sustain a history of development, rather than merely perform the same operation until its inherited parts wear out.

Disequilibrium remains the proposed physical driver. Material transformations power the activities of production, repair, learning, and construction. Assembly establishes their organization; autopoiesis concerns its continuing regeneration. The book's maximizing hypothesis concerns how this development changes the accessible pathways toward greater rates of energy extraction and entropy production under specified constraints. Its operation has to be followed through actual changes in the organization.

Consider a factory organized around a common source of motion. A shaft serves several machines. Their positions, the order of operations, and the timing of work are related to the means of transmitting power. At Cromford, Arkwright and his partners brought water power, machinery, buildings, investment, and workers together in a cotton mill begun in 1771.[2] The site supplies a concrete instance of an organization whose capacities depended on connected operations.

The point of the example is what production has to renew. Machines wear. People require food, rest, and care. Materials must be replaced. Skills have to remain available when a particular worker departs. Schedules and accepted responsibilities connect these activities. The useful output leaving the factory is only one result of the process; another is the changing capacity to operate again.

Some of this renewal occurs outside the factory. A household prepares meals and supplies care. Another enterprise makes replacement parts. A public service may maintain a necessary route or capability. The factory can depend on these activities without containing them. The boundary of the candidate self-producing organization has to follow the dependencies relevant to the explanation and state which inputs arrive from outside.

Regenerative closure concerns the return through those operations. It does not mean that the organization produces its own raw materials from nothing or becomes independent of an environment. It means that its ongoing activities participate in renewing the organization through which further activities occur. A durable account identifies those returns and their limits.

The imagined pump workshop provides a simple test. Making a pump and teaching a replacement worker can compete for the same person's time. Output can rise in the present if teaching is postponed, while the capacity to make future pumps deteriorates. A production record alone misses the loss. To identify the regenerative operation, we would have to observe what teaching preserves and what happens when that support is removed or replaced.

Maintenance has a related structure. A machine can keep running while damage accumulates. If inspection and repair are deferred, the organization can temporarily obtain more output by consuming part of its own future capability. The later failure may look sudden even though the missing renewal has a history. Self-production directs attention to that history before the breakdown becomes visible.

A reserve can preserve the same relation through an interruption. Spare parts, stored supplies, or people able to perform more than one task can make the difference between a temporary disturbance and the loss of a whole capability. Their value belongs to the organization of possible responses. Counting only their present utilization would miss the conditions they help maintain.

This is the first transformation of the argument about constraints. Some constraints are themselves repeatedly produced. A tolerance remains meaningful because instruments are calibrated and parts inspected. A skill remains available because people practice and teach it. A contribution remains expected because records, recognition, and enforcement sustain a relation. If those operations stop, the effective possibilities can change while a written specification remains exactly the same.

The system is jointly material and Platonic in this continuing sense. Physical components realize relations of compatibility, sequence, and dependence. Those relations constrain which combinations can work. Their practical existence requires embodied operations, while the operations themselves depend on the relations. A replacement part must fit both the material situation and the organization of requirements within which it will be used.

Consider a shared standard. Compatible dimensions can allow separate workshops to contribute parts to an assembly. The relation reduces the need to redesign every connection from the beginning. But conformity has to be produced through tools, measurement, interpretation, and rejection of unsuitable work. A standard changes the reachable search only to the extent that an organization can realize and maintain it.

Renewal also permits variation. A workshop can replace a worn tool with one able to achieve a tighter tolerance. That change may make another design practical. A revised procedure can permit a different material. A new means of measurement can expose failures that previous tests could not distinguish. The organization regenerates itself while changing the constraints under which regeneration and further construction occur.

The effect extends beyond one product. A machine tool can produce a component for another machine tool. A measuring device can support the construction of improved measuring devices. Training can prepare someone to teach a successor. These returning relationships allow a productive organization to develop the means of changing its own productive possibilities.

The same history concentrates search. An investigator inherits usable components, established procedures, and demonstrated failures. Combining available elements is different from searching all conceivable configurations with equal probability. Arthur and Polak's model of technological development makes a related accumulation explicit: useful combinations can become components for further construction.[3] The present argument asks how the material capacity to realize those components is maintained and extended.

A design library alone is insufficient. Drawings can survive the loss of the workshop able to make their parts. Conversely, a practical competence can persist without a complete written description. The relevant inheritance is a connected repertoire of realizable operations. Its preservation changes what a later attempt costs, what it can test, and which intermediate results it can retain.

A useful component can also make several later assemblies possible. Its value need not be exhausted by the first product incorporating it. If an improved instrument makes a range of experiments feasible, its production has changed more than the performance of one finished device. It has reorganized a space of accessible next steps.

This supplies a material connection to convergent discovery. Different investigators who can realize the same operations may encounter related constraints, even when their individual routes differ. Their independence does not mean that every resource and relation available to them was independently created. The shared repertoire has its own history of production and renewal. Chapter 7 investigates when that history can make a class of intellectual outcomes robust across different investigators.

The returning organization can also resist a change. Existing equipment, skills, and agreements make familiar operations easier. Their benefits supply reasons to continue using them. Arthur's account of increasing returns and technological lock-in shows how adoption can reinforce an initially favored route.[3] A theory of self-production must be able to explain persistence that narrows subsequent possibilities as well as persistence that expands them.

The factory's power arrangement gives the problem a concrete form. A layout organized around a shared shaft can be productive under that arrangement. Individual electric motors permit different layouts and patterns of operation. Paul David's historical analysis emphasizes the complementary organizational changes required to realize the potential of electrification.[4] Installing a source of power and reorganizing production around its possibilities are different operations.

Imagine a factory retaining its old layout after installing electrical equipment. Further gains might require moving machines, changing material routes, and learning new procedures. Those changes can interrupt the production that pays for the organization attempting them. The established arrangement now supplies both the means of transition and a dependency that makes transition difficult.

This is the dialectical problem in a material form. An organization develops a capability through particular relations. Its continuation can then obstruct a different use or further development of that capability. A successor must preserve enough working capacity to remain viable while changing the relations that have become restrictive. The conflict concerns connected operations that can be investigated, rather than two verbal opposites awaiting a philosophical formula.

Simply destroying the old arrangement does not supply a successor. Removing a shaft may end its spatial constraints while also ending the means of completing current work. A staged conversion, a parallel facility, a reserve, or support from another organization might carry the transition. These are different ways existing activity could sustain the reconstruction of its own conditions.

The barrier from chapter 4 has therefore returned at another level. The problem is now to assemble a changed organization while the existing organization continues to supply indispensable functions. A cheaper final arrangement can remain inaccessible if the transition cannot be carried. Conversely, directing more current resources toward the change can make it feasible without first reducing every cost of the new design.

The formal and material constraints can move together during such a transition. A new machine arrangement changes which sequences are physically practical. A revised allocation of time, access, or responsibility changes which operations people can undertake. Each depends on actual implementation. A new rule can authorize a trial, but the trial still needs equipment and support; a new machine can permit a different task, but the organization still has to make its use possible.

This brings the account toward economics. A factory's technical capabilities do not determine who may authorize a change, who carries its interruption, or who receives the resulting return. Marx's account of reproduction includes the renewal of the social relations under which production proceeds.[5] The next chapter makes those relations central by tracing wages, claims, protection, and monetary obligations through their own recurrent operations.

For the present argument, the key consequence is a possible mismatch between levels. A participant can preserve a claim while weakening a larger productive capacity. An organization can maintain its own operation by shifting necessary work onto households or other institutions. What persists locally and what becomes possible across the wider network can move in different directions.

This is where autopoiesis and the maximizing hypothesis require a connection rather than an equation. Regenerating an organization does not establish that it realizes the greatest accessible rate of entropy production. A well-maintained institution can obstruct another pathway. The energetic account must explain when the development of flows favors continued organization, when it supports a change, and when inherited constraints prevent a transition.

That question cannot be answered by moving the system boundary after observing the result. A useful comparison fixes the relevant network, interval, and alternatives in advance. It follows construction and renewal costs as well as changed operation. If a wider maximizing tendency is proposed, the local processes capable of producing it have to be identified; a persistent obstacle cannot automatically be renamed a preparation for a later improvement.

Consider two illustrative organizations with similar productive equipment. One renews teaching, measurement, and the capacity to test departures. The other retains only the operations required for current output. Over an appropriate interval, they may differ in the successors they can construct even if their initial production totals are similar. The proposed explanation concerns the different capacities being regenerated, not an unexplained advantage assigned to the more successful organization afterward.

The comparison could also fail. Perhaps outside suppliers provide the missing knowledge so effectively that internal teaching makes little difference. Perhaps the trials do not address a relevant constraint. Those results would change the account of the regenerative boundary or the proposed route to new capability. Naming the operations makes such correction possible.

We can now identify the chapter's causal sequence. Activity renews an organization. Renewal preserves or modifies components, skills, and formal relations. Those changes alter the reachable space of subsequent assemblies. Some returns reinforce the established route; others make a successor practical. The organization of that return is the mechanism through which continuity can become development.

The factory is an example within this argument, not its limit. Scientific inquiry must reproduce the means of testing and correction. Artificial intelligence would need a wider network to realize designs, maintain computation, and return physical capacity to further inquiry. The same questions apply: what regenerates what, which inputs remain external, and how does that regeneration change the possibilities of the next operation?

The workshop with which we began failed to preserve a capacity because particular connections disappeared. Another workshop might reproduce the same pump, improve its production, or develop machinery that changes what can be built after it. The difference lies in the operations and relationships its activity renews.

We turn next to the economic system that organizes many of those relationships. Its persistence depends on payments, provisioning, recognized claims, and enforcement regenerating one another. The problem of transformation then becomes historical: when does that closure cease to reproduce an existing order, and what organization can take its place?

## Former chapter 8 — Self-Domestication and the Making of Society

*A species can become part of the environment that selects it.*

Imagine a group returning with food that no one person could have obtained alone. The difficult work appears to be over. The food is present, the participants are hungry, and the means of satisfying them lies within reach. Yet the next interaction can decide whether the undertaking will be repeated.

If the strongest individual can seize the result, everyone else has learned something about the value of cooperating. If a dispute becomes a fight, the group may lose more than the food. If there is a dependable way to divide the return and restrain someone who refuses the arrangement, the successful expedition can become a practice. Its next occurrence depends partly on what happens after the first.

This is an illustration, not a recovered episode of prehistory. Its point is that obtaining a resource and assembling a society able to keep obtaining it are connected problems. The bodies, skills, expectations, and means of settling a dispute belong to the same productive arrangement. A resource can be physically present while a social relation blocks sustained access to it.

We have already encountered this problem in a granary, a factory, and a laboratory. Here we turn back to the animals who make those institutions possible. How did human beings become capable of maintaining such extensive dependencies on one another? What changed when the social world they assembled began to alter the selection pressures acting on them?

Human self-domestication is a proposed answer to part of that question. In its evolutionary sense, the term describes selection within our lineage against tendencies that make some forms of social coexistence difficult, especially reactive aggression. No external breeder is required. Other members of the species help constitute the environment in which different dispositions have different consequences.

This process would connect two histories that are often told separately. People inherit biological capacities that make particular social arrangements possible. Their social arrangements, in turn, can change which biological variants survive and reproduce. The assembly is acting on some of the conditions of its own subsequent assembly.

The book proposes a deeper physical cause for that development: disequilibrium drives assembly toward maximum rates of energy extraction and entropy production within accessible constraints. Human cooperation would be one such pathway. Social selection, care, punishment, and learning are possible mechanisms through which the driver operates. They identify work the explanation must do; calling them shared conditions would leave the central causal question unanswered.

The investigation begins with a distinction inside aggression itself. Richard Wrangham separates reactive aggression, associated with immediate responses to threat or provocation, from proactive aggression, which can be planned and instrumental. He argues that humans combine relatively restrained reactive aggression with a considerable capacity for proactive violence.[1] A group can therefore become easier to live within while becoming more capable of organizing an attack. A decline in one kind of violence does not imply the disappearance of the other.

Return to the imagined distribution of food. Someone who habitually turns frustration into an assault may interrupt every attempt at agreement. Someone capable of patiently organizing a coalition may help prevent those assaults. Both can threaten violence, but the effects on ordinary interaction differ. One makes a shared undertaking unpredictable; the other can help impose a predictable order. Whether that order is just remains another question.

In Wrangham's execution hypothesis, coordinated groups could kill persistently domineering and aggressive individuals. Language would help participants agree on action that none could safely undertake alone. If such deaths systematically reduced the reproductive success of people with heritable tendencies toward reactive aggression, the practice could produce evolutionary change.[2] This is a proposed prehistoric mechanism. Ethnographic accounts of sanctions do not provide a direct census of Pleistocene executions or establish their genetic effects.

The proposition is nevertheless important to the book. Individual physical superiority need not remain the decisive constraint once several individuals can coordinate. The attainable balance of power changes. A person who could defeat each opponent separately may face a coalition that makes continued domination impossible. The social assembly changes the relation between an individual's capacities and their consequences.

There is a crucial interval between changing conduct and changing a population. A threatened person may learn to control an outburst without any genetic change occurring. Children may learn the same restraint through instruction and example. An evolutionary account requires a further connection: inherited variation must affect survival or reproduction under the relevant social conditions. Punishment by itself does not establish that connection, and a death after reproduction may have different consequences from one before it.

These distinctions let us ask precise questions. Which disposition is being constrained? How consistently does the constraint operate? Does it change behavior during one life, cultural transmission across lives, or the frequencies of inherited variants? What other traits vary with it? A theory that moves silently among these possibilities can appear to explain much more than its evidence supports.

Execution is not the only candidate route. Choices of partners and mates, shared care, and learned emotional regulation could also alter the consequences of aggressive behavior. They pose different problems about how cooperation becomes possible and how it persists. The mechanisms may interact, but their relative contributions cannot be settled by arranging them into one appealing story. Wrangham's own comparison of hypotheses makes their differences explicit.[2]

Nor should an account of society begin and end with adult men controlling other adult men. The reproduction of a community includes pregnancies, feeding, protection, childhood, illness, and the transmission of practical knowledge. These activities can occupy different people at different times. An explanation centered only on spectacular confrontations would overlook much of the work that makes repeated collective action possible.

Comparative evidence makes care especially relevant. Judith Burkart and colleagues tested prosocial behavior across fifteen primate species and found that the extent of care by individuals other than the mother was a strong predictor of unsolicited helping in their sample.[3] The result does not reconstruct human prehistory or prove self-domestication. It gives a different empirical starting point: social dependence associated with raising young can help explain variation in cooperation.

Consider the productive consequences of shared care as a conceptual example. One person remains with a dependent child while another acquires food. The first person's contribution may produce nothing that can be counted at the point of collection. Yet the arrangement can change which journeys are possible, how long they can last, and whether the next generation survives to acquire the group's skills. The productive unit cannot be understood by looking only at the person who returns carrying something.

Care also supports a capacity before it can support itself. A learner consumes food and attention while making mistakes. The return, if it arrives, may come much later and benefit people beyond the original caregivers. The relation resembles the support of an early engine in one specific respect: an immature capability requires existing resources during an interval before its eventual contribution. In the human case, that interval is organized through relationships among people.

None of this requires treating affection as a disguised calculation of calories. The experience of caring and the physical consequences of care belong to different levels of explanation. A parent can act from love while participating in an organization with demographic and energetic effects. The proposed physical account must explain how motives and capacities arise without pretending that people consciously pursue a thermodynamic quantity.

The word domestication raises a further question. Why should a proposed change in behavior be connected to changes elsewhere in the body? Wilkins, Wrangham, and Fitch suggested that developmental changes involving neural crest cells could help link traits found in some domesticated mammals.[4] The proposal offers a possible bridge from selection on behavior to a cluster of anatomical and physiological effects. It does not make every resemblance between humans and domestic animals evidence of the same selective history.

The bridge is contested. Lord and colleagues challenged the familiar interpretation of the farm-fox experiment and the claimed generality of a domestication syndrome.[5] Shilton and colleagues offer an alternative emphasis on socially shaped emotional control and plasticity in human evolution.[6] These objections matter because resemblance, a developmental mechanism, and the historical cause of selection are three separate things to establish. Evidence for one cannot simply be counted three times.

For the argument here, the strongest question survives those disagreements. Can a social organization alter the development and selection of the organisms that reproduce it? The self-domestication hypothesis gives that question a particular biological form. Its proposed answer should be investigated alongside alternatives. The book's broader assembly account would be weakened if it required one uncertain anatomical interpretation to carry the entire explanation of society.

It would also be weakened by a chronology in which a fully finished human mind waited passively for history to start. Fossils from Jebel Irhoud place early Homo sapiens roughly three hundred thousand years ago.[7] That date does not mark the completion of every feature of our species. Nor does the much later development of agricultural societies imply that earlier people lacked active social worlds.

The often-used date of around seventy thousand years ago should not become a locked door through which humanity first stepped out of Africa. Evidence for earlier movements complicates that picture. Hershkovitz and colleagues reported a maxilla from Misliya Cave assigned to the Homo sapiens lineage and dated to roughly 177,000–194,000 years ago; aspects of its dating were subsequently debated.[8] Whatever further revisions the record requires, the history cannot be reduced to one sudden departure by an otherwise unchanged population.

Climate belongs in that history as a changing constraint on movement and subsistence. Beyer and colleagues modeled how rainfall availability could open or close migration routes out of Africa over the last three hundred thousand years.[9] A possible route is not evidence that a particular population used it. The relevant implication is that the same social capacities can have different consequences when water, habitat, and the continuity of a journey change.

This is the distinction between a capability and an accessible assembly. A group can possess knowledge of travel and still be unable to sustain a crossing. It can encounter a hospitable region and still fail to retain enough people, skills, or connections to remain there. Neither a genetic date nor a favorable climate is a complete account of expansion. The history has to include their relation to organized activity.

The storage evidence introduced in chapter 4 belongs to much later developments than the early history of our species. They did not create social life from solitary individuals. Storage and cultivation depended on existing capacities for learning, coordination, and managing delayed consequences. They could then transform the scale and stakes of those capacities. A society's means of reproduction became part of the environment to which its members had to adapt.

At this point the joint material and Platonic character of the system becomes particularly clear. A sharing rule specifies a relation among contributions, claims, and returns. That relation can be described independently of any particular meal. For it to organize a meal, however, people must recognize it, remember enough of what happened, and respond to violations. Its formal structure acquires physical force through embodied practices.

Different rules open different possibilities even when the available food and the participants' bodies remain the same. An arrangement that recognizes a delayed contribution can sustain work that would otherwise appear unrewarded. An arrangement that allows each arrival to seize everything may undermine that work. The difference is relational, but its consequences can include who eats, who learns, who leaves, and which undertaking happens again.

A rule also costs something to maintain. Participants must communicate, observe, remember, and sometimes contest a decision. A coalition that restrains a bully needs more than an abstract numerical majority. Its members must be able to act together and bear the consequences. Social organization can change a barrier while also requiring expenditure to keep that change effective.

The autopoietic question concerns the return through these activities. Provisioning supports care and learning; care and learning prepare people to provision, teach, and cooperate in turn. Repeated enforcement can renew a recognized rule, while participants learn how to contest or alter it. To explain the continuity of the social organization, we must follow those operations and their material support.

This social renewal is distinct from producing offspring and from a genetic change in the population. Self-domestication proposes a particular interaction between social conditions and biological selection. An account of the community's ongoing self-production identifies how its practices continue between such evolutionary changes. Neither process can be established simply by observing that a group has survived.

This is where self-domestication meets the earlier steam-engine argument. Some constraints can be relaxed: a less disruptive interaction makes coordination less costly. Other constraints can be crossed through increased commitment: more people contribute food, time, or protection to an undertaking before it pays. The two mechanisms can coexist. A tolerable social environment may make it possible to assemble the support needed for a difficult experiment without making the experiment itself easy.

Imagine, as a second illustration, a community developing a laborious new method of obtaining food. Early attempts yield little. Someone must gather familiar foods while others practice; someone must care for dependents; participants need confidence that a later return will not all be seized. The successful method, if one emerges, can enlarge the resources available for further learning and coordination. Its beginning depends on capacities that precede its eventual success.

The direction of causation matters. Later abundance does not reach backward to feed the first experimenters. Existing food, labor, and relationships carry them through the interval. Subsequent returns can then help preserve or extend the arrangement. The proposal is a history of physically supported transitions, not an appeal to the future as a cause.

Within the book's hypothesis, this is a route by which disequilibrium can build a social means of extending energy extraction. Organized activity accesses food and fuel; some of the resulting capacity supports cooperation and learning; those capacities change which further resources are reachable. Self-domestication would influence the organisms participating in this process, while cultural institutions could change much faster than inherited dispositions.

That causal chain still contains a demanding unresolved step. Greater reproductive success, a larger food return, and a higher rate of entropy production across a specified system are different outcomes. A cooperative group might consume fewer resources to achieve the same result, or use the released capacity to expand its activity. The strong maximizing claim requires tracing what happens next. It cannot be established simply by calling cooperation useful.

The appropriate boundary might include households, their productive activities, and the environment supplying them. It would have to account for the work of care and enforcement as well as collection, processing, and construction. It would also need a timescale long enough to include costly learning but fixed independently of the result. Otherwise any temporary expenditure or saving could be redescribed afterward as confirmation.

This accounting requirement makes the thesis more specific. Shared norms are not an alternative ultimate cause offered in place of disequilibrium. They are proposed physical arrangements through which its tendency could acquire a durable social expression. Whether they actually generate the predicted wider trajectory is an empirical question. A history of assembly must expose that connection to failure as well as success.

The development of society adds another transformation: the means of restraining domination can themselves become means of domination. In the opening illustration, the coalition protects a common undertaking from unilateral seizure. But the people who decide whether a violation occurred can also decide whose account is heard. The capacity to enforce a rule can be used to preserve a fair arrangement, to protect a faction, or to punish an inconvenient departure.

The distinction between reactive and proactive aggression makes this possibility intelligible. Quiet daily interaction can coexist with severe planned punishment. Reliable cooperation within a group can support violence against another group. A population does not need to become uniformly gentle for its members to undertake increasingly elaborate collective projects. Social tolerance concerns particular relationships, and its boundaries can be fiercely maintained.

Here lies a dialectical problem continuous with the preceding chapter. Individual domination can be constrained by collective power. Collective power can then concentrate, making renewed domination possible on a larger scale. No verbal sequence guarantees that outcome. The question is which arrangements preserve the capacity for cooperation while changing who controls its conditions.

The granary helps make the transformation concrete. Storing food increases what a group can carry through time. If access to the store becomes dependent on a small number of people, those people may acquire power over others' ability to wait, learn, or refuse a demand. A capacity that once reduced exposure to environmental uncertainty can create a new social dependency. This is a possible mechanism of concentration, not a claim that every granary produced a state.

Nor does agriculture divide humanity into people who have society and people who do not. The relevant changes concern settlement, productive practices, networks, claims, and forms of authority. Different combinations can persist. A material account should explain those differences rather than arrange living peoples along a single ladder of advancement.

Conformity has a comparable double effect. Copying a practice can spare a learner from repeating dangerous or expensive failures. Expecting others to follow a shared procedure can make a difficult undertaking dependable. Yet the same expectation can penalize the person who notices that a procedure no longer works. A community can preserve its capacity for coordinated action while progressively losing its capacity for correction.

This matters to the question of discovery. A brilliant proposal has little effect if its bearer cannot obtain food, instruction, tools, or an audience. Conversely, a society capable of supporting inquiry can still direct all its support toward familiar answers. The transition from collective discipline to collective intelligence requires arrangements in which inherited knowledge can be questioned without making every shared activity collapse.

The new requirement is neither universal obedience nor universal refusal. It is the ability to distinguish a dependable practice from an unquestionable authority. A claim can be tested; a role can be challenged; a disagreement can persist long enough for evidence to become available. These are social achievements with material costs, not automatic gifts of having a large brain.

A useful research program would therefore compare more than levels of aggression. It would ask how particular forms of restraint affect participation in collective work, how the benefits and costs are distributed, and whether those changes alter survival, reproduction, cultural retention, or productive access. Evidence that a norm spreads would not by itself establish a genetic change. Evidence for a biological change would not identify execution as its cause.

For the book's wider hypothesis, comparisons must also include cases in which cooperation stabilizes a small, durable way of life rather than expanding throughput. They must include communities where greater enforcement suppresses experiment. Such outcomes would help distinguish a general account of social coordination from the stronger claim of energetic maximization. The theory cannot count every successful relationship as support while treating every obstructive one as outside its boundary.

The central possibility is substantial even before those questions are resolved. Humans may have assembled social environments that altered the kinds of humans able to flourish within them. Biological dispositions, learned restraint, care, productive cooperation, and coercion could then develop through interacting histories. Society would be both an achievement of its members and part of the process that makes subsequent members possible.

Return to the food with which we began. The group has more to retain than the location of a resource or a technique for obtaining it. It has a way of undertaking the work together, carrying those who cannot yet contribute, and responding when the arrangement is threatened. That social inheritance changes what the next generation can attempt.

The next chapter follows what happens when such an inheritance includes accumulated ways of knowing. The question becomes how partial minds, joined through teaching, tools, records, and correction, can possess a competence that no one mind contains. Self-domestication concerns a proposed transformation of the animals who participate. Collective intelligence concerns what those animals can assemble between them.
