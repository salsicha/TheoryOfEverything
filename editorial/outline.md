# The World That Builds Itself

*A Material History of Everything*

## The argument

A gradient makes work available. Physical and formal constraints determine which arrangement of existing parts can do that work. If the gain pays for producing the arrangement again, its frequency can rise. The new assembly changes the starting conditions for those that follow. The book asks how far this material history can go, from a quantum ground state to the organization of a power grid.

## Prologue · The Sample Without a Name

Astrobiologist Heather Graham hides the origins of a meteorite sample and bay sediment. A team tests whether repeated molecules with difficult construction histories can give life away. The wager opens the book’s question. What evidence of assembly might survive at quantum, chemical, biological, social and technological scales? A flooded coal mine and a Finnish heating meter set two promises the book will return to. [Read the prologue](../manuscript/book/prologue.md)

## Part I · Physics

Constraint acquires a physical meaning in a slit, a cell, a shaken vessel and a star. At an Atlantic vent, earlier assemblies provide the ingredients and the usable difference for a new sort of chemistry.

<a id="chapter-01"></a>

### 1. What Moves at Absolute Zero?

In Vienna, narrowing a slit spreads the sideways momentum of seventy-carbon molecules. In California, photons scattered by a cooled silicon beam reveal an asymmetry associated with its ground-state fluctuations. What do these measurements entitle anyone to say about absolute certainty, nothingness and the possibility that physical laws themselves have a history? [Read chapter 1](../manuscript/book/01-what-moves-at-absolute-zero.md)

<a id="chapter-02"></a>

### 2. The Square with One Answer

A Sudoku program wastes time filling the first blank while another square already has an answer. A cloudy flask in Richard Lenski’s long-running study offers the harder case. An inherited change makes an existing nutrient accessible. Formal relations constrain both searches, but only the living lineage changes what its descendants can do next. [Read chapter 2](../manuscript/book/02-the-square-with-one-answer.md)

<a id="chapter-03"></a>

### 3. Why the Beads Choose a Side

Ko van der Weele’s team watches a shaken population of glass beads accumulate on one side of a partition. The motor, collisions and a threshold explain why an even split can become unstable. Chien-Shiung Wu’s cobalt experiment asks a different question about left and right. Together they distinguish instability, a physical asymmetry and the need to explain where new gradients come from. [Read chapter 3](../manuscript/book/03-why-the-beads-choose-a-side.md)

<a id="chapter-04"></a>

### 4. What Lights a Star?

A dark stripe in Webb’s picture of L1527 marks the young star’s disk. Follow gas as it loses heat, contracts and warms, then follow a neutrino from solar fusion to Borexino beneath an Italian mountain. Comparing a star with a brown dwarf reveals which earlier steps let nuclear binding energy become available. The elements stars leave behind become raw material for planets. [Read chapter 4](../manuscript/book/04-what-lights-a-star.md)

<a id="chapter-05"></a>

### 5. The White Towers of Lost City

Deborah Kelley’s team takes Alvin down to investigate white towers on an Atlantic undersea mountain. Seawater reacting with rock supplies chemical differences that microbes can exploit. Reuben Hudson’s flow experiment and Eric Smith and Harold Morowitz’s metabolic argument show how a sequence of reactions can open where a single step stalls. Calling life the optimal route requires comparing actual competitors. [Read chapter 5](../manuscript/book/05-the-white-towers-of-lost-city.md)

## Part II · Humans

A living system can produce its parts and leave descendants. A person can also represent her own conduct, anticipate another person’s judgment and alter the conditions in which children grow up.

<a id="chapter-06"></a>

### 6. Who Makes the Boundary?

A link breaks in a computer model built by Varela, Maturana and Uribe. A machine in Bristol turns away from its own charging light. These cases distinguish feedback from the production of the parts that do the regulating. A marine alga and its nitrogen-fixing partner show what a daughter must inherit for the arrangement to become more frequent. [Read chapter 6](../manuscript/book/06-who-makes-the-boundary.md)

<a id="chapter-07"></a>

### 7. The Observer Who Makes Mistakes

Darwin made a practice of recording observations he expected himself to forget. An artificial player estimates where its own attention is directed. A weaver’s teacher decides when a learner should ask for help. These cases develop the book’s definition of intelligence as a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system and ask what performance should suffer when the self-model is lost. [Read chapter 7](../manuscript/book/07-the-observer-who-makes-mistakes.md)

<a id="chapter-08"></a>

### 8. The People Who Divided the Ox

Richard Lee buys a big ox for a feast and hears the people who will eat it dismiss it as skin and bones. The first cut reveals fat. Their account of the teasing, the owner’s right to distribute meat and evidence of shared childcare give substance to the self-domestication hypothesis. Fossils, migration and early grain storage put different parts of the human story on different clocks. [Read chapter 8](../manuscript/book/08-the-people-who-divided-the-ox.md)

## Part III · Technology

Institutions determine who can put labor, machines and information to use. An assembly may spread because it unlocks more energy, because people can enforce a claim, or because it does both.

<a id="chapter-09"></a>

### 9. Who Owes Whom a Living?

A bishop writing from a burned city asks what a lord owes his vassal. Plague-era wage controls, Alice Chaucer’s division of land, a wartime Massachusetts bill and a Lyon weaver’s unpaid forty francs trace what happened when people could demand different guarantees. Marx, Luhmann and cybernetic feedback help explain how claims on labor become durable institutions and how those claims can fail. [Read chapter 9](../manuscript/book/09-who-owes-whom-a-living.md)

<a id="chapter-10"></a>

### 10. A Chariot and a Steam Engine

A clerk at Knossos enters twenty-two pairs of new wheels and a single spare. At Saltom a horse turns a drum while the engine pumps a flooded shaft. At Kaiping, Tang Tingshu prices the carts needed to sell the extra coal. Across these settings, technologies extend power when people can feed, repair, finance and transport what has been built. Military and commercial power shape who can use it. [Read chapter 10](../manuscript/book/10-a-chariot-and-a-steam-engine.md)

<a id="chapter-11"></a>

### 11. What a Ship Could Say

Two publications circulated information about the Liverpool slave ship Brooks. A register let a merchant act on a vessel’s identity and condition. The abolitionists’ print let a reader judge the space allowed a captive. Olaudah Equiano’s experience restores what a diagram omits. A paired visual task shows when communicated confidence helps people act on the quality of another person’s evidence. [Read chapter 11](../manuscript/book/11-what-a-ship-could-say.md)

## Part IV · Paradox

Four apparent improbabilities put the proposed mechanism at risk. In each case the answer depends on which alternatives were physically reachable, how long they had to form and what observations would count against the proposal.

<a id="chapter-12"></a>

### 12. Could Physics Have Acquired Its Laws?

Adam Riess calculates a negative matter density under the assumption of zero cosmological constant. A model of vacuum retention and two photons used as an internal clock give the book’s answer to fine-tuning concrete tasks. Could uncertain physical relations acquire and renew effective constants that favor energy extraction? Could internal time emerge from a symmetry, and what further condition would give it a direction? [Read chapter 12](../manuscript/book/12-could-physics-have-acquired-its-laws.md)

<a id="chapter-13"></a>

### 13. How Soon Could Life Begin?

A Jack Hills zircon, a model of persistent RNA chemistry around 4.33 billion years ago, and a molecular-clock estimate for the common ancestor date three different things. The 1977 drought on Daphne Major shows how quickly selection can act on inherited alternatives. The early-life claim concerns the wait *after* ingredients, energy and a workable route coincide. [Read chapter 13](../manuscript/book/13-how-soon-could-life-begin.md)

<a id="chapter-14"></a>

### 14. Who Has Had Time to Get Here?

Voyager’s plasma instrument identifies its crossing into interstellar space. The five small planets around ancient Kepler-444 make the chemical and chronological version of the Fermi paradox harder. Old planets, old biospheres and old toolmakers have different implications. Probes that can send a result home also have a different reason to travel from populations that must survive the voyage. [Read chapter 14](../manuscript/book/14-who-has-had-time-to-get-here.md)

<a id="chapter-15"></a>

### 15. Two Papers in June

Twenty-five days separate Poincaré’s and Einstein’s June 1905 papers. Rotating an interferometer in Cleveland and synchronizing distant clocks help explain why different questions converged on a relation between time and place. Their remaining disagreement defines the limits of convergence. A later radiation argument shows what conceptual reconstruction preserves, and a satellite clock shows what practical use can keep in circulation. [Read chapter 15](../manuscript/book/15-two-papers-in-june.md)

<a id="epilogue"></a>

## Epilogue · What the Heat Meter Read

In Mäntsälä the servers used more electricity in July, yet the town received more heat in January. The two meters, an array of interconnected processors and a winter cooling decision let the reader follow what computation consumes, earns and makes available. The final comparison asks what evidence would distinguish a useful, recurring assembly from one that achieves the greatest feasible rate of entropy production.
