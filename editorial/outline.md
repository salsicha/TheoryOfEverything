# The World That Builds Itself

*A Material History of Everything*

## The argument

How does change happen? Existing parts can meet, bind, break or be put to a new use. Their material properties and the relations they must satisfy in Platonic space limit the possible next steps. A driving difference supplies work. What sets the pace is how often a particular change can occur, how readily it is undone, and how its occurrence changes those rates for whatever comes next. The sign of that last effect matters. One extra bead can make its neighbors more likely to stay. One extra pump can leave the next investor poorer if coal has nowhere to go.

The book’s stronger claim is that the routes which take hold together attain the greatest sustained entropy production the same system could reach. A route that draws more power can expand when part of the gain pays for more of the means of extraction. A faster route can also be too expensive to start, lose its gains to a rival or feed an already saturated process. Define the rivals, their entry costs, the common boundary and the period *before* looking at the outcome. Predicting which arrangement forms and measuring which one dissipates most are independent ways to put the argument at risk.

## Prologue · The Sample Without a Name

Heather Graham’s meteorite and bay sediment ask what a molecule can tell us about its making. A minimum number of joins leaves open which reaction will occur tomorrow. The pump at Saltom introduces the book’s way of answering that question. Compare water coming in with water lifted out, then trace how the first engine could earn the fuel and money for the next. ## Part I · Physics

Follow the sources and limits of the rates themselves. A driven population moves away from symmetry, a contracting star reaches fusion, and rock and water feed a chemical current. In each case the next step takes time, and its appearance can change the pace of everything that follows.

<a id="chapter-01"></a>

### 1. What Moves at Absolute Zero?

A slit changes a molecule’s momentum distribution, and a cooled oscillator retains fluctuations. When Jezouin’s team heats an electronic island, the temperatures and the allowed quantum states predict how much heat one fully open channel can carry. The team reaches that bound by opening the channel itself. A theory of a world that builds itself must also explain what opens the channel. [Read chapter 1](../manuscript/book/01-what-moves-at-absolute-zero.md)

<a id="chapter-02"></a>

### 2. The Square with One Answer

Sudoku shows how a constraint can remove a whole family of possible moves. In Lenski’s flasks the same two carbon sources could feed glucose specialists, citrate users or a community that exchanged by-products. The long delay tells us that access to a resource and access to the inherited machinery for using it have different clocks. The fate of the carbon would distinguish population growth from greater dissipation. [Read chapter 2](../manuscript/book/02-the-square-with-one-answer.md)

<a id="chapter-03"></a>

### 3. Why the Beads Choose a Side

The escape rates from two piles of beads predict whether a 301-to-299 split will widen or disappear. In an upright heated cylinder, one tall circulation roll carries more heat than two stacked rolls, yet the water spends time in both. Tilt the cylinder and buoyancy favors the tall roll. The measured heat transport rises because the water spends longer in the faster state. [Read chapter 3](../manuscript/book/03-why-the-beads-choose-a-side.md)

<a id="chapter-04"></a>

### 4. What Lights a Star?

A proposed Sun powered by contraction has about thirty million years’ worth of today’s luminosity in its gravitational energy budget. Fusion can sustain the observed output over billions of years. Two nuclear pathways operate in the same core, and their neutrinos distinguish their contributions. Confinement gets a route started, while expansion regulates the rate it actually sustains. [Read chapter 4](../manuscript/book/04-what-lights-a-star.md)

<a id="chapter-05"></a>

### 5. The White Towers of Lost City

Lost City’s alkaline fluid can mix with seawater, react on a mineral or meet catalysts that earlier parcels helped produce. The advantage of a retained catalyst depends on how much reactant would otherwise leave unused and how many parcels pass before the catalyst is lost. These conditions give physical content to the book’s claim that a living population could become the fastest sustained route through a chemical gradient. [Read chapter 5](../manuscript/book/05-the-white-towers-of-lost-city.md)

## Part II · Humans

Cells can turn part of a current into more cells able to draw on it. People can reinvest work in correcting a mistake, raising a child or teaching someone how to get food. The proposed energetic advantage depends on how rapidly those investments yield a return.

<a id="chapter-06"></a>

### 6. Who Makes the Boundary?

A broken link starts a race between repair of a wall and escape of the catalyst needed to repair it. In a marine alga either partner can limit growth. A model predicts the relative sizes at which the partners can feed each other, and coordinated division passes both on. Repair, multiplication and extra dissipation each require their own evidence. [Read chapter 6](../manuscript/book/06-who-makes-the-boundary.md)

<a id="chapter-07"></a>

### 7. The Observer Who Makes Mistakes

Darwin kept a record of what he tended to forget. A player that tracks its own attention illustrates the book’s definition of intelligence as hierarchical and recursive self-modeling. Three wind turbines show what one part of that ability can buy. Under winds that put one rotor in another’s wake, a controller that models the array’s own effect can sacrifice upstream power and deliver more electricity from the same machines. [Read chapter 7](../manuscript/book/07-the-observer-who-makes-mistakes.md)

<a id="chapter-08"></a>

### 8. The People Who Divided the Ox

An ox shared at a feast changes who eats. A child who can pick berries can change how much food a family brings home. At a Hadza camp the measured returns predict opposite choices for two families with the same nine hours and different numbers of children. Cooperation can pass on a productive arrangement. Genetic self-domestication would also require that heritable differences affected who raised descendants. [Read chapter 8](../manuscript/book/08-the-people-who-divided-the-ox.md)

## Part III · Technology

A harvest can wait only so long. A flooded mine can lose a seam overnight. An institution, a machine or a message can alter how fast energy and goods pass through a society. The people who bear the costs may have good reason to resist a route that pays someone else.

<a id="chapter-09"></a>

### 9. Who Owes Whom a Living?

A higher wage could save a harvest or simply move a reaper from the next field. A union fare could put an idle carpenter to work where a vacancy had gone unfilled. The harvest deadline, the availability of hands and the authority to change the terms explain when an institution can increase production and who receives the return that keeps the bargain in force. [Read chapter 9](../manuscript/book/09-who-owes-whom-a-living.md)

<a id="chapter-10"></a>

### 10. A Chariot and a Steam Engine

Water must be lifted before more coal can be raised. At Saltom, pumping with coal and winding with horses could put more fuel on the market for years. At Kaiping, Tang’s freight budget predicted where a pump alone would strand saleable coal. The rate of inflow predicts the pressure to change. Transport, customers and credit help predict whether an engine gets built. [Read chapter 10](../manuscript/book/10-a-chariot-and-a-steam-engine.md)

<a id="chapter-11"></a>

### 11. What a Ship Could Say

A register helped strangers finance ships. A picture of a slave ship could stop at one reader or circulate through a petition to people with power over many sailings. The Commons’ 1792 decision to delay abolition identifies a real alternative to immediate prohibition. Enforceable decisions changed which voyages could proceed. Port records would have to establish what happened to ships, cargoes and fuel afterward. [Read chapter 11](../manuscript/book/11-what-a-ship-could-say.md)

## Part IV · Paradox

Fine-tuned laws, an early origin of life, an apparently empty galaxy and nearly simultaneous discoveries pose four questions about timing. Each asks what fixed the earliest possible opening, what happened when it arrived and what evidence could expose a longer wait.

<a id="chapter-12"></a>

### 12. Could Physics Have Acquired Its Laws?

Cold dust reradiating starlight lets cosmologists compare modeled universes with different vacuum energies. A larger region can produce a greater lifetime total while an equal parcel produces entropy at much the same rate. For physics itself to have been assembled, rival field states must be reachable under common starting laws, and the flow through the faster state must help it prevail over trapping in a slower one. An internal clock gives this conjecture a timescale. [Read chapter 12](../manuscript/book/12-could-physics-have-acquired-its-laws.md)

<a id="chapter-13"></a>

### 13. How Soon Could Life Begin?

The book argues that life took the first enduring opening on Earth. Estimate how often supplied pores produced a useful combination and how often it survived washout and left descendants. The waiting time then becomes a prediction. RNA catalysts competing for one feed illustrate what can happen after copying begins. The 4.33-billion-year model for persistent RNA chemistry and the roughly 4.2-billion-year estimate for an ancestral population date distinct events. [Read chapter 13](../manuscript/book/13-how-soon-could-life-begin.md)

<a id="chapter-14"></a>

### 14. Who Has Had Time to Get Here?

Old planets test the proposal that durable chemical gradients suitable for life arose comparatively late. Voyager 2’s power shortage shows why one scientific route can repay a fixed energy supply better than another while both release much the same heat. A probe can beat a passenger for a specified observation. For probes to spread through the Galaxy, builders at a destination would also have to outlast the time required to make successors. [Read chapter 14](../manuscript/book/14-who-has-had-time-to-get-here.md)

<a id="chapter-15"></a>

### 15. Two Papers in June

The book argues that relativity was discovered as soon as its experimental and mathematical prerequisites came together. A round-trip clock and ruler conversion shows which result a physicist could predict before June 1905. Poincaré and Einstein reached common ground twenty-five days apart while their accounts of the ether still differed. The constraints explain convergence more tightly than they date it. Later satellite clocks give a direct test of what that knowledge can do. [Read chapter 15](../manuscript/book/15-two-papers-in-june.md)

## Epilogue · What the Heat Meter Read

One Finnish data center drew 4.58 MW. The table compares its dissipation with lunar rock, a microbe, an animal and England and Wales in 1560 and 1950. An equal-powered resistor ties the computers at a common final temperature. Orders, cooling, a grid connection and the town’s use of the waste heat decide whether a computing center induces greater sustained dissipation across the wider system. A queue of customers and spare grid capacity give a different forecast from vacant racks on a rationed grid. [Read the epilogue](../manuscript/book/epilogue.md)
