# Chapter 11 — LEO Takes the Bakery Orders

At one of the Lyons teashops, the staff used to ask customers to leave the telephone free for a few minutes each day. The shop had no private line. When the phone rang, a voice said, “LEO calling.” By then the staff had their figures written down. Someone read out what the shop wanted. In due course a sheet came back showing the whole order.[1]

LEO was the Lyons Electronic Office, a computer at the company’s London headquarters. There was a person on the other end of the phone. Some Lyons shops had no telephone at all. An employee had to go outside at the agreed time and wait beside a public phone for the call. This was how a business that owned one of the most advanced calculating machines in Britain got its figures from the counter to the bakery in time to bake.[1][2]

What the employee said in those few minutes explains why the computer was worth building.

## Before the ovens could start

J. Lyons & Co. supplied about two hundred teashops. Each afternoon a shop manager had to order from hundreds of bakery and kitchen products for the following day. Under the old system the written orders had to reach the Hammersmith works in person. Then someone had to turn a pile of sheets, each describing the needs of one shop, into instructions for all the people who would make, pack and deliver the food overnight. The food had to be fresh. Every hour the office spent sorting orders was an hour the kitchens lost from the night’s production.[3]

The difficulty is easiest to see in a single entry. The quantity of scones ordered by one shop matters in two different ways. The baker needs it added to all the other shops’ scones before mixing begins. The packer needs to keep it attached to that particular shop and to all the other goods on its order. The person loading the van needs to know where the shop comes on the route. A correct total for the whole bakery is compatible with two shops getting each other’s orders. Getting the quantities right and keeping them attached to the right places were separate jobs.[1][2][3]

By 1947 the company was already in the habit of studying how its office work was done. It sent two managers, Raymond Thompson and Oliver Standingford, to the United States. That October they put a report before the board recommending that Lyons investigate electronic computing. At Cambridge, Maurice Wilkes was building a stored-program computer called EDSAC. Lyons contributed to the project and undertook to build a machine adapted to its own work. Engineer John Pinkerton led the construction. David Caminer, who knew the office procedures from the inside, worked out what jobs to give it. A stored program meant that the same circuits could value a week’s baking or prepare a payroll when supplied with different instructions. Each set of instructions still had to be written.[3][4]

The obvious attraction was the speed of calculation. Caminer’s harder problem lay at either end of the machine. Cards and tapes had to be prepared, results had to come out in usable form, and the bakery had a deadline. When the magnetic tape equipment the project had counted on proved impractical, major jobs had to wait. Caminer proposed starting with an easier one. The accounts department needed a weekly valuation of what the bakeries at Cadby Hall had produced, sent out and held in stock. Its figures could wait longer than an order for tomorrow. The first complete run finished on November 30, 1951. Lyons went on running the job every week. The people who eventually entrusted the night’s baking to LEO first learned how to get a week’s accounts out of it.[3][5]

## What did a manager have to say twice?

Caminer worked through stacks of old shop orders. A Monday at a given shop tended to resemble its previous Mondays. There were seasonal changes and differences between shops. Both mattered. An average order for the entire chain would require a manager to correct the ways in which her shop *usually* differed from the others, every afternoon of her working life. Caminer’s team used a normal order for that shop and that day of the week. That was the order the shop would get unless the manager changed it.[2][3]

Now the person on the telephone could read out just the changes. If most lines stood as usual, the call could be short even for a large shop. If local conditions were unusual, the manager could change as many items as she thought necessary. The new rule preserved her knowledge of the people who bought food there. It also spared her the daily work of describing their ordinary habits to head office. In an age of public phones and card punches, that saving bought minutes for everybody downstream.[1][2]

At headquarters an order taker punched the amendments. Once they had been collected, LEO applied them to the standing orders. One set of entries could now answer the baker’s question and the packer’s. It yielded the quantities needed in the kitchens, the goods and packing materials for each shop, and instructions for assembling loads in delivery order. It also supported stock records and charges between departments. A manager’s decision about tomorrow’s scones could reach the baker as part of a total and the loader as part of one consignment. Nobody in the van needed to know the bakery total. Nobody mixing a batch needed to know the route.[1][2][5]

A clerk with a file of cards could have used Caminer’s Monday rule. It would still have taken someone to gather the amendments from two hundred shops, revise the kitchen totals, then work back out to the separate consignments before the vans left. Conversely, a computer waiting for two hundred complete paper forms would have inherited the old delay. The shop routine and the program each removed a different part of the wait. Together they gave the kitchen more of the night in which to bake.[2][3]

A punched card had served another purpose in Michel-Marie Carquillat’s workshop in Lyon a century earlier. It told the loom which warp threads to lift as the weaver made his silk portrait. A card punched during the London phone call supplied a quantity to instructions already in the computer. Changing the quantity changed one shop’s order. Changing the instructions could give every baker a different way of reading the next day’s work.[5][6]

The ordering system went into regular service in October 1954. A few days later the Wembley teashop sent head office a line in its daily report. Staff there wanted to thank the people responsible for the time and work the change had saved them. That message tells us who felt a gain, and where. A Wembley worker had one less chore to do in the afternoon. A saving in flour or fuel across the whole chain would have left its trace in different records.[3]

Accurate orders acquired greater reach in this system. So did mistakes. One wrong quantity could reach the kitchen, the packer and the accounts office, with three sets of paperwork that agreed perfectly. Lyons kept the shop in the conversation. The worker who remembered the shared telephone also remembered receiving a written statement of the order. Caminer’s team built checks into its programs and planned how to restart an interrupted run. In the bakery valuation job they reconciled figures that came from different parts of the business. Agreement between independent counts could reveal something that three copies of one wrong entry never would.[1][2][3]

## Which jobs came back?

Several other early LEO jobs help explain precisely what made the shop orders worth running again. Britain was still rationing food when Lyons designed a program to allocate ingredients from reserve stores. The company had stocked sweetened fat among other materials to get through sugar shortages. When rationing ended, it could dispense with the reserve stores. Frank Land, one of the systems analysts, recalled the fate of the program. It was dropped. The computer could still have run it. The condition that had made the calculation worth doing had gone.[2]

Something similar happened to some of the management reports. The team had worked hard to calculate figures for individual shops. Land recalled that some reports made much less difference than their designers expected. A figure costs little to print once it has been computed. Its value still depends on who receives it, what that person can change, and whether the change is worth making. The packers had an immediate use for the delivery sequence. A shop manager handed another performance statistic might have no corresponding decision to make.[2]

Other jobs travelled. Ford managers came to Lyons to see its payroll. Their workers had different pay arrangements, but the two payrolls shared enough structure for the programmers to reuse some code. In 1955 Caminer drafted a specification for Ford in May. In November the new and existing procedures ran alongside each other. In December the first live payroll ran on Lyons’ machine. Ford could buy a result before buying a computer. Lyons could use both the machine and part of what its staff had learned in a business with no particular demand for cakes. This is one route by which computerization left the bakery.[3]

In Middletown a wire had let one steam engine do work at places a single shaft could scarcely reach. At Lyons instructions and records could travel through a business while keeping the connection between an order and the goods it represented. The physical inheritance included a power supply, electronic circuits, telephones, card punches and vans. The formal inheritance included the shop’s identity, the usual quantities for its Mondays, the arithmetic that joined its orders to the other shops’, and the sequence of delivery. These are the material and Platonic sides of the book’s argument. Change the rule for what a shop must report, and the same length of telephone call can direct much more work. Take away the person with access to the phone, and the fastest computer in London has yesterday’s information.

This adds a qualification to the proposal that new assemblies can put more energy to work. The program supplied neither heat to the ovens nor fuel to the vans. It could change when the ovens were fired, how much they were asked to bake and what the vans carried. The overnight deadline made those distinctions valuable enough for a catering company to develop a computer. Every minute saved before the bakers received their totals was available for production. A claim about total fuel use would also need to follow what was baked, what sold and what went stale.

There was still something no part of LEO had been instructed to do. It could carry out the rule for a standard order. It had taken Caminer, studying those piles of paper, to notice that last Monday could tell him something about next Monday. The possibility of giving part of *that* work to a machine begins the next chapter.
