# Chapter 10 — The Factory That Kept Its Steam Engine

In February 1897 the Keating Wheel Company had a new bicycle factory in Middletown, Connecticut, but its machines were still in Holyoke, Massachusetts. The old works had the season’s bicycles to make. As soon as the company could spare a machine there, it would move it to Middletown. An engineering journal described the move while it was still in prospect. It also described what would be waiting for the machines. Fifty-horsepower electric motors hung from the ceiling of the new factory. In a separate building stood a steam engine.[1]

Robert Keating had helped design the works. He and his builder, Casper Ranger, had laid out a main building a thousand feet long. Six shops projected from it at right angles. Keating wanted room to expand his bicycle business, and Middletown had offered tax relief to bring him there. Those facts explain why he was building. They leave an odd feature of his design to explain. He had bought an engine perfectly capable of turning a shaft. Why pay for a generator, wiring and a set of motors to turn a shaft all over again?[1][2]

## Turning the corner

Start at a lathe in a shop powered by steam. The work turns against a cutting tool. Follow the drive belt upward and it runs around a pulley on a revolving shaft. Other belts off the same shaft run to other machines. Somewhere along the line an engine supplies the turning force. One engine can do the work of many. The men who set out the shop must settle where the shaft can run, how to support it and which machines a belt from it can reach.[3]

This was a good way to power machines in a row. It was a harder way to get power to six rows running at right angles to a seventh. A shaft can turn a corner with the right transmission. Each turn calls for equipment, room to install it and bearings to keep in order. Keating also had a reason to keep certain trades apart. His company’s account places the machine tools on the ground floor, away from the grinding and polishing that threw dirt into the air. Upstairs, workers built wheels, assembled bicycles and crated them. The builder had to satisfy the people who needed a clean work surface as well as the people who needed turning power. Simply adding horsepower would do nothing about the dust.[1][2][3]

At Middletown the steam engine drove a generator by a belt. Wires carried current from the generating plant to the shops. Only there did electric motors turn the shafts that ran the tools. On the ground floor of the main building there were still about eight hundred feet of shafting in all. It ran in three sections. Smaller motors drove shafts out in the shops. The motors had pulleys on both ends, enough to drive as many as four shafts apiece. The company could move a belt-driven machine out of Holyoke and give it a place on a belt in Middletown. The long mechanical connection back to the engine had disappeared.[1]

What Keating bought was a choice about where to make the last conversion from electricity to motion. He could take a wire around a corner and put a motor on the far side of it. Within a group of machines the familiar restrictions still held. Between groups, the builder could lay out the shops according to the work each had to do. The grinders could work some distance from the machine tools and draw power from the same engine. The separation Keating wanted for the sake of the work became easier once a wire could join the rooms electrically.[1][2][3]

## How much of the old shop was worth keeping?

There were at least three ways for a manufacturer to buy electric power. One large motor could take over the job of the old engine and turn the whole system of shafts. Several motors could each take a group, as at Middletown. Or a motor could drive a single machine. All three arrangements would count as electrification. They gave a machinist different working conditions.[3]

A single motor at the head of an existing line was a sensible purchase for an owner with serviceable shafting and a supplier willing to sell current. The machines could stay put. Group drive bought another degree of independence. One shop could stop its motor while another worked on. A motor for each machine carried the change further. A worker could stop one tool without having to keep its share of a long shaft revolving. Each machine’s drive could be sized and controlled for its own work. Moving the tool close to the next operation became a matter of finding suitable floor space and an electrical connection. Someone still had to pay for all those motors. A row of similar machines that ran the same hours could continue to earn its keep on a common drive.[3]

The order in which these arrangements became attractive helps explain why electrification took decades. Keating was buying a building. He could use the new freedom while his builder was still deciding where to put the walls. An owner across town with years of service left in a shaft faced the cost of discarding it, stopping the shop and moving machinery. For that owner, waiting until a line needed replacing could make good sense. As machines and buildings came due for replacement, more manufacturers faced Keating’s choice. In Warren Devine’s reconstruction from the US manufacturing censuses, electric motors supplied less than five percent of the capacity available to drive factory machinery in 1899. By 1929 they accounted for about seventy-eight percent. Those figures measure installed capacity. They tell us how far the change spread. They cannot tell us how many bicycles any one shop sold.[3]

The steam engine out back matters to the book’s argument. Its boiler already had access to heat, and its piston already produced work. Electricity gave some of that work a route to a place where it had been difficult to use. The generator and the motor each incurred a loss. A manufacturer could willingly incur both if a better arrangement meant that workers spent more of their day making things and less of it hauling them between machines. Samuel Vauclain, superintendent of the Baldwin Locomotive Works, later said that he would have chosen electric drive even at a substantial penalty in power consumed. The gain he valued occurred on the shop floor. An explanation that looks only at the coal bill misses a decision a manufacturer was prepared to defend in public.[3]

At Keating’s we know the arrangement the company chose. We even have a glimpse of what went out the door. In April 1898 the local paper recorded a Saturday shipment of over two hundred bicycles. Bicycle prices fell, and in 1899 part of the factory was given over to motor vehicles. In June 1901 the plant was sold to the Eisenhuth Horseless Vehicle Company. A power system could make another product physically possible. It could do little to hold up the selling price of a bicycle. Nor does the sale tell us the motors failed. A building and its equipment were valuable enough for another manufacturer to take them over. The company, the machinery and the method of sending power through a building had three different histories.[2]

## Who pays for the wire?

Inside Keating’s walls there was one buyer and a shopful of machines at the end of a relatively short wire. The same wire presented a different proposition outside town. Bartlett, Texas, had electric service in 1905. Farmers living around it waited another thirty years. There was plenty a farm family could do with electricity. There were also long stretches of line to build between customers. The cost of the first miles fell due before the first family could turn on a light.[4]

In October 1935 three farmers signed a charter to bring power to the countryside around Bartlett. A federal Rural Electrification Administration loan of $33,000 financed the first fifty-nine miles of distribution line. Employees and volunteers set poles by hand. In March 1936 Charles Saage threw the switch at his farmhouse. The loan let the farmers pay for a line out of the service the completed line would sell. They had changed who could bear the cost of building it and when that cost had to be met. The electrical properties of the wire had been available to them all along.[4]

The farm makes clear something easy to overlook in the factory. A gradient can be present for years while the people who could use it lack a way to meet the first expense. Sometimes a change in the shape of the apparatus opens the way. Sometimes people have to change the terms on which they can build it. The book proposes that assemblies able to put more available energy to work acquire more opportunities to be built again. Here we can identify the reason another shop or another group of households might pay for the next installation. Establishing which arrangement dissipated energy fastest would require a different reckoning, including the generating plant, the load over time and the work the competing arrangements could deliver.

Back in Holyoke, the condition for releasing a machine was the completion of its share of the season’s orders. In Middletown its new motor answered the question of how to turn it. Neither the engine nor the motor could tell Keating when it was safe to move. Making that sort of decision across a business required another means of connecting the people doing the work. At a London bakery, the connection began with a telephone call.
