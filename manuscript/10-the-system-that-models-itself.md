# Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

**Part IV · Cybernetics**

In Bristol, in the middle of the twentieth century, the neurophysiologist W. Grey Walter put a mirror in front of a tortoise. The animal performed a little dance. It was a performance sufficiently unlike anything it did in the ordinary course of its day that Walter thought a biologist might have credited it with recognizing itself. Walter had built the tortoise. He knew what happened when its light went out.[1]

The creature had wheels, a shell, a photoelectric eye and a small indicator lamp. The instant the eye detected its own light in the mirror, the lamp went out. This deprived the eye of the very thing it had detected, whereupon the lamp came on again. The light flickered and the tortoise jigged. An observer behind it saw an animal doing something distinctive in the presence of its own image. A suitable white surface could draw much the same performance. A great deal depends on which side of the shell one occupies.[1]

The January 1841 report from Lyon tells us that Bron’s employer saw the apprentice falling asleep and that his mother had warned the employer about it. Bron’s own account is missing. There is an important capacity at issue here, beyond anything Walter put in his animal. A person can discover something about the reliability of his own judgment and change the way he works. Charles Darwin left a record of doing precisely that.[2]

## A drink that nobody gets

Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow opened a little investigation into purpose in 1943 with the movement of a glass toward a mouth. A person generally knows where the drink is going well before settling the successive angles of the elbow. How, then, does the hand arrive? Once it is moving, information about how far the glass has got can help determine where the hand goes next.[3]

On the following page the authors hand a glass to a patient with a disorder of the cerebellum. This is a clinical illustration. At rest the hand is steady. As the glass approaches the mouth, it swings to one side, corrects, and swings farther to the other. Water spills. It would be quite unfair to attribute the result to any deficiency in the man’s desire for a drink. Something has gone wrong with the relation between where the glass is and how vigorously the hand tries to put it right.[3]

In 1948 Wiener gathered questions like these into a book called *Cybernetics*. He gave an engineer trying to steady the speed of a machine and a physiologist studying a shaking hand a reason to take an interest in each other’s work. In both cases the consequence of a movement can affect the next movement. The patient’s wobbling glass disclosed something easy to miss when a healthy person takes a drink. Increasing the response to an error can make the error grow. *Feedback* names the route the information takes. What happens depends on what the recipient does with it.[3]

In the weaving films from Nabenchauk the route went through different people a generation apart. Katal Pavlu’s mother saw where her daughter needed help and put her hands to the loom. When Katal’s daughter Loxa needed her older sister Xunka’, the request came from Loxa. Some of what the younger weaver had acquired was the judgment to decide when to call. A pattern could settle which threads to lift. It took watching the two generations at work to discover who could act on a difficulty with the pattern.[4]

W. Ross Ashby gave this problem an exact formulation. The variety of outcomes a regulator can secure depends on the variety of responses available to it. In Bristol, Walter’s animal could turn away from light, approach it or stop at the charging contacts. Which of those responses the same lamp called forth changed as the battery ran down. Connecting the state of the battery to the circuit gave its builder a new use for an old signal. A light which had meant an obstacle could now lead the animal to power.[1][5]

## How the tortoise got its supper

Walter put a lamp inside the hutch and a charger far enough in for a tortoise to reach it. A well-charged animal approaching the entrance encountered a light bright enough to turn it away. As its battery ran down, its response changed. Now it could go right up to the charger. Contact cut out the motors while the battery charged. Afterward the animal could leave under its own power. The Science Museum keeps one of the hutches. Its entrance marks a physical threshold that had acquired two quite different meanings for the same machine.[1]

The new achievement was considerable. The battery’s condition now helped determine where the machine went to get energy. Walter had supplied the electrical contacts, the light and the current. His animal could find the source for itself. It could also dance in front of a mirror. Both performances followed from the wiring. Neither required the tortoise to judge whether it had been a good judge of light.

Varela, Maturana and Uribe’s model of a cell took up a further question. Some components in their simulated world joined into a boundary around the activity that produced them. Fresh components formed in the presence of a catalyst and replaced ones that broke down. The enclosing structure altered the conditions under which the next components formed. They called the organization *autopoietic*. What crossed Walter’s threshold was an animal going home. What came into existence in the cell model was the boundary itself.[1][6]

This is where autopoiesis and cybernetics meet. A boundary helps regulate what can pass. When the reactions on the inside help produce that boundary, the regulation has a consequence for the construction of the cell. We can now ask two different historical questions. How did a new way to use a gradient first become possible? Once achieved, what gave later cells that possibility at birth? Self-production has its explanatory place in the second question. It presupposes that there is already some way of doing the work.[6]

A comparable distinction ran through the case of Bron. The Lyon labor council had the authority to dissolve an apprenticeship. Its ruling could settle an obligation and change who controlled the boy’s time. That power alone could never have taught him to weave. Marx’s question about how a skilled workforce comes into existence meets Luhmann’s about what an authorized communication can set in motion. A society gains new capacities when there are people with the training to act and arrangements that let their judgment alter what happens.[2][4]

## What Darwin wrote down

Looking back on the making of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin described a rule he had followed for years. Whenever he came on an observation or a thought that went against his general conclusions, he made a note of it at once. He had discovered a curious partiality in his own memory. The inconvenient facts slipped away more readily than the ones that suited him. Left to an honest recollection of the evidence, he might at length have remembered an excellent case for an inferior theory.[7]

Darwin’s rule operated at more than one level. An observation bore on a conclusion. His estimate of his memory determined which observations he must put on paper. The account he gave of himself as an observer thus changed the evidence available to him as a theorist. Anatomists can trace hierarchy of a different kind. Nikola Markov and colleagues mapped pathways in the macaque visual cortex and found distinctive patterns of connections running up and back down between areas. That anatomy establishes routes for influence in both directions. Darwin’s memorandum tells us something an anatomical map alone cannot tell us about the use a thinker makes of such a capacity.[7]

His remedy was economical. He could leave many agreeable observations to a memory already well disposed to keep them. It was the objections that had to be given another chance. He had, in effect, applied his gift for noticing patterns to his own habit of noticing them. This is the sense of *recursive* that matters here. The method available for judging the world had itself become something he could judge and change.[7]

By *intelligence* I mean **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. The self-model can be as modest as Darwin’s knowledge that he forgot some kinds of evidence faster than others. It earns its place in the explanation by the new action it makes possible. A man who had worked out which arguments his memory would quietly lose could arrange to meet those arguments again.[7]

What might such an arrangement buy a machine? Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano gave an artificial player a ball to catch. On a square field a ball falls toward a paddle. Most of the field is obscured by visual noise. The player can move a clear patch over it and can move the paddle. The researchers also gave the player a second square. It showed where the clear patch was. There was no ball in that second display. One could study it as long as one liked and learn only where one was looking.[8]

After training the player with both displays, the researchers took away the map of the clear patch. The patch itself continued to clear the view wherever it went. Yet the player had more difficulty following the ball and making the catch. It had been using a fact about its own attention to control its attention. Walter’s mirror lets us see why the extra display matters. A creature can react strikingly to its reflection with a lamp and a switch. Here we can withdraw the information about an activity while leaving the means to perform the activity in place, and watch what the player loses. The score answers a question about what that information did for the player. Whether it felt the frustration of missing is a question no ball in the game was equipped to answer.[1][8]

My conjecture is that a human self takes shape as a mind comes to model its own activity across time. Darwin was able to identify a failing that extended over many observations and to adopt a rule for the naturalist he would be the next time a difficulty arose. The Bristol animal, the paddle player and Darwin make different demands on an explanation. The tortoise reacted to its reflection. The player drew on a representation of its own attention. Darwin could change the standard by which he judged the observer. Only in the last case do we also have the participant’s account of what that was like.[1][7][8]

## What a foundation bought for $7,500

In January 1947 Wiener published a letter declining to send an aircraft researcher a paper from his wartime work. He knew what project the man had in mind. After Hiroshima and Nagasaki he wanted scientists to take responsibility for what became of the knowledge they handed over. The government owned the paper, as Wiener pointed out, and the man was free to ask for it there. What Wiener could withhold was his own participation.[9]

Eight years later John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon asked the Rockefeller Foundation for $13,500 to bring a group together at Dartmouth the following summer. They called their subject *artificial intelligence*. Robert Morison, writing for the foundation, offered $7,500. In his letter of November 30, 1955, he explained that mathematical models of thought seemed promising and were hard to get a clear view of. The foundation was willing to finance a small gamble. It had also helped pay for Wiener and Rosenblueth to study feedback. Morison’s stated concern was how much risk the idea justified. One might reasonably ask whether Wiener’s politics made other sponsors favor the new name. Following that money would take other letters and accounts. For this particular summer, both traditions had found a benefactor at the same address.[9][10]

Names do help decide who gets to meet and what questions they spend the summer asking. McCarthy later recalled making a point of avoiding Wiener at MIT. He had read *Cybernetics* and expected a difference of opinion. Wiener wanted to know how the distance left to go could guide the correction of a movement. McCarthy wanted to know what a machine could deduce from the facts available to it. The Dartmouth application made room for both deductions and networks of neurons that might learn concepts. A name capacious enough to accommodate both projects is an excellent name for bringing people together and an unfortunate substitute for saying how a particular machine works.[10][11]

McCarthy provided his own illustration. By his account, when he set out to write a chess program, the rules for legal moves went well. Strategy went rather less well. He handed the program to students, who finished it. There is the difficulty with the logic-first account of intelligence in a form a child with a chess set can appreciate. It can be quite clear which moves are legal and an excellent idea to decline nearly all of them. In the wider world the difficulty begins even earlier. Someone has to recognize which pieces are on the board.[11]

One of the images Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton published in 2012 shows a mite well away from the center of the frame. Their network identified it. Nobody had supplied a rule specifying where in a photograph a mite belonged. People had labeled roughly 1.2 million training images. Correct and incorrect classifications changed the strengths of connections in a network whose architecture allowed a feature useful at one spot to be useful at another. Two graphics processors did the training. The new capacity lay in the combination. The labels told the machine whether its guesses had been any good. The architecture determined which lessons it could carry from one photograph to the next.[12]

On the separate 2012 ImageNet challenge the team’s combined entry missed the right category among its first five guesses on 15.3 percent of the test images. The runner-up missed on 26.2 percent. Deep learning had given people who wanted to build a better classifier a reason to change how they went about it. I think McCarthy was wrong about the primacy of explicit reasoning. It assigned too little of the work of intelligence to acquiring the distinctions from which a piece of reasoning can begin. Learning from error, a project with a long career in cybernetics, came to enjoy one of its greatest successes under the name its old rival had chosen.[12]

Formal reasoning has a further part to play. At the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, people translated five non-geometry problems into a formal language for a system called AlphaProof. It solved three, using learning to guide a search for steps a proof checker could verify. It had much more computing time than a contestant had at a desk. The machine could learn which move to try because, once it had tried a move, the rules could tell it something dependable. McCarthy’s unfinished chess program would have presented a rather different prospect if he could have bought a supply of good judgment one checked move at a time.[13]

The Toronto group had joined three things that already existed, labeled photographs, an architecture that could reuse a feature across an image, and processors that could afford the repetitions. Together they put a new distinction into a machine. That is why the mite belongs in a history that began in a flooded mine. In each place a specific constraint directed work toward a capacity that an increase in raw power alone would have left unspecified.[12]

The mite also marks a limit. The researchers chose the photograph and ran the classifier. Identifying an animal in an image and deciding there is an image worth examining are different accomplishments. Long before anybody was training a network, Ada Lovelace had asked how much of what a machine did could properly be credited to the machine.
