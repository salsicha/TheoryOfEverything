# Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

*The regulator must also be renewed.*

Imagine carrying a full cup across an uneven floor. Your undertaking is simple enough to name: take the drink to someone in the next room. Its execution involves many adjustments you would struggle to list. The cup tilts; your hand changes position. A foot encounters an irregularity; your next step changes. You slow down near the doorway. The success of the undertaking depends on corrections made before any single error becomes a spilled drink.

Now imagine recognizing that you are too tired to carry it safely. You put the cup on a tray or ask someone else to help. Something has changed in the organization of the task. You are responding to a judgment about the person carrying the cup. Your own capacity has become part of what you are taking into account.

The scene is an illustration, not an experiment in brain anatomy. It introduces three questions. How does activity correct itself? How does the organization performing those corrections remain capable of acting? And what becomes possible when that organization represents aspects of itself?

The preceding chapter followed the operations through which a society produces another day of its existence. Food arrives, equipment is maintained, work is organized, and relationships of contribution and entitlement are renewed. Among the capacities sustained by those operations are the abilities to notice a discrepancy, anticipate a difficulty, learn a distinction, and change a course of action. These capacities have their own material histories. The society capable of supporting inquiry is supporting bodies and institutions in which regulation can become investigation.

That chapter placed the Marx–Luhmann synthesis within the creation cycle: material production sustains communicative organization, which directs subsequent production. Cybernetics explains regulation within the required autopoietic return. The relevant next level is a productive organization whose regulator enables energy access or prevents its loss. Its recurrence depends on renewing the controller and the work it coordinates. A repeated control signal alone does not establish that renewal. Self-modeling adds the possibility of representing the system's own limits as its activity changes the conditions of further development.

Cybernetics supplies a language for the first question. Norbert Wiener's 1948 book brought control and communication in organisms and machines into a common inquiry. W. Ross Ashby developed an account of regulation in terms of the disturbances a system encounters and the responses available to it. The central relation is a circuit: an action changes a condition, information about that condition becomes available, and subsequent action changes accordingly.[1]

The Jacquard cards give us an unusually clear case of what must be added to get feedback. With the card sequence fixed, a broken warp thread can spoil the face while the mechanism keeps issuing the correct selections. The sequence has no input for a streak on Jacquard’s cheek. A weaver who sees it, stops, mends the thread and inspects the next crossing closes a circuit through the cloth. At one boundary there is a sequence of commands. At the wider boundary the consequence of a command can change what happens next. Paying and training a successor keeps that capacity for correction alive after the weaver leaves. That last step connects cybernetics to autopoiesis.

A familiar thermostat makes the relation visible. Its operation depends on a measured temperature and a setting. Heating changes the temperature; the changed measurement affects whether heating continues. The circuit helps keep one variable within a range despite some disturbances. It also has limits. A thermostat connected to an inadequate heater cannot overcome any possible cold. A faulty sensor can sustain the wrong response. Regulation requires distinctions and effective actions, not merely the presence of a loop.

Ashby's requisite-variety argument makes the limitation sharper. When disturbances require different responses, a regulator must be able to distinguish and counter enough of them to hold the relevant outcome within its permitted range. Protection can also reduce what reaches the regulator: insulation makes temperature control easier by changing the disturbance before the heater must respond.[2] A system can improve regulation by adding responses, improving information, or reorganizing the environment.

The person carrying the cup uses all three routes. They can change their grip, notice a tremor, or put the cup down before fatigue becomes a fall. A useful self-model matters because it makes a difference in the actor available to regulation before the object is lost. That gives the chapter a bounded prediction: access to the relevant self-related information should improve action under disturbances that require it. More elaborate self-description without a corresponding improvement would not supply the same evidence.

Even a perfectly functioning thermostat leaves a second question unanswered. Who supplies the electricity, replaces the failed component, repairs the heater, and maintains the building? A diagram can close the information circuit while leaving the renewal of its physical parts outside the account.

Autopoiesis addresses that missing relationship. Varela, Maturana, and Uribe developed the concept for a network of processes that produces the components and organization through which the network itself continues. The biological claim concerns continuing self-production, including the constitution of a boundary. Its closure is organizational: the processes help produce the conditions for one another's recurrence. Matter and energy still cross the boundary.[3]

Cybernetics and autopoiesis therefore ask different questions about processes that can be deeply intertwined. Regulation concerns how activity responds to disturbances and differences. Autopoiesis concerns how an organization produces the means of remaining that organization. In a living system, regulated processes contribute to self-production, while self-production continually renews the means of regulation.

Consider a cell at the level of a schematic example. Transport through its boundary affects the materials available to internal reactions. Those reactions help produce and replace components involved in transport and in maintaining the boundary. Changes in the internal conditions can alter the activity of these processes. The boundary, production network, and regulation participate in one continuing organization. None needs to receive instructions from a miniature supervisor outside the cell.

Draw the dependencies in both directions. Regulation helps keep production within conditions in which it can continue. Production renews the structures through which conditions are detected and altered. A failure in the first direction can let a disturbance disable production. A failure in the second can leave an initially successful regulator without the components needed to keep regulating. Each may temporarily conceal the other's failure because reserves and replacement intervals delay the consequences.

This is why stability alone cannot establish autopoiesis. A refrigerator may maintain an internal temperature for years while relying on an outside industrial organization for its manufacture and repair. Nor does self-production imply that every variable is held constant. Growth, adaptation, and recovery require changes. What continues is an organization of dependencies through changing components and activities. Its identity cannot be reduced to the unchanging reading of a gauge.

The distinction also clarifies what a goal means in different settings. A thermostat's setting is ordinarily supplied by a designer or operator. A living system's continued existence depends on conditions that cannot be set arbitrarily: some arrangements sustain its processes and others destroy them. That gives regulation a relation to viability without requiring the cell to formulate a conscious intention. A human purpose, with its social history and possible conflicts, demands a more elaborate account. We cannot derive every human aspiration from a requirement to maintain one physiological variable.

Return to the cup. The movements depend on a body whose capacities are being sustained through food, rest, repair, and care. That body's actions also contribute to obtaining food, maintaining relationships, and caring for others. The capacity to regulate a movement sits within processes that renew the mover. At the social scale examined in chapter 9, provisioning and organized work sustain the people who detect shortages, change plans, and perform the next round of provisioning.

The Marx–Luhmann synthesis gives this reciprocal dependence a social form. A shortage becomes consequential for regulation through operations such as reporting, accounting, negotiation, and decision. Those operations distinguish what counts as a shortage, whose claim is recognized, and which response is authorized. Their effects depend on people, equipment, energy, and enforceable relationships capable of carrying out the response. Successful provisioning can then renew the participants and institutions performing those operations. This is how formal distinctions can acquire material consequences while depending on material activity for their own continuation.

The social extension needs its own evidence. A company is not a cell, and a payment system is not a membrane. The useful question is which operations reproduce which conditions. A purchasing department can respond accurately to stock levels while the institution becomes unable to pay its suppliers. Local regulation may remain effective while a larger regenerative dependency fails. Conversely, financial reserves may preserve an institution through a period of poor operational control. A theory that calls every surviving feedback loop autopoietic loses these distinctions.

For the book's central argument, the intertwined relationship matters because a new pathway must do more than appear once. An assembly can open a possibility; regulation can help it operate under disturbance; regenerative organization can renew the means of its operation. None of these achievements automatically supplies the other two. An ingenious apparatus may be too unstable to use. A reliable apparatus may be impossible to replace. An enduring institution may reproduce equipment that prevents a better arrangement from becoming accessible.

The question from chapter 1 returns in a different form. Can a constraint help create a capability? A regulatory circuit narrows what happens: some deviations elicit corrections, some actions are inhibited, some conditions are preserved. That narrowing can make a longer undertaking feasible. Carrying the cup becomes possible because the body does not explore every movement available to its joints. Yet a restriction that prevents a needed adjustment makes the task harder. The value of the constraint depends on what it allows the organized system to accomplish.

Complex action requires relationships among regulations. Keeping the cup level contributes to carrying it through the doorway, which contributes to bringing someone a drink. The undertakings operate over different spans of time and admit different kinds of correction. If the doorway is blocked, adjusting the wrist more precisely will not solve the problem. The route must change. A useful organization must permit information about failure to alter the level at which the response is selected.

This suggests hierarchy, but the brain should not be pictured as a single chain of command terminating in an all-knowing executive. Markov and colleagues' research on macaque visual cortex describes hierarchical relationships involving feedforward and feedback pathways. That is evidence about specified cortical connections, not proof that every mental activity occupies one rung of a universal ladder.[4] The cup scene asks how coordination works; it does not locate each undertaking in a corresponding anatomical box.

Four ideas need to remain distinct. Hierarchy concerns relationships among levels. Recurrence means that influence returns through a circuit. A recursive procedure applies an operation within a further application of that operation. Self-modeling represents aspects of the system doing the modeling. A multilayer network has a hierarchy without necessarily possessing recurrent connections. A thermostat contains feedback without describing its own competence. A procedure can be recursive without representing anyone's self.

The more interesting possibility arises when these arrangements combine. A system can monitor how well a process is working and change the conditions under which that process operates. It can then assess the adequacy of its monitoring. Such an arrangement need not extend indefinitely. It needs enough relevant distinctions to make a useful correction before the costs of further assessment exceed its value.

Recognizing fatigue illustrates the point. Instead of waiting for the cup to fall, the person predicts that their usual manner of carrying it has become unreliable. The prediction changes the procedure. A model of a limitation has altered the practical constraint imposed by that limitation. It has neither eliminated fatigue nor created energy. It has made another course of action available with the resources at hand.

The book's proposal is that a self emerges through an elaboration of this relationship: the mind's model of its world comes to include the body, capacities, attention, memory, and activity of the modeler. The representation can preserve continuity across undertakings and make the system's own conduct available for interpretation. A person can ask what they know, what they are trying to do, and whether they are likely to succeed.

That proposal does not require a complete internal duplicate. A map can help someone navigate without containing every stone along the route. A model of attention could help regulate attention without reproducing every event that implements it. Nor does it require a little spectator reading the map. The explanatory task is to identify how represented distinctions alter subsequent processes. Installing another observer merely repeats the problem.

Attention schema theory supplies a particular version of this approach. Michael Graziano proposes that a simplified representation of attention contributes to its control and to attributions of awareness. The broader theory is a hypothesis about consciousness. Wilterson and Graziano's artificial-agent study tests a narrower functional relationship: access to a descriptive model of attention improved performance in their task, and disabling that access impaired it.[5] The result gives a specified self-related representation a causal role. It does not demonstrate a complete human self or establish that the agent experiences anything.

This separation strengthens the investigation. We can ask whether a representation improves regulation without first claiming to have solved subjective experience. We can also distinguish a useful self-model from an accurate autobiography. A system might represent a limitation well enough to avoid a particular error while holding mistaken descriptions of other aspects of itself. A fluent explanation of one's conduct is evidence to examine, not an automatic window into the process that produced it.

The self also acquires descriptions from other people. Someone is taught to call an experience distraction, exhaustion, uncertainty, or confidence. Those inherited distinctions can change how they interpret and regulate their activity. A teacher's correction may become a question the learner later asks without the teacher being present. Chapter 8's collective inheritance enters the organization of an individual undertaking. This does not make society one conscious subject. It identifies routes through which a person acquires instruments for describing and changing themselves.

Here the joint material and Platonic thesis has a concrete application. A relation represented in a model can guide a physical action, but the representation exists through a material organization with costs and limits. Its formal content matters: representing the wrong limitation can redirect effort badly. Its embodiment matters: a useful distinction has no operational effect if the system cannot retain or use it. Learning changes which relations can participate in action.

Artificial intelligence inherited competing accounts of how to construct that capacity. Cybernetics placed regulation, communication, and the organization of behavior within a common inquiry. A different emphasis put explicit symbolic representation and logical deduction at the center of intelligence. In his recollections, John McCarthy described his preference for logical approaches over Wiener's emphasis on feedback.[6] This records a consequential methodological commitment. It does not establish that the preferred method was sufficient.

The criticism matters. The broad ambition of producing general intelligence principally by supplying explicit symbolic structures and rules was not fulfilled by that program. Major later advances depended on systems acquiring useful internal organization through training. The change was more than a faster execution of a complete rulebook already written by a programmer. It altered where the detailed organization responsible for performance came from.

The neural history was not a clean break from everything that preceded the AI label. Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville trace successive periods of cybernetics, connectionism, and deep learning in the development of neural-network research.[7] These periods involved different methods and resources. Contemporary learning is not simply Wiener's program vindicated in every detail. Nevertheless, the history frustrates the idea that the name of a research field permanently identifies the method through which its ambitions will be realized.

AlexNet provides a bounded example. Its 2012 image-classification result combined a trained convolutional network with an implementation making effective use of GPUs.[8] The important relation for this book is between a learning procedure, its material execution, and a capability acquired through that execution. The result did not establish that all intelligence reduces to image classification. It demonstrated a productive route to a specified capability without an exhaustive hand-written account of how to recognize every image.

The first steam engine helps interpret the cost. Existing productive activity must sometimes carry an expensive intermediate before that intermediate can make a useful contribution. Training consumes resources before deployment can supply a return. Better methods may reduce that cost; committing more resources may make a previously unaffordable process possible. Those changes are distinct, and neither guarantees success. The failed runs belong in the account alongside the celebrated result.

The reversal also has limits. Learned representations did not make logical consistency or explicit verification irrelevant. AlphaProof illustrates a combination in which reinforcement learning supports search for proofs in a formal environment, with formal checking retaining a definite role.[9] Learning can change how candidates are found while a specified logic determines whether a candidate is a proof. The acquisition of a search strategy and the standard of correctness are different parts of one assembly.

This is a more informative dialectical development than a simple succession of winners. A previous organization supplies techniques and achievements that a successor can retain while changing their role. The ambition to construct intelligence survives a revision in the account of how it is constructed. Formal relations remain indispensable, but they need not all be installed as explicit rules for selecting every action.

What, then, would show that this account explains anything? For self-modeling, vary access to a specified representation while controlling task and resources. Does regulation change in the predicted way? Could an equally capable controller succeed without that representation? For the connection to autopoiesis, identify what renews the regulator and what its regulation helps renew. A proposed model or controlled artificial system could test interruptions, reserves, and substitutes. Calling every successful controller self-maintaining would evade the test.

Historical operating records may also identify these dependencies when an interruption or change in access creates a credible comparison. They must distinguish failure of regulation from the deterioration that caused both poor regulation and failed renewal. A natural experiment and a designed intervention can answer the same kind of causal question when their respective assumptions hold. The afterword makes that standard explicit; neither a feedback diagram nor a fluent self-description supplies the comparison by itself.

The energetic preference requires following those corrections into the work they support. A regulator may consume energy while preventing the loss of a much larger productive pathway. The higher assembly is the regulated organization, including the means that renew its controller. The hypothesis predicts greater sustained usable-energy access where these gains support maintenance and further construction. Reduced control error or a lower learning loss is evidence about one link, not a measurement of that entire return.

The cup arrives. The achievement is modest, but its conditions are extensive: a supported body, coordinated regulations, inherited knowledge, and perhaps a judgment about the carrier's own capacity. Cybernetic activity helps sustain a living undertaking. Living and social self-production renew the conditions under which that activity can occur. The mind's ability to model some of these conditions can change what becomes possible next. The same distinction runs from a corrected thread to a workshop able to teach its next weaver. To explain the latter we have to follow the person who mends the thread through the end of the shift and into the next generation of workers.

The next chapter asks how the same general history assembles artificial cognition. It also asks what the resulting machines cannot do merely by having been assembled. A capacity to generate answers does not by itself supply a reason to begin, an unlimited means of continuing, or an organization capable of producing its own tomorrow.
