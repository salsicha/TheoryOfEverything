# Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

**Part IV · Cybernetics**

A glass of water appears twice in a paper published in 1943. The first time, you pick it up to take a drink. The authors invite you to consider how little you had to decide. You wanted the water. You left the angle of your elbow to take care of itself. A page later the authors give the glass to a patient with cerebellar disease. His hand is quiet at rest. As the glass approaches his mouth, the hand swings wide, corrects, and swings wider in the other direction. The water spills. The man knew just as well as you did where he wanted the glass to go.[1]

The physiologist Arturo Rosenblueth, the mathematician Norbert Wiener and the engineer Julian Bigelow saw in that second glass a problem familiar to anyone who has tried to steady a machine. Each correction can give the next one a larger error to correct. Five years later Wiener published *Cybernetics*, a book about what engineers and physiologists could learn from each other by attending to such things. It is a curious origin for a science of purposeful behavior. One of its founding examples is a drink somebody never gets.[1]

There is a third glass worth considering. Imagine carrying a cup to someone in another room late in the day. Halfway across the floor you see the water lap against the rim and steady your hand. On a different evening, having felt your grip go slack once already, you put the cup down and let someone else carry it. Both times the drink arrives. What does the work the second time is something you know about the person carrying it.

That is the missing testimony in the Lyon apprentice's case. Bron's mother could see how sleepy he got at work. Condamine could tell the tribunal what it cost him to have a boy he could hardly keep awake. We have no account from Bron of how much warning he got before his attention went, or what would have happened if he had asked to stop. Knowing when to put the cup down is a considerable human achievement. So is having the liberty to do it.[2]

## What the cards cannot see

Give Carquillat's loom the cards for Jacquard's face. Suppose a warp thread breaks as the cheek is being woven. The next card arrives on time and makes precisely the selection the pattern requires. Now a pale streak has appeared where it has no business being. Where on the cards could the streak register a complaint?[3]

A weaver can give it a hearing. She stops the loom, finds the break, mends it and watches the next few crossings to see whether the mend takes. The cards still prescribe the same portrait. But the cloth already woven has acquired a say in what the workshop does next. The weaver's action changes the cloth, the changed cloth changes what she sees, and what she sees governs her next action. A cybernetician would call that return through the cloth *feedback*. Draw a boundary around the cards and the part of the mechanism that reads them, and you can follow a succession of commands. Include the weaver and you can follow a mistake all the way back to the place where another mistake can be prevented.

W. Ross Ashby gave a precise account of a difficulty the weaver now faces. If a snag and a broken thread call for different remedies, she needs some way to tell them apart and some means of making both repairs. A bell that rings for either can be enough if all she has to do is stop. It will tell her very little about how to start again. The quantity of information she needs depends on how much she wants to keep intact. A foreman can count every yard that comes off a loom and miss altogether the first streak to cross a face.[4]

Some of the work of control can be done before anything goes wrong. Clear a path before carrying a tray and there are fewer collisions to avoid. Fit the room with a thermostat and its switch can govern the heater while everyone attends to something else. On a night cold enough to exceed the capacity of the heater, the thermostat can make a flawless series of measurements while the room grows colder. More insulation would have given the same switch authority over a colder night. The formal relation that matters is between the disturbances that get through and the responses still available against them.[4]

Who replaces the switch when its contacts wear out? A wiring diagram can account for every signal that regulates the temperature and never answer that question. At the loom, eyes tire, people leave the trade, and someone must learn which of two very similar streaks calls for an entirely different repair. A workshop might have an excellent year by keeping its best weaver at the loom through every hour in which she might have taught a successor. An order book full of finished portraits could be the last record of a skill.

Here cybernetics meets *autopoiesis*, the term Francisco Varela, Humberto Maturana and Ricardo Uribe developed for the organization of a living thing. Imagine a cell whose membrane admits material that its internal reactions use to make fresh membrane. Damage to the boundary can put those reactions in jeopardy. Regulating what comes through helps keep them going. In return, their products replace parts of the boundary through which the regulation works. Matter and energy cross it continually. What closes is a chain of dependencies we can follow in both directions. Interrupt the repair while leaving the regulator working. For a while a measurement taken inside the cell might look perfectly ordinary. The bill comes due as parts wear out.[5]

At a loom, the return takes a different route. A weaver's judgment saves silk. Selling the cloth can buy food for the weaver and time for the apprentice to stand beside her. Whether any money gets back to either of them is a question for the accounts and for the people who have claims on them. Marx followed the work required to bring a worker back the next day. Luhmann gives us a way to see how the tribunal's decision about who owes whom can direct that work. An award of sixty francs can buy a shop some time. It cannot award a boy a night's sleep. Regulation and self-production meet where a decision actually renews a capacity that later decisions will need.[2][5]

## Where am I looking?

Suppose the person receiving the drink is on the other side of a closed door. You can hold a cup exceptionally level while standing outside the room indefinitely. Set it down and you can work the handle. For a moment, relaxing one demand on your hand serves the purpose for which you were making that demand. The useful hierarchy here has to do with which correction you can make. Wrist, route and undertaking offer different places to intervene, and news can travel in either direction. A spill can send you all the way back to the tap.

There are actual hierarchies in the brain. Nikola Markov and his colleagues used anatomical tracers to follow connections among areas of the visual cortex in macaques. They could distinguish pathways that ran up the hierarchy from pathways that ran back down, in part by the layers in which the projecting cells lay. In such a recurrent network, a region receiving visual information can in turn alter activity in a region that helps supply it. The discovery gives us a way to picture looking at cloth without imagining signals carried upstairs until somebody at the top looks at them.[6]

It takes another step to get from a circuit that comes back around to a mind that can take itself into account. Read two lines at the bottom of a page. Turn the page and discover you have no recollection of either line. You knew enough about the marks to move your eyes along them and enough about books to turn the page at the right time. Then something you usually expect from your own reading failed to appear. You go back. This time you ask yourself, halfway across the first line, whether you could tell someone what it means. You have begun to check how well your own checking works. That is a little piece of recursion you can do with a finger holding your place.

What would a machine gain from even a crude piece of knowledge about itself? Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano made the question small enough to fit in a game. A ball falls across a square field toward a paddle. Most of the field is full of visual noise. The player, an artificial neural network, can move a small clear patch over the field and can move the paddle to make a catch. Then the researchers give it a second square. All that square shows is the location of the clear patch on the first one. You could look at the second square all day and never learn where the ball was. You would know where you had been looking.[7]

The researchers let the player learn the game with both squares. When they took away the information in the second one, the clear patch on the first kept working. The player had as good a view through it as before. Yet it had more trouble keeping the patch on the ball and making the catch. The missing fact was about the operation of its own sight. That is a modest and quite exact thing to have shown. We can tell what the player could do with that fact by watching the paddle. Whether it felt anything when it missed is beyond the reach of the score.[7]

I think a self begins to take shape when a mind's account of what is happening comes to include, with some continuity, the one to whom it is happening. The useful content can be quite spare. *I lose the thread when I am tired. This hand is occupied. I have read this passage, but I could not tell you what it says.* Any one of those judgments can redirect a body before the world administers a more expensive correction. An account of the mind that requires another little reader inside to understand these judgments leaves us with two readers to explain. Follow a judgment far enough to see the hand set down the cup, and there is already something for an explanation to get hold of.

Other people help furnish that inner account. A good teacher can lend a student a question that the student will eventually ask alone. At the loom it might be as simple as *When did you last look at the cloth?* Two workers facing the same pattern may have very different capacities to notice their attention going and very different permissions to act on what they notice. One can change the angle of the lamp, ask a friend to take over, go home. A boy whose wages depend on staying may learn exactly the same lesson about his own limits and have to put it to a different use. The self that grows up around those choices has a social history as well as a nervous system.

## A name for the summer

In 1955 John McCarthy and three colleagues put a new name on a request for money. They wanted a summer at Dartmouth in which to work on *artificial intelligence*. Years later McCarthy remembered avoiding Wiener at MIT. He had read *Cybernetics* and expected the two of them would disagree. Wiener, as McCarthy understood him, wanted to know how far you were from a goal and how the difference guided your next move. McCarthy wanted a machine to reason from what it knew to what it ought to do. Both questions can arise at a closed door. How far is your hand from the handle? And given that both hands are full, what should you do about it?[8]

Wiener himself cared deeply about who got to choose the goal. In a letter published in 1947 he declined an aircraft researcher's request for a wartime paper. The man had told him what the project was. In his reply Wiener asked what responsibility a scientist bore for the uses of his work after Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The Dartmouth organizers in turn left us their proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation. It included a question about how a network of neurons might form concepts. As McCarthy recalled it, the foundation supplied about half the money requested and participants came and went according to their own interests. It is easy to imagine why a sponsor uneasy with Wiener's public stand might welcome a different name for the research. To show that money changed hands for that reason we would need the sponsor's side of the story.[8][9]

McCarthy was quite capable of telling a good story against himself. In an oral history he described starting a chess program. He could write the part that specified the legal moves. He got stuck on the strategy and handed the program on to students. It is a lovely distinction to get stuck on. A machine can know every move permitted on the board and still have no idea which is worth making.[8]

My disagreement with McCarthy concerns the weight he placed on explicit reasoning. It is possible to state rules from which a machine can draw deductions. Where is it to get a description of the world good enough to reason from? Try giving someone who has never seen Carquillat's portrait rules for recognizing the window behind the man's chair. You might begin with straight lines and rectangles. Now deal with the folds in the curtain that partly conceal them. A child who has seen windows through curtains may find this an easy picture to read and an almost impossible set of rules to dictate.

In a 2012 paper Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton published a photograph of a mite sitting off center in the frame. Their network recognized it. Look at the evidence beside that little success. They had trained a deep network on roughly 1.2 million labeled images. Its structure let a feature learned in one part of an image be looked for elsewhere. Much of the detailed organization that made a mite recognizable arose through training on two graphics processors. Somebody supplied the pictures and the names, designed the network and paid for the machines to run long enough for its errors to change it. Nobody had to write the test for *mite near the edge*.[10]

The winning entry the team submitted to that year's ImageNet competition missed the target among its first five guesses on 15.3 percent of images. The runner-up missed on 26.2 percent. People who wanted to build machines that could see had a reason to change what they built next. A branch of research with roots in cybernetics, later known as connectionism and deep learning, had delivered under the name McCarthy helped establish. The logic-first program fell short of its ambition to account for intelligence in general. One of its mistakes was to take the facts from which reasoning could start too cheaply. Years of somebody's life can go into learning to see what in another person's rulebook occupies a single line.[10][11]

Nearly seventy years after the Dartmouth proposal, a proof still had much the same virtue it had for McCarthy. Every step could be checked. Consider a result from the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad. The AlphaProof system found proofs for three of the five non-geometry problems. Its developers used reinforcement learning to guide a search in a formal mathematical language. The rules could settle whether a proposed proof was valid. Learning helped the system choose a move worth trying. Even with the problems translated into the formal language, the computation ran far beyond a contestant's time allowance. McCarthy's chessboard has come around again. This time there was a way to pay for acquiring some strategy, and the exactness of the rules made the acquisition possible.[12]

That expense brings us back to the flooded mine. Someone has to supply the first load of dry coal before an engine can give access to a seam. Someone likewise has to provide the electricity and the examples through the long interval in which a machine learns to detect an error. A training score measures how its answers change during that interval. If the machine is later used to prevent a waste of fuel, the question of how much fuel it saves belongs out at the plant. The stronger claim of this book, that new assemblies tend to open and sustain greater access to energy, must be settled there, where a pump draws power or stands idle and someone has to pay for the next one.

Put a photograph on the desk beside the woven portrait. An image classifier can give a name to what it sees. A weaver can notice that she has gone too long without looking at the cloth. If she puts her hand out to stop the loom, she may save the face that remains to be woven. We have learned to supply the first ability on an extraordinary scale. The second leaves a more elusive record. If the weaver succeeds, no streak in the finished silk will tell you where she stopped. Now leave the photograph beside a trained machine, switch on the current, and give it a night to itself. What would give it a reason to look?
