# Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

**Part IV · Cybernetics**

In Bristol, W. Grey Walter set a mirror in front of his mechanical tortoise. It wheeled about as if dancing with its reflection. A patch of white that reflected enough light could make it dance too.[1]

The tortoise carried a lamp and a photoelectric eye. When the eye picked up the lamp’s reflection, the circuit changed the steering and switched the lamp off. The reflection vanished. The lamp came back on. The wheels kept moving through the cycle. Walter had built a machine that could respond to a consequence of its own movement without having to recognize its own image.[1]

A creature can react to something it has done with no way of telling what part it played. That is the gap an account of intelligence has to cross. My claim is that a new capacity appears when a system can use what it knows about its *own way of finding out* to change its next decision. A reflection and a steerable eye give us a way to ask how much of that capacity even a very simple machine possesses.[1]

## A shaking hand

In a 1943 paper Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow used the example of a patient with a disorder of the cerebellum lifting a glass of water. At rest his hand could be steady. As the glass approached his mouth it would swing to one side. A correction took it too far in the other direction. The nearer the glass came, the wider the swings, until the water spilled.[2]

An engine governor can cut the steam supply when an engine runs too fast. If it cuts too much, the next correction has to go the other way. The patient’s hand illustrates the same difficulty. Information about an error returns to the mechanism that can correct it. That is *feedback*, a central concern of Wiener’s 1948 book *Cybernetics*. Both the size of a correction and the delay before it takes effect determine whether it steadies the movement or makes it worse.[2]

W. Ross Ashby asked how many different disturbances a regulator could handle. The answer depended on how many different responses it could make. Walter gave the tortoise another response by connecting its battery to the circuit that steered it toward or away from a light.[1][3]

## Inside the charging hutch

Walter put a lamp and charging contacts inside a hutch. A well-charged tortoise turned away when it got close to the light. As the battery ran down its response changed. It advanced far enough to touch the contacts and its motors stopped. Once charged, it could leave. The same lamp could turn the animal away or bring it in, depending on the charge in its battery.[1]

The battery circuit makes a difference we can locate. The same light reaches the eye on the way into the hutch and on the way out. Its usefulness depends on a second piece of information, the state of the battery. That simple coupling gives the machine a way to obtain energy when it needs it. The hutch, the charging contacts and the tortoise itself still have to come from Walter’s workshop.[1]

Francisco Varela, Humberto Maturana and Ricardo Uribe built a computer model to ask how a cell could make the boundary that held it together. In their model, a catalyst helped components form and join into a boundary. When parts of the boundary broke down, reactions inside it made replacements. The boundary in turn kept the components close enough to react again. The researchers called an organization that produces its own components in this way *autopoietic*.[4]

Autopoiesis and cybernetics are intertwined here because some of what the boundary regulates goes into making the next stretch of boundary. The difference from Walter’s machine can be stated as a prediction. If a piece of the tortoise’s charging apparatus goes missing, the light-seeking circuit has no means to replace it. In the cell model a break small enough to keep the catalyst inside can call on the same production that formed the boundary in the first place. Producing a regulator, using it to obtain energy and improving the way it regulates are three achievements with different requirements.[1][4]

## What Darwin wrote down

Charles Darwin made a point of writing down an objection to his theory as soon as he met it. He explained why in his autobiography. A fact in his favor would stay with him. An awkward one had a way of slipping his mind.[5]

Sterile worker ants presented a difficulty Darwin thought might undo natural selection. They could have jaws quite unlike their fertile parents’ jaws, yet they had no offspring to inherit an improvement. Frederick Smith offered him driver ants from a single West African nest. Darwin dissected workers of different sizes. John Lubbock drew their jaws with a camera lucida, which let him see the specimen and drawing surface together. Between the biggest and smallest jaws they found a series of intermediate forms.[5]

Darwin argued that fertile parents could leave more descendants when the workers they produced were useful to the colony. Selection could preserve a family’s tendency to produce particular workers, including ones that never bore young.[5]

Darwin examined the jaws. He also examined how he chose which evidence to keep. That second judgment changed the way he made the first. This is what I mean by *recursive* here. The brain has physical means for levels to interact. Nikola Markov’s group traced pathways in the macaque visual cortex running both up and down a hierarchy, with different patterns of connections in each direction. The anatomy identifies routes for information. Darwin’s account identifies a use he made of his own ability to reconsider.[5]

By *intelligence* I mean **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. Darwin needed a judgment about ants and a second judgment about why he might misjudge the evidence about ants. By acting on the second he could improve the first, including on some future occasion when the facts themselves would be quite different. That is the advantage I see in a hierarchy of this kind. Detecting one error can correct an answer. Detecting a habit of error can change how answers are reached.[5]

Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano tested what a simple self-model could do for an artificial player. A ball fell toward a paddle on a square field obscured by visual noise. The player could move the paddle and a clear patch through which it could track the ball. A second display showed only where it was looking.[6]

Once the player had learned the game, the researchers took away the second display. The player could still move the clear patch and see the ball through it. Yet it had more trouble following the ball and catching it. Since the noisy field and the ability to move the clear patch were still available, the loss tells us what the separate record of its own attention contributed. Knowing where it was looking had helped it decide where to look next.[6]

The score measures success at catching a ball. My conjecture about the emergence of a human self concerns what happens when the same ability extends across years. A mind that can represent its own attention, memories and habits can use their history in deciding what to do. Those decisions then become part of the history it can reconsider. A sense of the same person acting over time could form through that recursion. Darwin’s rule gives a reason such an ability might be worth its cost long before anyone could write a theory of it.[5][6]

## The Dartmouth grant

In January 1947 Wiener published a letter declining to send an aircraft researcher a paper from his wartime work. After Hiroshima and Nagasaki he wanted scientists to answer for the uses of their work. The government owned the paper. Wiener told the man where he could ask for it and withheld his own help.[7]

Eight years later John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon asked the Rockefeller Foundation for $13,500 to bring researchers to Dartmouth the following summer. They called their subject *artificial intelligence*. Robert Morison offered $7,500. In his letter of November 30, 1955, he said that mathematical models of thought looked promising but were hard to assess. The foundation had also helped pay for Wiener and Rosenblueth’s research on feedback. It is reasonable to ask whether Wiener’s opposition to military work influenced other sponsors. The surviving letters about this grant show the same foundation supporting both groups.[7][8]

McCarthy later recalled avoiding Wiener at MIT because he expected them to disagree. Wiener wanted to know how the distance a hand had left to travel could guide its movement. McCarthy wanted to know what a machine could deduce from facts. Even so, the Dartmouth application included both logical deduction and networks of neurons that might learn concepts. The new name gave researchers with quite different ideas a place to meet.[8][9]

McCarthy could tell a chess program which moves were legal in a position. Choosing the few worth examining proved harder. He passed his unfinished program to students who completed it. The difference matters well beyond chess. A set of rules can tell a machine whether a step is admissible while giving it very little help in choosing where to spend its next moment of work. Learning offers a way to acquire that judgment from earlier failures.[9]

A photograph published by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton shows a mite well off center. A rule that inspected the middle would have missed it. Their network applied the same set of connections to different patches of the picture. A shape learned in one position could be recognized elsewhere. The Toronto group trained the network on about 1.2 million labeled images using two graphics processors. When it guessed wrong, the label supplied a correction. On the published photograph it recognized the mite.[10]

In the 2012 ImageNet challenge, the Toronto team’s combined entry missed the right category among its first five guesses on 15.3 percent of test images. The runner-up missed on 26.2 percent. I think McCarthy was wrong to give explicit reasoning first place in intelligence. Before anyone can reason about a mite, someone has to pick it out in a photograph. The network learned to do that from examples and corrections. The researchers who made this work used the name McCarthy had chosen and an idea Wiener would have recognized.[10]

Formal reasoning can contribute when there is a way to check an answer. Researchers translated five problems from the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad into a formal language for AlphaProof. The students had two sessions of four and a half hours each. AlphaProof solved three of the five problems and took as long as three days on a proof. Learning helped it choose a step to try. A proof checker could establish whether the step was valid.[11]

A system that learns where to spend its effort can create a capacity nobody had to specify move by move. The mite classifier and the proof system give us evidence for that proposition. Calling them intelligent in the everyday sense leaves a question open under the definition I use here. Recognizing a mite or choosing a promising proof step does not establish that a system can model and revise its own way of making that judgment. Who supplies the correction turns out to matter as much as who supplies the computing power.[6][10][11]
