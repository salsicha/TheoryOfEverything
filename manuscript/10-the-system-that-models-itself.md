# Chapter 10 — The System That Models Itself

**Part IV · Cybernetics**

In Bristol, the neurophysiologist W. Grey Walter put a mirror in front of a mechanical tortoise. It began to dance. Walter thought a biologist watching a live animal behave like this might say it recognized itself. He had built the tortoise and could account for every turn.[1]

The tortoise had wheels, a photoelectric eye and a small lamp. When the eye detected the lamp’s reflection, the circuit switched the lamp off. The reflection disappeared, so the lamp came on again. As the light flickered, the wheels kept changing direction. Walter could get much the same response with a white surface. A machine that recognized its own reflection would have to do something his tortoise couldn’t.[1]

Charles Darwin left a record of a capacity that is harder to explain. He learned something about the way his memory worked and used it to change how he did science. To see what Darwin could do that Walter’s tortoise couldn’t, it helps to start with the movement of a hand.[2]

## A shaking hand

In 1943 Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow described a person lifting a glass to drink. You know where you want the glass to go. You can start to lift it long before you have worked out every movement of your elbow. As the glass travels, you can see how far it still has to go and adjust your hand.[3]

The authors then described a patient with a disorder of the cerebellum. At rest his hand was steady. As the glass approached his mouth, his hand swung to one side. He corrected too far and the glass swung farther to the other. The water spilled. He knew exactly where he wanted the glass to go. Each attempt to correct his aim made the next movement less accurate.[3]

In 1948 Wiener brought engineers and physiologists together in a book called *Cybernetics*. A mechanic trying to steady the speed of an engine and a doctor studying a shaking hand had a problem in common. In both cases the result of one movement helped determine the next. Wiener called this return of information *feedback*. The spilling glass showed what could go wrong when a correction was too large.[3]

In the weaving films from Nabenchauk the route went through different people a generation apart. Katal Pavlu’s mother saw where her daughter needed help and put her hands to the loom. When Katal’s daughter Loxa needed her older sister Xunka’, the request came from Loxa. Some of what the younger weaver had acquired was the judgment to decide when to call. A pattern could settle which threads to lift. It took watching the two generations at work to discover who could act on a difficulty with the pattern.[4]

W. Ross Ashby asked how many different disturbances a regulator could handle. The answer depended on how many different responses it could make. Walter gave the tortoise another response by connecting its battery to the circuit that steered it toward or away from a light.[1][5]

## Inside the charging hutch

Walter put a lamp and a pair of charging contacts inside a hutch. When the battery was well charged, the light at the entrance was bright enough to turn the tortoise away. As the battery ran down, its response to the same light changed. Now it rolled up to the contacts. The motors stopped while the battery charged, then the tortoise could drive out. One of Walter’s hutches is in the Science Museum.[1]

The tortoise could find its own power supply. Walter had built the hutch and connected it to the current. What would it take for a living thing to make some of the equipment it needed for itself?

Francisco Varela, Humberto Maturana and Ricardo Uribe built a computer model of a cell to investigate this question. In their model, a catalyst helped components form and join into a boundary. When parts of the boundary broke down, reactions inside it made replacements. The boundary in turn kept the components close enough to react again. The researchers called an organization that produces its own components in this way *autopoietic*.[6]

Autopoiesis and cybernetics are intertwined in a cell. A boundary regulates what can pass through it. Reactions that depend on that regulation also produce the boundary. This helps explain how later cells can inherit an organization that works. The origin of that organization requires a further explanation of how its components first came together and tapped a source of energy.[6]

In Lyon the apprentice Bron needed a ruling on who controlled his time. The Lyon labor council had the authority to dissolve an apprenticeship. Its ruling could settle an obligation and change who controlled the boy’s time. That power alone could never have taught him to weave. Marx’s question about how a skilled workforce comes into existence meets Luhmann’s about what an authorized communication can set in motion. A society gains new capacities when there are people with the training to act and arrangements that let their judgment alter what happens.[4][7]

## What Darwin wrote down

While writing about the making of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin described a rule he had followed for years. Whenever he came across a fact or an idea that went against his general conclusions, he wrote it down at once. He had noticed that contrary evidence slipped from his memory much sooner than evidence that pleased him. If he relied on what he remembered, a theory would look better with time even if he had discovered nothing new in its favor.[2]

There were two levels of judgment here. Darwin could assess an observation. He could also assess his own reliability as an observer. Brains have a physical hierarchy too. Nikola Markov and colleagues traced pathways between areas of the macaque visual cortex and found distinct patterns of connections running up and back down. Darwin’s notebook gives us a different kind of evidence. We can see what one person did after he realized how his own judgment worked.[2]

Darwin used the same capacity for spotting a pattern to detect a pattern in his own mistakes. That is what *recursive* means here. He could examine and change the way he examined the world.[2]

By *intelligence* I mean **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. Darwin’s knowledge that he forgot objections was a simple model of himself. It allowed him to make a practical change. The next time he sat down to consider his theory, he could read the strongest objections he had found.[2]

Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano tested what a simple self-model could do for an artificial player. A ball fell toward a paddle on a square field obscured by visual noise. The player could move the paddle and a clear patch through which it could track the ball. A second display showed only where it was looking.[8]

Once the player had learned the game, the researchers took away the second display. The player could still move the clear patch and see the ball through it. Yet it had more trouble following the ball and catching it. The score showed that information about where it was looking had helped it decide where to look next.[8]

My conjecture is that a human self takes shape as the mind learns to model its own activity across time. Walter’s tortoise reacted to light from its own lamp. The paddle player used a record of its own attention. Darwin could recognize the same mistake across years of work, decide how to prevent it and tell us why he had changed his practice.[1][2][8]

## The Dartmouth grant

In January 1947 Wiener published a letter declining to send an aircraft researcher a paper from his wartime work. He knew what project the man had in mind. After Hiroshima and Nagasaki he wanted scientists to take responsibility for what became of the knowledge they handed over. The government owned the paper, as Wiener pointed out, and the man was free to ask for it there. What Wiener could withhold was his own participation.[9]

Eight years later John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon asked the Rockefeller Foundation for $13,500 to bring researchers to Dartmouth the following summer. They called their subject *artificial intelligence*. Robert Morison offered $7,500. In his letter of November 30, 1955, he said that mathematical models of thought looked promising but were hard to assess. The foundation had also helped pay for Wiener and Rosenblueth’s research on feedback. It is reasonable to ask whether Wiener’s opposition to military work influenced other sponsors. The surviving letters about this grant show the same foundation supporting both groups.[9][10]

McCarthy later recalled avoiding Wiener at MIT because he expected them to disagree. Wiener wanted to know how the distance a hand had left to travel could guide its movement. McCarthy wanted to know what a machine could deduce from facts. Even so, the Dartmouth application included both logical deduction and networks of neurons that might learn concepts. The new name gave researchers with quite different ideas a place to meet.[10][11]

When McCarthy set out to write a chess program, he could tell it which moves were legal. He had much more trouble getting it to choose a good one. He gave the program to students, who finished it. Every chess player faces that gap. Knowing the rules still leaves you with most of the work of choosing your next move.[11]

One of the images Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton published in 2012 shows a mite well away from the center of the frame. Their network identified it. Nobody had supplied a rule specifying where in a photograph a mite belonged. People had labeled roughly 1.2 million training images. Correct and incorrect classifications changed the strengths of connections in a network whose architecture allowed a feature useful at one spot to be useful at another. Two graphics processors did the training. The new capacity lay in the combination. The labels told the machine whether its guesses had been any good. The architecture determined which lessons it could carry from one photograph to the next.[12]

In the 2012 ImageNet challenge, the Toronto team’s combined entry missed the right category among its first five guesses on 15.3 percent of test images. The runner-up missed on 26.2 percent. I think McCarthy was wrong to give explicit reasoning first place in intelligence. Before anyone can reason about a mite, someone has to pick it out in a photograph. The network learned to do that from examples and corrections. The researchers who made this work used the name McCarthy had chosen and an idea Wiener would have recognized.[12]

Formal reasoning can contribute when there is a way to check an answer. At the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, people translated five non-geometry problems into a formal language for AlphaProof. Given considerably more computing time than a contestant had at a desk, the system solved three. Learning helped it choose a step to try. A proof checker could establish whether the step was valid.[13]

The Toronto group needed labeled photographs, a design that could recognize the same feature at different positions, and processors that could run the calculation enough times. Greater computing power alone would never have told the machine which animal was in the picture. The examples and the design narrowed the work to changes that could improve its answer.[12]

The researchers still chose the photograph and started the classifier. Who gets credit for what happens once a machine starts? Ada Lovelace had asked the question before anyone had built a machine that could follow her instructions.
