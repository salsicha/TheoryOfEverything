# Chapter 15 — Two Papers in June

*Part IV · Paradox*

On June 5, 1905, Henri Poincaré presented a short note to the French Academy of Sciences. On June 30 the editors of *Annalen der Physik* received a paper by Albert Einstein. Poincaré wanted to know what could be said about the motion of an electron. Einstein began by moving a magnet past a wire. Before either paper was finished with the question, a reader who wanted to know what time it was in a moving system had to say where the clock was.[1]

I argue that special relativity was discovered as soon as it possibly could have been in the history of physics. Consider what makes the timing remarkable. Physicists had worried about the motion of light for decades. Within one month, two men working from different starting questions arrived at a common relation between a clock’s time and its position. A dirty clock could always have run slow. Why should the position of a perfectly built, perfectly set clock enter the rule at all? To explain the near-simultaneous answers, we need dates for what made the question answerable before either paper arrived.[1]

## Turning the stone

In Cleveland in 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley had a problem with a slab of stone. They had put mirrors and a device for dividing a beam of light on the slab and floated it on mercury so that they could turn it. The two halves of the beam took perpendicular routes, then rejoined to make bright and dark stripes. If Earth moved through a stationary medium that carried light waves, the exchange of the beams’ directions ought to shift the stripes by an amount they could see. They marked sixteen positions at which to read the instrument. Stopping it at a mark set up strains. By the time the stripe held still, temperature drift could carry it elsewhere. They learned to read it as the stone turned. A revolution took about six minutes.[2]

The anticipated displacement was about four tenths of the distance between stripes. Michelson and Morley got a small fraction of that. It was 1887. The eighteen years that followed are an objection to any claim of immediate discovery unless we can say what was still missing. The result made a large predicted effect untenable. Several accounts could accommodate the small one. A set of equations for comparing measurements across moving systems, and a way for observers in those systems to set distant clocks, would give physicists two further constraints they could actually use.[1][2]

In 1900 Poincaré had already described two observers exchanging light signals to set their watches. Four years later, Lorentz published transformations relating electrical and optical quantities in moving systems over a range of speeds, and Poincaré returned to the clocks in his St. Louis lecture. A physicist in 1904 could find both pieces in print. In his June 1905 note he required the equations to give the same conversion whether one went straight to a second frame or by way of a third, and to restore the original measurements on coming home. With rotations included, this demand fixed a scale factor at one. Poincaré credited Lorentz with reaching that value by a different argument. A reader can inspect the earlier work and see how little mathematical freedom remained before opening either June paper.[1][2][3]

## Two ways to set a clock

Einstein’s magnet posed another problem. Move it past a conducting loop and a current flows. Move the loop past the magnet in the opposite direction at the same relative speed and the same current flows. Textbook accounts assigned different causes to the two results. Einstein wanted the same laws for both observers and a common speed of light in a vacuum. He had to tell his readers how separated observers could synchronize two clocks. Send a light signal from A to B and reflect it straight back. Set B at reflection to the midpoint of the departure and return times measured at A. Observers travelling with the clocks can use the rule.[1]

Watch the same exchange from a frame in which the two clocks travel along the line between them. On the outward leg the light must catch the forward clock. On the return leg the other clock comes toward it. For the observer who sees both clocks in motion the two travel times differ. The place of the reflection now enters the reckoning of its time. Nobody had to nudge Einstein toward that particular outcome once he had accepted his requirements. He thanked his friend Michele Besso. Poincaré cited Lorentz and the optical work. Their papers do not establish what one man had privately read, or a direct path from Michelson’s eyepiece into Einstein’s thoughts.[1][2]

They did disagree. Poincaré could preserve a distinction between local time, which moving observers obtained with their signals, and a time assigned in the ether’s frame. Einstein let the moving observers’ own clocks suffice. The area where their arguments converged was narrower than a whole theory and wider than a shared turn of phrase. They reached a relation about clocks through different questions. It constrained what observers could report even where the authors differed about what lay behind the reports.[1][3]

Now the claim about speed has a clock of its own. The Cleveland result preceded the two papers by eighteen years. Poincaré’s first clock-setting discussion preceded them by five. Lorentz’s 1904 treatment came roughly a year before the two June papers. The papers themselves came twenty-five days apart. I take this compression as evidence that a long assembly of experimental and mathematical work had opened a short route to the result. Poincaré could start by asking which transformations fit together. Einstein could start by asking what two observers could agree to call simultaneous. Each could move through the few choices still open while the other was doing the same. Their disagreement about the ether tells us exactly which part of the answer history had failed to force.[1][2][3]

Why should such a route have been built? In the account developed here, work on motion, electricity and time drew on the energy and practical demands of industrial society. That work paid for instruments, measurements and the means to put a result into print and use it again. A stone that took two experimenters six minutes to turn in Cleveland could spare a physicist in Paris years spent developing a theory that predicted a large fringe shift. In that sense cognition joined the book’s race toward faster use of energy. A practical question had helped sustain a community that could bring two different approaches to the same discovery in one historical window.[1][2][4]

The dates carry different weights. They establish near-simultaneous written results. They cannot reveal the day either man first understood them, or certify the first day an earlier physicist could have done so. A convincing earlier reconstruction would matter. If someone could identify a date when the requisite measurements, transformations and clock procedure were already available together to a working community, and show a long interval of active work with the common relation still unrecognized, the earliest-possible reading would lose force. Likewise a grant for an optical instrument records who paid for it. Inferring maximization of entropy production across the society that supported it takes a further argument.[1][2][4]

Einstein returned to a related difficulty in 1909. Light spread like a wave and exchanged energy in discrete amounts. His calculation of fluctuations in radiation yielded terms of both kinds. A description that gave up everything wave theory had established was as unsatisfactory as one that gave up the discrete exchanges. The dialectical *negation of the negation* names the move the book finds interesting here. A later description can preserve what each rival got right after abandoning the opposition that once made it necessary to choose. Einstein did not give his argument that name. His radiation paper and his relativity paper addressed different problems. Their kinship lies in the evidence a satisfactory replacement had to keep.[5]

In 1977 the team behind a navigation satellite switched its on-board clock from quartz operation to full-power cesium operation after fifteen days. Tracking measurements from Panama put the clock’s net gain at about thirty-eight microseconds a day against ground time, close to the expected difference from motion and gravity together. People setting up a navigation service had acquired a material reason to get both corrections right. A benefit seventy-two years later cannot pay for an investigation backward in time. It can pay to keep hard-won relations in use, to put them into hardware and to teach them to another generation. Einstein’s distant clock had become a piece of equipment someone had to switch on.[6]
