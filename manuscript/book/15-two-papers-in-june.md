# Chapter 15 — The Constraints Behind Simultaneous Discovery in Special Relativity

*Part IV · Paradox*

One Thursday in May 1905, Albert Einstein offered his friend Conrad Habicht a trade. If Habicht sent him his dissertation, Einstein would send four papers in return. He could describe the fourth, on bodies moving through electric and magnetic fields, although he had only a rough draft. It involved a change in what physicists meant by space and time. Around the same month Henri Poincaré wrote to Hendrik Lorentz. He had missed the chance to talk to Lorentz in Paris and had been working through the Dutch physicist’s equations. In a second letter he corrected his own argument. He could now show, by combining two changes of motion, why a number left open in the calculation had to be one.[1]

On June 5 Poincaré presented his result to the French Academy of Sciences. On June 30 the editors of *Annalen der Physik* received Einstein’s paper. Why twenty-five days, after physicists had spent a generation on the problem? By that spring a physicist could combine the clock readings and the equations already in print and rule out every rival for one number. That would put the common result within reach of both men at once. To see when it first came within reach, start with a time when the evidence still allowed two answers.[1]

## Turning the stone

In Cleveland in 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley had a problem with a slab of stone. They had put mirrors and a device for dividing a beam of light on the slab and floated it on mercury so that they could turn it. The two halves of the beam took perpendicular routes, then rejoined to make bright and dark stripes. If Earth moved through a stationary medium that carried light waves, the exchange of the beams’ directions ought to shift the stripes by an amount they could see. They marked sixteen positions at which to read the instrument. Stopping it at a mark set up strains. By the time the stripe held still, temperature drift could carry it elsewhere. They learned to read it as the stone turned. A revolution took about six minutes.[2]

The expected shift was about four tenths of the distance between stripes. Michelson and Morley saw a small fraction of it. In 1889 George FitzGerald pointed out a way to save the stationary ether. Suppose motion through it slightly altered the lengths of solid bodies. The arm of the instrument pointing along Earth’s motion could shorten by just enough to cancel the delay the observers expected. A physicist who knew the Cleveland result could predict trouble for a rigid instrument moving through the ether. That physicist could still believe in the ether and in a single time common to everyone.[2]

Over the next seventeen years, three things accumulated. Different instruments failed to reveal the expected motion. Among them was a charged condenser that Frederick Trouton and Henry Noble had suspended so it could turn. In 1900 Poincaré worked out how observers travelling together could set distant clocks by exchanging flashes of light. In St. Louis four years later he described what time those clocks would show. And in 1904 Lorentz published equations connecting measurements in a moving system to measurements in one at rest. He had grown dissatisfied with inventing a fresh hypothesis whenever an experiment gave no sign of Earth’s motion. His equations covered a range of speeds. They contained a scale factor, and he argued from his model of the electron that it should equal one. By the end of 1904 a physicist could read about the length, the clocks and the electrical experiments that the next answer had to fit.[1][3]

One choice still mattered. Lorentz and Paul Langevin had proposed different ways a moving electron might change shape. In June Poincaré reported that the available electron measurements could accommodate either. Poincaré imposed a further condition. Take a measurement from one laboratory to a second, then from the second to a third. The answer must agree with a direct conversion from the first to the third. Otherwise the reading assigned to a ruler in the third laboratory would depend on which physicist forwarded the figures. Changing the orientation of the laboratories also had to leave the rule intact. Poincaré showed that these requirements favored Lorentz’s contraction and fixed the scale without having to choose a model of the forces inside an electron.[1]

## Two ways to set a clock

Einstein’s magnet posed another problem. Move it past a conducting loop and a current flows. Move the loop past the magnet in the opposite direction at the same relative speed and the same current flows. Textbook accounts assigned different causes to the two results. Einstein wanted the same laws for both observers and a common speed of light in a vacuum. He had to tell his readers how separated observers could synchronize two clocks. Send a light signal from A to B and reflect it straight back. Set B at reflection to the midpoint of the departure and return times measured at A. Observers travelling with the clocks can use the rule.[1]

Now let an observer on the ground watch the signal pass between two clocks on a moving platform. On the outward leg the light must catch the front clock. On the return leg the rear clock comes toward the light. The traveller sets the clocks by dividing the round trip equally. The ground observer assigns unequal times to its two legs. Both can keep Einstein’s rule for setting clocks and both can measure the same speed of light. What they have to give up is agreement on which events at different places happened at the same time. A clock’s position along the direction of travel enters the equation for its time.[1]

Einstein even ran into Poincaré’s spare number. In his paper it appears as a factor that could stretch or shrink a ruler laid *across* the direction of travel. Follow the ruler from one observer’s description to the other’s and back. Whatever factor the outward conversion introduces, the return conversion must undo. Now reverse the direction of travel. A sideways ruler has no reason to grow when the laboratory moves to the right and shrink when it moves to the left. The two factors have to be equal, and their product has to be one. The only positive value left is one. Einstein got there by turning a ruler sideways. Poincaré got there by requiring successive transformations and rotations to fit together. Lorentz had obtained the value the year before. The June advance was to show why an entire class of measurements required it.[1]

This is where quantity turned into quality. An additional result like the Cleveland one could have ruled out another instrument design while leaving physicists free to repair the theory once again. Requiring *one rule* for every inertial laboratory changed what they could deduce in a single calculation. Each candidate now had to preserve the speed of light, work with the clocks observers could set, and give back the same ruler on the return trip. Once those demands met in the same problem, the family of conversions narrowed to one, for a given relative speed and choice of axes and units. A physicist starting fresh could derive what two distant clocks would read before anyone built them.[1][2][3]

Why should two people reach that threshold together? The work that took years had already been paid for and could be used again. Michelson and Morley had built the apparatus and published the readings. Others could bring a rival explanation to those readings without floating another slab on mercury. Lorentz put a family of equations into circulation. Poincaré’s letters show him actively trying to complete it. Einstein’s letter shows him already at work on the same physical difficulty by another route. A test that had once taken a workshop could now, for the remaining scale factor, be made on a sheet of paper. Parallel efforts have a good chance of converging quickly when a short calculation can eliminate every remaining rival to a particular result.[1][2][4]

That argument predicts exactly where the agreement should end. Poincaré could keep an ether with a true time that the synchronized moving clocks did not show. Einstein took the moving clocks’ readings as time in their own frame. No round trip with the transverse ruler could decide between those interpretations. The May letters put both men to work on the common rule before Poincaré’s presentation and the arrival of Einstein’s manuscript in June. The dates record when others could see the result. If an earlier group had worked for years with the same measurements, clocks and transformations and still missed the conversion, that delay would count against discovery at the first opportunity. The letters also leave a different quantity open. Comparing rates of entropy production would require the energy spent on rival lines of research and the physical work their results set in motion.[1][2][3][4]

Einstein returned to a related difficulty in 1909. Light spread like a wave and exchanged energy in discrete amounts. His calculation of fluctuations in radiation yielded terms of both kinds. A description that gave up everything wave theory had established was as unsatisfactory as one that gave up the discrete exchanges. Both terms had to survive in a single calculation. In dialectical language, that recovery of two results once thought incompatible is a *negation of the negation*. Einstein wrote about radiation in 1909 and about moving clocks in 1905. What the two problems shared was the obligation to keep the measurements that each previous account could explain.[5]

In 1977 the team behind a navigation satellite switched its on-board clock from quartz operation to full-power cesium operation after fifteen days. Tracking measurements from Panama put its net gain against ground time at about thirty-eight microseconds a day, close to the prediction from motion and gravity together. Keeping the same satellite, tracking stations and clock, there were several ways to run a service. Let the discrepancy accumulate and the timing error grows. Measure and correct each clock repeatedly and a ground crew can keep individual readings in line. Build in a rule for the expected drift and the service can go longer between corrections for that effect. Calibration is still needed for other errors.[6]

Engineers could calculate the direction and size of that drift before they turned on the clock. To find out how much energy a navigation system built on that correction brought into use, we would have to follow its customers as well. In June 1905 the test was already within the reach of both physicists. Convert the coordinates of the ends of a ruler into another observer’s frame and back again. Both men had to recover the length the first observer wrote down. They could keep their disagreement about the ether and still have only one number to put into the calculation.[1][6]
