# Chapter 5 — Self-Modeling

*Part II · Humans*

Charles Darwin wrote down unwelcome facts. He explained the practice in his autobiography. An observation that favored his theory could be trusted to stick in his head. One that ran against it had a way of vanishing. The notebook gave the objection a second chance. It also left us evidence that Darwin knew something about the instrument with which he was attempting to understand nature, namely his own attention.[1]

Sterile worker ants gave him a particularly stubborn objection. A worker might have jaws different from those of the fertile parents that produced her. She could leave no daughters to inherit an improvement in those jaws. Frederick Smith supplied driver ants from one West African nest. Darwin dissected workers of different sizes. John Lubbock used a camera lucida to draw what he saw, looking at the specimen and the drawing surface together. Between the smallest and largest workers they found intermediate forms.[1]

Darwin argued that parents could leave more descendants when the workers they produced helped their colony. A trait could be favored through the success of a family even when the individual bearing it never had offspring. Darwin had also caught a weakness in his own investigation. He could expect his memory to lose just the objections that a theory about inheritance had to explain. Writing an objection down while he still remembered it meant he would have to answer it when he next opened the notebook.[1]

## Knowing where one is looking

Intelligence in this sense requires **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. Darwin could observe an ant, then notice which observations he tended to forget. One level kept track of the ants. Another kept track of the observer. When he used what he had learned about his memory to change the way he recorded the next ant, the higher level altered the work of the first.

This definition asks more than selecting a successful response. Walter’s tortoise could approach a charger on a low battery and avoid it on a full one. The use of an internal signal can be demonstrated by disconnecting the battery circuit. To find a self-model, look for a representation of the system’s own activity that can be used to revise activity again. There are degrees of such ability. A model of where one is looking can be useful long before anyone can write an autobiography.[2]

Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano gave an artificial player a paddle, a falling ball and a noisy field of view. A small area of the field could be made clear. The player could move this window to see the ball, then move its paddle to catch it. It also received a separate display of the location of its own attention. After training, the researchers took away the second display. The window still worked. The player could still move the paddle. Its catches suffered.[2]

With or without its attention signal, the trained player faced the same task and could clear the same small patch of the screen. A ball that happened to cross that patch could still be caught. When the patch missed, the intact player had information about where it had already looked. If the whole field were clear on every trial, the advantage of keeping track of the window should shrink. The score measures what that extra information lets the player do. An energetic return requires a task in which a better decision can redirect power.[2]

A brain supplies many opportunities for this kind of return. Nikola Markov’s group traced connections running in both directions between levels of the macaque visual cortex. Information from a lower area can alter a higher estimate, and signals can travel back. The anatomy identifies roads on which a correction could travel. Knowing that two towns share a road does not tell us what message a particular traveler carried. The separate records of the ant observation and Darwin’s rule give a functional example of the sort of traffic we are looking for.[2]

## When the instrument is a person

The return can pass through other people. In Nabenchauk, Mexico, researchers filmed a young weaver learning at a loom. An experienced weaver could put her hands to the threads while the learner continued holding her place. Later learners called for different amounts of help. One question for an instructor is whether a thread has gone wrong. Another is whether the learner is ready to see and correct that error herself. Help given a moment too soon can finish the cloth while depriving the learner of the chance to find out what she missed.[3]

The Jacquard loom offers the other side of the comparison. A pattern embodied in a chain of punched cards can select threads accurately for thousands of passes. In Lyon, J.-L. Moulin prepared a squared design for Carquillat’s woven portrait of Jacquard. He used brown to stand for black silk because he needed to see the marks on the drawing. The loom required the distinctions in the pattern. The draftsman needed to know which distinctions he could still see after he had covered the square with ink. A change of color for his own benefit could preserve the desired color in the silk.[3]

This suggests how a self could emerge from the recursive activity of a mind. Memories of success and failure include information about one persisting source of those events. A person can consult that history, redirect attention and add what happens next to the same history. No little observer has to sit behind the person doing the observing. Recognition of one’s habits can accumulate while the machinery and much of the material that does the recognizing change. Those corrections can be observed in what a person does. What the experience feels like to that person is a further question.[2]

## When turning away from the wind pays

On a wind farm in northwestern India, the first turbine in a row could take so much speed out of the wind that its neighbors downwind had less to work with. Michael Howland and colleagues gave a group of three turbines two ways to work. Each could face into the wind. Or the upstream machines could turn a little aside on the strength of a model of the wakes they themselves made. A machine that gave up some of its own output could steer its slower-moving air clear of the next rotor. The return would show up on another turbine’s meter.[4]

For most of the wind directions they tested, the model placed the best angle for the upstream turbine within five degrees of the measured one. The team then alternated standard and coordinated operation every two and a half hours. In the tested sector, at wind speeds of six to eight meters per second, the coordinated setting delivered about three percent more electricity. If the wind shifted far enough to carry the wake clear of the next rotor, the reason to turn disappeared. The model could say when to leave the machine facing the wind.[4]

The actual change began with a measurement of the incoming wind. The model estimated the effect of a turbine’s own action on the row. A controller used that estimate to set the machines, and subsequent readings showed what the choice had bought. This is one limited ability a mind also needs. Darwin had to represent what his memory did to an awkward fact before he could correct for it. Developing or running a model pays when its correction buys more than it costs over the time it will be used.[1][4]

Of the two settings tried, the coordinated one could supply the higher sustained electrical load in the aligned winds. If the additional work reaches a load and dissipates there at the same temperature, entropy production at that load rises with the watts. Across the whole wind field the slower air behind the turbines would eventually have mixed and dissipated energy anyway. The meter establishes where useful power went. Measuring the wider effect would also require the wakes and what the extra electricity displaced. A better model earns its place in this argument by predicting that difference, including conditions under which to leave the machine facing the wind.[4]

A child learning when to speak of a successful hunt encounters an especially severe test. It is possible to judge the animal correctly, to know that there will be plenty to eat, and still to misunderstand what everybody is saying about it. Richard Lee made that mistake with an ox he had bought himself.
