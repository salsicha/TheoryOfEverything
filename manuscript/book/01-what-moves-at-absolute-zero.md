# Chapter 1 — What Moves at Absolute Zero?

*Part I · Physics*

Olaf Nairz and his colleagues narrowed a slit in the path of a beam of carbon molecules in Vienna. The molecules that made it through landed across a wider strip of the detector. The narrower the opening, the more uncertain the sideways momentum. Making a better-defined starting position had cost the researchers something they could measure at the far end of the apparatus.[1]

Each molecule consisted of seventy carbon atoms. A molecule that large can carry energy in its vibrations and rotations. It is a hard object on which to hang the notion that uncertainty belongs only to tiny, insubstantial things. The team directed many such objects through the apparatus and built up a distribution of arrival positions. A single impact left a single mark. The spread emerged when they examined the marks together.[1]

There are two reasons two marks on a detector might be far apart. A bad joint might let the apparatus shake. That is an engineer’s uncertainty, the sort that can be reduced by finding a loose mounting. There is also the relation between the spreads in position and momentum in the state being prepared. Quantum mechanics puts a lower bound on their product. For a beam aimed along a table, the slit makes the sideways position more definite. The wider range of sideways momenta has room to reveal itself as the molecules travel on. The experiment changed the width of the slit and tested how the distribution responded. Money spent on a firmer table would answer a different question.[1]

## The beam that kept a difference

At Caltech, Amir Safavi-Naeini and his collaborators worked with a patterned beam of silicon. One of its mechanical modes vibrated nearly four billion times a second. Light confined in the structure allowed the team to take energy out of that mode. Another beam of light let them probe it. Cooling by itself presented a problem. A small signal could mean that very little motion remained. It could also mean that the probe had become bad at detecting motion.[2]

Light scattered from the vibrating beam could leave at a slightly higher frequency or a slightly lower one. To leave with more energy, the light had to take a quantum of vibration already present. To leave with less, it could give one up and create a vibration. As the team cooled the mode close to its lowest state, the two signals became unequal. Their difference gave the group a way to measure motion on a scale set by the oscillator itself. They had cooled one mode close to its ground state. They had not brought every part of a laboratory to absolute zero.[2]

In the quantum description of a simple oscillator there is a lowest energy, half a quantum above the bottom of the corresponding classical potential. There is a reason for it. Confine an oscillator more closely to the position of least potential energy and its spread of momentum increases. Allow a broader spread of positions and some lie farther up the potential. The lowest state is the best compromise the relation allows. Physicists call its residual energy *zero-point energy*. A ground-state oscillator cannot keep paying out that energy and returning to the same ground state. There is no lower rung to take it from. The lasers in the experiment had to be paid for.[2]

Cooling takes us to one end of the argument. Once an oscillator occupies its ground state, it can supply no further work in a cycle that restores it to that state. There is still uncertainty about what an individual measurement will find. Is that uncertainty a feature of our description, or can a distinction become definite in the course of physical events? I use *ontological uncertainty* for the second possibility.

Bas Hensen’s group made one version of that difference answerable in Delft. Two diamonds stood 1.3 kilometers apart. The team prepared pairs of entangled electron spins, chose which measurements to make at the two stations, and later brought the records together. Across 245 trials the correlations exceeded the bound for accounts that assign independent local answers in advance under the assumptions tested. Putting the diamonds that far apart made a difference. A light signal carrying the late choice at one station could not reach the other in time to fix its answer. The result admits more than one interpretation of quantum mechanics, including accounts that keep determinism and give up locality.[3]

## How much heat fits through one channel?

Sébastien Jezouin and his colleagues heated the electrons in a small metal plate. They could let the electrons carry heat away through a known number of conducting channels to a colder bath. They kept track of the temperature by listening to electrical noise. How much extra heater power did it take to keep the plate at the same temperature when they opened another channel? Taking the difference let them separate the heat carried by the electrons from heat leaking into the surrounding solid.[4]

Here the fastest route has a value one can work out before turning on the heater. Fix the temperatures of the two ends and allow one electronic channel. A constriction that reflects some of the incoming electrons carries less heat. A channel that transmits them all reaches a ceiling set by the number of quantum states available to carry energy in each direction. Every extra watt crossing that fixed temperature difference adds the same amount to the entropy produced each second. The open channel therefore gives the highest entropy production rate available through that channel. The measured heat flow agreed with the quantum prediction to within the experiment’s roughly ten-percent accuracy.[4]

The distinction that matters for the rest of the book is who opened the channel. Here an investigator set the connection. The measured maximum tells us what a physical constraint permits. To explain a star or a cell we also need a process that builds the connection and keeps it open. At identical temperatures even perfect transmission carries zero net heat. A difference that can keep driving the flow is as essential to the argument as the parts that carry it.[4]

The proposal here goes further. If indeterminacy belongs to reality, absolute nothingness would be a peculiar candidate for an initial condition. It would require every possible change to be excluded. A quantum vacuum already has fields, relations and a rule for what can count as an excitation. Calling it nothing would smuggle most of physics into the premise. My conjecture is that acquiring definite physical structure is natural to an indeterminate reality. The oscillator gives that conjecture a physical point of departure. It does not explain why there is a quantum rule in the first place.[2][3]

What would it mean for the universe to be assembled? Stars forming from gas are one answer at the level of objects. I want to entertain a more demanding answer. Some relations which every star now obeys may have acquired their fixed form through earlier changes. A satisfactory account would have to identify what could vary, what fixed a relation, and why it gives the same result in another laboratory. If time belongs to what took shape, even the word *earlier* incurs a debt. In Part IV we will meet an experimenter who used one photon as a clock for another.

The slit, the cold beam and the heated plate leave us with three different questions about creation. Which outcomes can occur together? What energy can still be drawn upon? How fast can the allowed route carry it? The first of those questions can sometimes be settled before a single part moves. It can even be settled with a pencil.
