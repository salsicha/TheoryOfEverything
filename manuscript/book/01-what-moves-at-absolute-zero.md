# Chapter 1 — Ontological Uncertainty and the Assembly of Matter

*Part I · Physics*

Olaf Nairz and his colleagues heated a powder in an oven in Vienna until molecules of seventy carbon atoms began flying out. Two slits let a narrow beam of them through. When the team closed the second slit from 1.4 micrometers to about a twentieth of that width, the molecules that got through spread *farther* across the detector. The researchers knew more precisely where each molecule had passed. They had less certainty about where to find it downstream.[1]

What spread out was the flight of the whole molecule. Its seventy carbon atoms stayed together in a cage from the oven to the detector. How can a piece of matter keep its shape when the positions and motions of the particles that make it up admit a range of values?[1]

The oven ran at about 900 kelvin. Heat can set a molecule vibrating. Yet Nairz could change the spread at the detector by adjusting the slit while leaving the oven alone. What he varied was how closely the slit confined the molecule’s sideways position. Quantum mechanics sets a lower bound on the product of the spreads in position and momentum. The narrower opening gave the emerging beam a wider range of sideways momenta. What would remain of such a spread if the heat could be taken away?[1]

## What cooling removes

At Caltech, Amir Safavi-Naeini and his colleagues used light to cool one vibration of a tiny silicon beam. This mode vibrated nearly four billion times a second. They sent in a second beam of light to measure it. A photon could leave with a little more energy by taking one unit of vibration from the silicon. It could also leave with a little less energy by adding a unit. That gave the group two signals to follow as the silicon cooled. The nearer the mode came to its lowest energy, the fewer units there were for a photon to take. A photon could still put one in. As cooling continued, the photons that added a unit increasingly outnumbered those that took one away. From that ratio the team could measure the fluctuations that remained.[2]

Absolute zero matters here because it sets a limit on what cooling can remove. At that temperature an oscillator in equilibrium occupies its lowest energy state. For the beam to sit at one exact position with exactly zero momentum would violate the same uncertainty relation the Vienna team had tested. Confining it closer to the center costs energy in the spread of momentum. A wider spread of positions stores more elastic energy in the silicon. Even the state of least energy retains some spread in both. Physicists call the energy of that remaining vibration *zero-point energy*. A mode in its ground state has no more energy to give up.[2]

## How a hydrogen atom forms

An electron attracted to a proton faces a similar constraint. Bringing the two closer lowers their electrical potential energy. Why, then, does hydrogen have any size at all? Richard Feynman gave his physics students a way to estimate it. Try halving the width of the region occupied by the electron. At that scale the electrical binding is about twice as strong. But half the width calls for about twice the spread of momentum, and the kinetic energy costs about four times as much. At sufficiently small distances, squeezing the electron in any closer raises the total energy.[3]

The lowest energy occurs at a finite scale. For hydrogen the characteristic radius is about five hundredths of a nanometer. The electron can occupy a spread of positions in that state, yet it takes a definite amount of energy to free the electron, about 13.6 electron volts for hydrogen in its ground state. The spread in the electron’s position helps set the size of the atom.[3]

Knowing that a bound state exists still leaves a question about its history. How did an electron ever get into it? A free electron can speed up as it approaches a proton and slow again as it flies away. To stay, the pair must lose energy. In a thin gas a photon can carry it off. The electron and proton then travel together as a neutral hydrogen atom. Another photon with enough energy can separate them again. Which happens more often depends on the gas and the radiation around it.[4]

That competition once changed what light could do across the whole sky. In the hot early universe, energetic photons readily freed electrons from newly formed atoms. Light scattered again and again from the free electrons. As the universe expanded, the radiation cooled. At an age of about 380,000 years, with the temperature still around 3,000 kelvin, enough electrons had bound to nuclei for much of the light to travel freely. The Planck satellite mapped that light across the sky. Its last scattering tells us when neutral atoms had grown abundant. Hydrogen could assemble thousands of degrees above absolute zero. Cooling the silicon beam had answered a question about uncertainty. Cooling the early universe changed how long a newly formed atom could last.[2][4]

A proton attracted an electron just as strongly in the hotter gas. What changed was how often a photon could break the pair apart. As captures began to last, neutral atoms became plentiful enough to feed another reaction. A hydrogen atom could pick up a spare electron, meet a second atom, and release the spare as the two atoms formed a molecule. In a contracting gas cloud, those molecules could gain energy in collisions and shed it as light, helping the cloud cool and contract further. One step in assembly left something that could change the rate of the next.[3][4]

Hydrogen has a definite binding energy even though its quantum state assigns a range of outcomes to a measurement of the electron’s position. Does a particular outcome become definite in the interaction itself? If so, the uncertainty belongs to physical reality. This is *ontological uncertainty*. In Delft, physicists measured correlations between two electron spins far enough apart that a signal from either measurement could not reach the other in time. The correlations exceeded the bound for local explanations in which the answers were fixed in advance. Several interpretations of quantum mechanics fit that result. They agree about the energy it takes to split a hydrogen atom.[3][5]

An account of the first atoms already assumes electrons, electric charge and the quantum rules for binding. Even a quantum vacuum has fields and laws. Absolute nothingness would lack all of them. Asking whether the universe itself was assembled takes the argument one step further. Did the relations that give hydrogen its size also acquire their values through a physical process? An answer would have to explain how those values became the same wherever hydrogen forms. With the values known, a physicist can rule out some arrangements before a single encounter takes place. On a Sudoku grid, a penciled digit can exclude a whole set of arrangements just as decisively.[3][5]
