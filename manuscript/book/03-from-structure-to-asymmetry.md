# Chapter 3 — From Structure to Asymmetry

Ko van der Weele and his colleagues shook a tube containing six hundred glass beads. Low walls divided the tube into three equal compartments. Under strong shaking beads hopped the walls and the three populations stayed much alike. With weaker shaking, one compartment could end up with most of the beads. All three still had the same floor, the same walls and the same way out.[1]

A compartment that gained a few beads offered more chances for collisions. The impacts cost the beads some of their speed. In a crowded compartment fewer could clear the partition and escape. Once more beads hopped into the crowded compartment than could hop back out, the surplus grew. Shaking the box harder could disperse the cluster again. With three compartments the drive at which a cluster broke up could differ from the drive at which it formed. The route into an arrangement had changed the route out.[1]

Which compartment would fill? The geometry gave none a preference. It did determine whether a small excess would dissipate or recruit more beads. That distinction gives the claim that structure becomes asymmetric some content. A symmetric state can last under one drive. Change the drive until the state becomes unstable and the same rules that sustained equality can amplify a departure from it. Does the universe carry records of differences broad enough to have changed what matter could do?[1]

## A nucleus distinguishes left from right

At 12:04 on December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler began a run with a sample of radioactive cobalt at the National Bureau of Standards in Washington. Chien-Shiung Wu and the low-temperature team had cooled the specimen to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. A magnetic field lined up the spins of its cobalt-60 nuclei. As the cobalt decayed, a detector at one end of the sample counted the electrons that came its way. The count began high. As the sample warmed and its nuclei lost their alignment, it fell.[2]

The team cooled the sample again and reversed the current in the coil. The detector stayed where it was. This time its count began low and rose as the sample warmed. Both runs ended at the rate for nuclei pointing every which way. The difference showed up when the nuclei faced the same way. More electrons flew opposite to the nuclear spin than along it.[2]

Reverse all three spatial axes in the description of a decay. An electron’s direction of flight reverses. The direction of spin, which describes a rotation, stays the same. An electron that flew against the spin in the original decay now flies along it. If a decay and its mirror image were equally likely, the two settings of the coil would have given the same initial count. One page in Ambler’s notebook contained the evidence that a law of nature could tell the difference.[2]

Physicists call the underlying handedness *chirality*. Reflection exchanges the left- and right-chiral components of an electron field. Only the left-chiral component couples to the charged weak current responsible for this decay. A second distinction matters for the history of the universe. As the early plasma cooled, the Higgs field took on the background value that gives the carriers of the weak force their masses. The theory already treats left and right differently above that crossover. Wu’s counts give any proposed history of that law something definite to explain.[2][3]

## What survived annihilation

A proton and an antiproton can annihilate when they meet. Remove one of each from any population and whatever surplus it had before is still there. In the hot early universe collisions could also make fresh pairs. Expansion cooled the plasma until it could no longer replace them in large numbers. Annihilation then used up almost all the pairs. We can estimate what survived. The oldest light and the abundances of the light elements put the net excess at about six protons or neutrons for every ten billion photons in today’s microwave background. Those few survivors, gathered over an immense volume, supplied the ordinary matter for stars.[4]

Could an equal amount of antimatter have survived in other regions? Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow calculated what we ought to see where such regions touch. Hydrogen and antihydrogen diffusing across a boundary would annihilate. The radiation from all those boundaries would reach us as a background of gamma rays. Its measured brightness leaves little room inside the observable universe for a patchwork of comparable regions of the kind they studied.[4]

In 1967 Andrei Sakharov set out what it would take to generate a surplus from an initially balanced population. Some reactions would have to change the net number of baryons, the family that includes protons and neutrons. Their rates would have to distinguish matter from antimatter even after exchanging left and right. And conditions would have to depart from thermal equilibrium, where inverse reactions wash out the gain. The expansion of the universe could provide that last opportunity if a reaction grew too slow to keep up with the cooling plasma.[5]

Part of that list can be investigated by counting decays. In 2025 the LHCb collaboration reported a difference between how often a particular heavy baryon decayed to a proton, a kaon and two pions and how often its antimatter partner took the corresponding route. The comparison accounted for differences in production and in what the detector could see. Here were unequal probabilities under the combined exchange of particle for antiparticle and left for right. In every decay counted, however, one baryon went in and one came out, or one antibaryon went in and one came out. Generating the six survivors per ten billion photons still calls for a process that changes that balance.[4][5]

## The arrow of time

In both of Ambler’s runs the cobalt warmed. Reversing the current in the coil reversed which way most electrons flew. To get the cold, aligned sample back, the team had to cool it again, boiling off liquid helium in the process. Ambler’s notebook thus preserves two orientations. The electron counts distinguished a direction in space. The flow of heat into the sample distinguished a direction in its history.[2][6]

In 1844 James Prescott Joule reported what happened when he joined two copper vessels with a stopcock. He compressed air into one, pumped the other nearly empty and immersed both in a single bath of water. When he opened the valve, air rushed into the second vessel. His thermometer registered no net change in the temperature of the bath. Yet the pressure difference that could have driven a piston was gone. Closing the valve and opening it again would give him no second rush of air.[7]

The ordinary mechanical equations for a collision between gas molecules work in either direction. Reverse their velocities just after they collide and they retrace their paths. For the whole gas to return to the first vessel, an extraordinary number of paths would have to fit together. For an ideal gas at equilibrium in two equal volumes, the chance that ten particular molecules are all on the original side is one in 1,024. Every additional molecule halves that chance again. Releasing the gas gave it access to a far greater number of microscopic arrangements. Its entropy rose.[6][7]

Time itself has no intrinsic direction. What breaks the equivalence of the two directions in this history is the low-entropy condition at one end. Joule had to pump the air to prepare that condition. Wu’s team had to cool the cobalt. Once a little heat left the warmer apparatus and entered the cold sample, the sample gained more entropy than the apparatus lost. In both experiments the direction of increasing entropy gives the history its *thermodynamic arrow of time*. A laboratory can account for how it prepared a low-entropy state. Where did the universe get its own?[2][6][7]

Julian Barbour, Tim Koslowski and Flavio Mercati asked what an undirected law could accomplish on its own. They followed a thousand particles attracting one another under Newton’s law of gravity. The group reached a point of minimum overall size. Follow the solution away from that point in either direction and some particles form close pairs while others draw far apart. A measure of clustering fluctuated but grew on both sides. There was a distinguishable direction of development on either branch, even though the equations preferred neither. The calculation establishes this result for gravitational clustering. Connecting it to the entropy of our universe requires evidence about our own past.[8]

The earliest light we can see shows matter spread almost evenly across the sky. A small region of the early plasma could be close to thermal equilibrium while gravity still had work to do on much larger scales. Matter could fall together, heat up and radiate for billions of years. Roger Penrose identified that smooth early distribution as a special, low-entropy condition for a gravitating universe. How such a condition arose remains open. Some microscopic laws also distinguish the two directions. The BaBar collaboration measured different rates for particular transitions of neutral B mesons and their time reverses. Joule’s gas needed only reversible molecular collisions and the pressure difference he had prepared.[6][7][9]

## How a difference became a gradient

In 1992 George Smoot and his colleagues reported the small departures from uniformity in the oldest light. The receivers aboard the Cosmic Background Explorer compared patches of sky sixty degrees apart. The team subtracted the large pattern due to our own motion and excluded the bright band of the Milky Way. Adding the maps from paired receivers kept a signal that disappeared when one map was subtracted from the other. On patches ten degrees across, the temperature varied by about thirty millionths of a kelvin.[9]

A slight excess of mass attracts its surroundings. In the early plasma, radiation pressure pushed the ordinary matter outward each time gravity compressed it. When the universe cooled enough for electrons to bind to nuclei, light could travel far between collisions. Gas could now fall into concentrations of dark matter. Tom Abel, Greg Bryan and Michael Norman followed one such cloud in a simulation. Falling gas heated as it collided. When a collision set a hydrogen molecule rotating or vibrating, the molecule could release the energy in a photon that escaped the cloud. More gas could fall. By the time roughly a hundred solar masses had gathered in the central region, its own gravity overcame its pressure.[9][10]

Once the gas could cool, the slight departure from uniformity fed its own growth. Each parcel that fell in increased the pull on the next. A surplus of matter supplied the hydrogen. A slight excess in one place gave the gas a direction to fall. A cold sky gave the heat of that fall somewhere to go. The handedness of the weak force will matter again inside stars, where a proton has to change into a neutron for ordinary hydrogen burning to begin. The claim that assembly takes the route with the greatest sustained rate of entropy production turns on what each route can carry. A cloud that traps its heat develops pressure against further infall. One that radiates can keep drawing gas down. To rank their rates takes both the energy escaping per second and the entropy carried by the radiation.[2][9][10][11]

These observations make different demands on a history of asymmetry. The weak interaction already distinguishes left from right in its laws. The matter surplus is a difference in populations. The warming cobalt had a cold past, and a gas cloud could grow because gravity amplified a difference in density. As the universe expands and cools, structures pass through conditions that used to keep differences small. The claim that every symmetric stage eventually gives way depends on identifying an instability or a source of asymmetry at each stage. The sky supplies a case where the difference grew large enough to make something new. A record of what stars did with that gas survives in grains smaller than a speck of dust.[2][4][6][9][10]
