# Chapter 15 — Fermi paradox

On September 28, 1943, Walter Baade pointed the 100-inch telescope on Mount Wilson just to one side of Andromeda’s bright nucleus. In earlier photographs the light there had run together. Baade gave a red-sensitive plate four hours behind a red filter. When he developed it, the smooth patch broke up into a dense field of faint stars. The blue-sensitive plates that worked so well on the spiral arms had barely registered the brightest of them.[1]

Baade distinguished two stellar populations. He called the group that included the bright blue stars of the spiral arms *Population I*. He called the group he recognized in Andromeda’s center and in old globular clusters *Population II*. The numbering runs backward through history. Population I is the youngest of the broad stellar populations. It includes stars forming in the Milky Way today and the Sun, which formed about 4.6 billion years ago. Its stars generally inherited more carbon, oxygen, silicon, iron and other elements heavier than helium. Astronomers call all of these elements *metals*.[1][2]

In 1950 Enrico Fermi asked his lunch companions at Los Alamos where everybody was. They understood him to mean visitors from other worlds. The Galaxy is billions of years older than the Sun. One might expect that somewhere a society had arisen, crossed the distance to another star and kept going. But which stars could first have supplied life with a home? And once a species had a home, what would give it a reason to leave?[3]

One answer to Fermi is that life had to wait for Population I stars. Only then, on this account, did planet-forming gas carry enough of the elements needed to sustain living chemistry. There is a second wait even for a species that has learned to build spacecraft. While its star can power a living world, its inhabitants can explore by sending machines. Only when that star begins to die, threatening the last viable home in its system, does migration to another star offer their descendants a better chance of survival than staying. Even if life is widespread, the first such departures could still lie ahead.[2][4][7]

## What a living planet had to inherit

The first stars, now called Population III, could shine with little more than hydrogen and helium. An ocean needs oxygen. Rock beneath it needs elements such as silicon and magnesium. Iron changes what the rock and water can do together. Earlier stars forged the heavier elements and returned them to space in winds and explosions. Later clouds incorporated them into new stars and into the disks where planets grew. Some silicate grains in our meteorites still carry the oxygen isotope proportions of stars that lived before the Sun.[2]

Cold seawater enters fractures in the Atlantic floor at Lost City. In the rock below, water reacts with iron-bearing minerals and hydrogen accumulates in the returning fluid. Where that fluid meets seawater, microbes draw energy from the chemical difference. As water reaches fresh rock, the supply is renewed. Another planet might contain as much iron as Earth yet hold most of it beyond the ocean’s reach. For life to get started by the route in Chapter 4, rock, water and carbon have to meet in a way that keeps supplying energy to growing cells. Life could arise promptly once those conditions came together and still come late in the history of the Galaxy. The long wait would be in the making of a suitable planet.[2][4]

NASA’s Kepler telescope recorded five patterns of dimming in the light from Kepler-444, a metal-poor star in the Milky Way’s old thick disk. Five small planets were passing in front of it. Tiago Campante and his colleagues measured oscillations in the star and estimated its age at about eleven billion years. All five planets are smaller than Earth. All five complete a year in less than ten of our days. The Galaxy could make small planets long before it made the Sun. The dimming tells us their sizes and orbits. What remains to be established is when a planet could first keep an ocean in contact with reactive rock for long enough to acquire a biosphere. Evidence that life arose on a world born from the older, metal-poor population would overturn the proposed Population I cutoff.[4][5]

## Immediate evolution on a ready planet

On Earth there is a way to separate the time spent preparing a habitat from the time life took to gain a foothold in it. The first clock is in a mineral older than the rocks that surround it.

The rock in which the Jack Hills zircon grew had long since eroded. On one grain recovered in Western Australia, Simon Wilde’s team used uranium and the lead left by its decay to date part of the crystal to 4.404 billion years ago, give or take eight million years. Within that grain they found zones with different proportions of oxygen isotopes. Those proportions suggested that some of the material that formed the zircon had interacted with liquid water at low temperature.[9]

An impact could still destroy the products of a young ocean’s chemistry. The claim here is that Earth spent geological ages becoming habitable, then acquired life in the first interval when a lineage could survive. Once water, raw material and chemical energy endured together, the waiting time for life belonged to reactions and generations.[9][10]

Oleg Abramov and his colleagues used an impact history and a thermal model to ask when RNA chemistry could persist between strikes. Their 2026 study placed favorable conditions around 4.33 billion years ago. The date marks a change in the modeled habitat. Finding a molecule made at the time would require another kind of evidence.[10]

Edmund Moody’s team worked backward from genes shared by distant branches of life. They anchored parts of the tree to dated events and estimated rates of change. Their last universal common ancestor lived roughly 4.2 billion years ago, with one reported interval from 4.33 to 4.09 billion years. This was already an evolved population. Earlier lineages that died out have no living descendants to put on the tree. In Western Australia, Tara Djokic and colleagues found evidence for microbes at hot springs about 3.48 billion years ago. Their rocks put organisms at a particular spring. The settlers could have had a long ancestry.[11]

Subtracting the two central estimates gives about 130 million years between a modeled chemical window and a population with ancestors. That interval can contain many generations after the founding. To date the founding itself we need to know how a family of reactions could establish before its habitat disappeared.[9][10][11]

### When reactions begin to leave descendants

At Lost City the rock determines much of what enters a pore. Which bonds can form and which products stay until the next flow restrict the next reaction. Counting all the arrangements of a modern cell ignores those material and Platonic constraints at each step.[12]

Wim Hordijk, Mike Steel and Stuart Kauffman modeled chains made of two kinds of building block. They fixed the chance that a chain could catalyze a given reaction and allowed successively longer chains. Each additional position doubled the number of chains of that length. In one series of simulations, by length thirteen there was a good chance of finding a set whose members could be built from the starting food and whose reactions all had catalysts among that food or the set itself. One new chain might supply a catalyst several incomplete routes lacked.[12]

In running water the catalysts must also form faster than they wash out. Once a network can replace them it can outlast the molecules it began with. Heredity adds a further requirement. A descendant must inherit enough of the arrangement to carry on, along with differences it can pass to its own descendants. When each founding unit leaves fewer than one viable successor on average, families dwindle. Above one, some families can survive and spread. Natural selection could begin while the whole family still fit inside one pore.[12]

At Scripps, Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce studied two RNA catalysts that helped make one another. Their population could double in about an hour. When several pairs competed for the same feed, new combinations arose and some displaced their parents. The team had supplied the starting catalysts. The hour timed their multiplication. On the young Earth the first pair, or other chemistry capable of passing on its organization, also had to form.[12]

Why expect even the first workable combination to turn up promptly? A field of mineral pores gave the same restricted chemistry many starts. Each additional kind of molecule could supply a missing reaction for several sets at once. Each additional pore offered another place where one set might begin operating before a current dispersed it. If attempts are independent and roughly steady, doubling the number of equally well supplied pores halves the expected wait for the first successful start. A common impact can cut all those attempts short. Cooling and the retreat of frequent impacts changed both how many places qualified and how long each one had.[10][12]

Once descendants occupied fresh surfaces, some chemical energy that would have gone out to sea in unreacted compounds could pass through living cells. To argue for an origin in the first habitable interval, the wait for that first surviving family must be short compared with the life of the habitat. To argue for the highest rate of dissipation one must also account for the energy its descendants carried away in cells and products.[10][11][12]

### One season on Daphne Major

Peter Boag and Peter Grant had measured Darwin’s finches on Daphne Major before the drought of 1977. As food grew scarce, large hard seeds remained. Large birds, especially males with large beaks, survived in greater proportion. The useful beaks existed before the soft seeds ran out. A single dry season changed who could eat.[13]

A flask that turned cloudy in 2003 gives a reason to ask what a population already carried before a change. Richard Lenski’s team had supplied twelve populations with glucose and citrate for years. One acquired the ability to grow on citrate in the presence of oxygen. From frozen samples the researchers could give earlier ancestors’ descendants another chance at the same food. Descendants of certain later ancestors acquired the trait more readily. The citrate had been there all along. What it took to reach it had accumulated over tens of thousands of generations.[14]

An observer who needs billions of years to evolve has a better chance of finding herself on a planet where life started early. David Kipping allowed for that bias. In 2025, using the proposed 4.2-billion-year date for the common ancestor and a start to habitability near 4.4 billion years, he obtained odds of roughly thirteen to one for rapid over slow abiogenesis. Here *rapid* is relative to a planet’s habitable lifetime. The first suitable interval is a finer division than those dates resolve.[9][11][15]

A grain like the Jack Hills zircon can date an early condition without relying on a fossil. Establish when water, chemical energy and building material persisted together, then look for independent traces of descendants. Evidence of sterile habitats enduring far longer than the chemistry required would put a second long wait after Earth became habitable. A lineage dating from the first such interval would put the delay in the cooling of the planet.[9][10][11][12][15]

A short wait for life on a ready planet would bring Fermi’s question forward only so far. A species still has to acquire the ability to cross between stars and a reason to take its living population along.

## What changes when the star starts to die

In 1977, about 4.6 billion years after the Solar System formed, people on Earth launched Voyager 1. Billions of years remained before the Sun would become a red giant. In April 2013 the spacecraft recorded electrons oscillating around it at about 2,600 cycles per second. From that frequency Don Gurnett and his colleagues inferred a density of about eight hundredths of an electron per cubic centimeter. The gas was dense enough to place the spacecraft beyond the Sun’s wind. Working backward through the readings, the team put the crossing in August 2012. What the scientists needed from interstellar space was a measurement. It came home by radio.[2][6][7]

Voyager needed electricity for its instruments and radio. A ship carrying a living community would also need to grow or store food, recover water and shed waste heat throughout the crossing. On arrival the travelers would need to build a place where their children could do the same. At home they already have a biosphere doing much of that work and can put more of their star’s energy to use there. Curiosity can pay for an instrument that radios home. What the instrument can never bring back is a new home when the old one is failing.[6][7]

The approach of the Sun’s old age will bring Earth more sunlight. The Sun grows more luminous even while it still burns hydrogen at its center. Eric Wolf and Brian Toon calculated how Earth’s climate responds. As the surface warms, more water vapor reaches the upper atmosphere, where sunlight can split water molecules and hydrogen can escape to space. A brighter star can cost a planet its ocean. Later, after the Sun exhausts hydrogen at its center, its outer layers will expand. Klaus-Peter Schröder and Robert Smith put the Sun at its greatest size on its first red-giant ascent about 7.6 billion years from now. In their calculation, tides draw Earth into the Sun even as mass loss tends to widen Earth’s orbit.[7]

A red giant can warm worlds farther out for a time. Descendants with somewhere else to live around the Sun could leave Earth and stay in the Solar System. Eventually the Sun will shed its envelope, leaving a white dwarf that cools. When the last local refuge is in sight of failure, a journey to a longer-lived star can buy generations of habitable time. That is the point at which the cost of staying overtakes the cost of leaving. The travelers would have to depart while they could still build and launch.[7]

That long warning could change the price of the journey itself. Stars move past one another. Brad Hansen and Ben Zuckerman calculated the closest approach to expect over a billion years in the Sun’s neighborhood. Their median was about 1,500 times the Earth–Sun distance, roughly a hundredth of the usual distance between neighboring stars. A society could have time to identify a suitable passing system and make the crossing when it was short.[7]

## Has that deadline come anywhere yet?

In NGC 6791, a metal-rich cluster about eight billion years old, Hubble photographs reveal white dwarfs. Some stars born from enriched gas have already died. How much time their planets had depends on the mass of the star. A massive blue star can burn through its fuel in a few million years. A star like the Sun can give a biosphere billions of years to develop and still have billions of years left. For living visitors to be due on Earth, some system within reach must have had the elements early enough, sustained life long enough to produce voyagers, and reached its stellar deadline long enough ago for them to get here. Earth has produced interstellar probes. Carrying a living community across remains unproved, and our star’s deadline lies ahead. Whether any other system has completed the sequence is open.[1][5][6][7]

A probe from a system with a healthy star could be doing what Voyager did for us. A machine that could manufacture copies at its destination might spread much farther while its builders stayed home. A radio signal could likewise come from people who still had a good place to live. A living settlement whose origin we could trace would tell us more. If its founders had left a system that could have supported them far into the future, they had found a reason to go before their star began to die.[3][6][8]

The stars that died before the Sun supplied Earth with the elements for a living ocean. The decline of the Sun itself would give its inhabitants a reason to seek another star. For living visitors to have reached us, both events must already have happened somewhere else, with time left for the crossing.[2][7]
