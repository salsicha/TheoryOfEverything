# Chapter 11 — What a Ship Could Say

*Part III · Technology*

In April 1789 members of Parliament received a drawing of a ship. Someone had taken off the deck so the reader could look straight down. Along the narrowing sides lay rows of people, their bodies turned to fit the hull. A section showed the clearance above their heads. Many people who saw the sheet could never have named the vessel. In the London printing its name, *Brooks*, occurs partway through a paragraph.[1]

A merchant could look up the same ship in a register. There her name mattered. She had first gone in as *Brook*, then as *Brooks*. The register could tell a subscriber which vessel he was dealing with. Lloyd’s Register had developed a compact language for the purpose. In its surviving 1764 register the compilers used vowels to grade the hull and the letters G, M and B to grade equipment as good, middling and bad. The symbols subsequently changed. The problem they solved remained. A man in one port had to decide whether to trust a vessel he might never see in another.[1][2]

Removing information made the register useful. Checking the soundness of a hull was expensive. Once someone else had done it a merchant could consult a line of print. He could compare dozens of ships on the same terms. He would learn much less about the experience of anyone shut below deck. A sound hull might give a merchant confidence in the very voyage whose conduct should have made other people want to stop it. One of the most consequential choices in making a medium is deciding which question a stranger will be able to ask of it.

## A ship drawn to scale

In late 1788 William Elford and his colleagues in Plymouth had obtained a different kind of information. A naval officer, Captain Parrey, had measured nine vessels engaged in the slave trade. The Plymouth group used the dimensions of the Liverpool ship to draw a plan. In London the abolition committee added views. James Phillips printed the result and the committee sent it to MPs and others. People copied it again, rearranging pieces and sometimes cutting much of the accompanying text. Parliamentary readers who disagreed about almost everything could inspect the same few feet of deck on the same sheet of paper.[1]

A naval measurement had changed employers. A measurement could tell someone how much a ship would hold. In the hands of Elford and the abolition committee the width all but instructed the eye to do a second calculation. How much room did one person have to lie down? The rows answered in units a reader carried around without having to consult a table. A body had shoulders. A person who could lie on one side might wish to turn over. The men who made the print could use the reader’s knowledge of that movement without having to tell the reader what to feel.

A drawing that could cross the country for the cost of a sheet of paper also exacted a price. No one in it has a name. Its orderly rows can be mistaken for a picture of a particular voyage, down to the number and posture of the people on board. It was constructed from dimensions for an argument. In his autobiography, published in 1789, Olaudah Equiano described a crossing on another vessel. He recalled the heat and foul air and the difficulty of turning over once the ship filled. People sickened. People died. He could say what it was like to have to remain in that space after the reader had been free to put down the print. His account also gave a name and a subsequent life to someone the drawing would have represented by an outline.[3]

What does it mean to call morality a communication technology? A sailor who thought a voyage wrong could decline work if he could afford to. A reader who believed other people would condemn the trade too had another option. She could help circulate evidence, sign a petition or demand that her representative act. A publicly recognizable judgment changes what people can ask of one another and what they can reasonably expect others to support. Here the print let the person doing the asking point to a fact the recipient could inspect. That use of morality presupposes people capable of caring about the treatment of strangers. The number of people a campaign persuades cannot settle whether the treatment was just.

The change took time. *Brooks* continued carrying enslaved people until 1804. Britain outlawed its transatlantic slave trade in 1807. Enslaved people across much of the British Empire waited decades more for emancipation. It would be convenient to show an MP receiving Phillips’s sheet and to credit the print with the vote. We know the sheet arrived. The years in which ships continued to sail tell us how much else a moral judgment had to overcome before it could change what investors, officials and captains were permitted to do.[1][4] An account of society driven by energy extraction has to reckon with a campaign organized to stop a profitable trade. Closing a route can redirect labor, ships and fuel elsewhere or leave some of them idle. Evidence that a picture traveled quickly cannot decide which happened to those resources. The people who opposed the trade had a reason to close it in either case.

## What a second opinion is worth

Confidence also travels. The register economized on inspection only so long as users could learn how much reliance to place on its marks. In a much smaller setting Bahador Bahrami and his colleagues arranged for two people to see patterns on screens. On each trial one of two displays contained a patch of higher contrast. Each observer picked the interval in which it had appeared. On trials where the partners disagreed, they had to reach a joint decision. One might have seen a difference at once. The other might have been a long time choosing.[5]

With similar visual sensitivity and a means to communicate, pairs could do better than either member alone. With a large difference in sensitivity, a pair could do worse than its better observer. Having a second opinion helped to the extent that each person could convey the strength of the evidence behind a choice, and the strength conveyed meant something comparable to the partner. Both people could be sincere. Confidence is information which changes the weight someone else gives an observation. Someone who speaks with assurance on every trial eventually ceases to transmit that information.[5]

A register built to communicate confidence in ships and a print built to induce moral judgment imposed different constraints on the same material. What each omitted helped it travel. Each offered different strangers a way to act together. Each could mislead in ways characteristic of its form. Return to the name partway through the paragraph. For a reader looking up an insured ship, leaving that name out would spoil the entry. Copies of the abolitionist drawing could lose it and still make their point. A reader could recognize what was being done to people on a ship whose name she never learned.
