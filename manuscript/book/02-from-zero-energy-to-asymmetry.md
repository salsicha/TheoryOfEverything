# Chapter 2 — From zero energy to asymmetry

In 2019 Gian Francesco Giudice, Alex Kehagias and Antonio Riotto calculated what would happen on either side of a bubble forming in the early universe. Inside its wall the energy of empty space dropped. The same event shifted a quantity that helps determine the masses of the particles that carry the weak force. Wherever the wall passed, it left the same pair of values behind.[1]

In their calculation a field stores energy in steps. A whole number counts the units of flux in a region. Forming a bubble wall costs energy. Once a large enough bubble forms, its interior holds one unit less, and the energy released as the interior grows drives the wall outward. Another bubble can form inside the first. If the last transition triggers a period of rapid expansion called inflation, one patch can grow large enough to contain the observable universe. Stars billions of light-years apart could then inherit some of the same physical constants from a single event.[1]

That is a concrete sense in which constants could have a history. The calculation still takes the size of a flux unit and the strength of its coupling to the Higgs field as given. Chapter 13 returns to what it would take to predict their values. Here a different question follows. A universe with the same laws everywhere can look much the same in every direction. Yet a radioactive nucleus distinguishes left from right. Nearly all the atoms we observe are made of matter. Heat carried away by a star records a direction in time. What would an account of our beginnings have to explain about each of those differences?[1][2]

## A nucleus distinguishes left from right

At 12:04 on December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler began a run with a sample of radioactive cobalt at the National Bureau of Standards in Washington. Chien-Shiung Wu and the low-temperature team had cooled the specimen to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. A magnetic field lined up the spins of its cobalt-60 nuclei. As the cobalt decayed, a detector at one end of the sample counted the electrons that came its way. The count began high. As the sample warmed and its nuclei lost their alignment, it fell.[2]

The team cooled the sample again and reversed the current in the coil. The detector stayed where it was. This time its count began low and rose as the sample warmed. Both runs ended at the rate for nuclei pointing every which way. The difference showed up when the nuclei faced the same way. More electrons flew opposite to the nuclear spin than along it.[2]

Reverse all three spatial axes in the description of a decay. An electron’s direction of flight reverses. The direction of spin, which describes a rotation, stays the same. An electron that flew against the spin in the original decay now flies along it. If a decay and its mirror image were equally likely, the two settings of the coil would have given the same initial count. One page in Ambler’s notebook contained the evidence that a law of nature could tell the difference.[2]

Physicists call the underlying handedness *chirality*. Reflection exchanges the left- and right-chiral components of an electron field. Only the left-chiral component couples to the charged weak current responsible for this decay. A second distinction matters for the history of the universe. As the early plasma cooled, the Higgs field took on the background value that gives the carriers of the weak force their masses. The theory already treats left and right differently above that crossover. Wu’s counts give any proposed history of that law something definite to explain.[2][3]

## What survived annihilation

A proton and an antiproton can annihilate when they meet. Remove one of each from any population and whatever surplus it had before is still there. In the hot early universe collisions could also make fresh pairs. Expansion cooled the plasma until it could no longer replace them in large numbers. Annihilation then used up almost all the pairs. We can estimate what survived. The oldest light and the abundances of the light elements put the net excess at about six protons or neutrons for every ten billion photons in today’s microwave background. Those few survivors, gathered over an immense volume, supplied the ordinary matter for stars.[4]

Could an equal amount of antimatter have survived in other regions? Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow calculated what we ought to see where such regions touch. Hydrogen and antihydrogen diffusing across a boundary would annihilate. The radiation from all those boundaries would reach us as a background of gamma rays. Its measured brightness leaves little room inside the observable universe for a patchwork of comparable regions of the kind they studied.[4]

In 1967 Andrei Sakharov set out what it would take to generate a surplus from an initially balanced population. Some reactions would have to change the net number of baryons, the family that includes protons and neutrons. Their rates would have to distinguish matter from antimatter even after exchanging left and right. And conditions would have to depart from thermal equilibrium, where inverse reactions wash out the gain. The expansion of the universe could provide that last opportunity if a reaction grew too slow to keep up with the cooling plasma.[5]

Part of that list can be investigated by counting decays. In 2025 the LHCb collaboration reported a difference between how often a particular heavy baryon decayed to a proton, a kaon and two pions and how often its antimatter partner took the corresponding route. The comparison accounted for differences in production and in what the detector could see. Here were unequal probabilities under the combined exchange of particle for antiparticle and left for right. In every decay counted, however, one baryon went in and one came out, or one antibaryon went in and one came out. Generating the six survivors per ten billion photons still calls for a process that changes that balance.[4][5]

## Which way heat goes

In both of Ambler’s runs the cobalt warmed. Reversing the coil reversed which way most electrons flew. To recover the cold, aligned sample for the second run, the team had to repeat a cooling procedure in which liquid helium boiled away. The apparatus kept two records on the same page. The electron counts picked out a direction in space. The passage of heat into the cold sample picked out a direction in time.[2][6]

When a little heat leaves a warm body and enters a colder one, the cold body gains more entropy than the warm one loses. Energy spreads among more possible arrangements of their particles. Getting the heat to gather back into the warm body would require coordinated motions among an enormous number of particles. For a sample the size of Wu’s, the conditions produced by ordinary thermal contact overwhelmingly favor the first course. To run that process again in a laboratory, someone prepares a cold sample again. Where did the universe get conditions that could play that part in the first place?[6]

Its earliest light shows matter spread almost evenly across the sky. The particles in a small region of that plasma could be close to thermal equilibrium. Across much greater distances gravity could still do work. A nearly uniform distribution had left open the possibility of matter falling together, heating up and radiating for billions of years. Roger Penrose identified this special early distribution as a problem in its own right. The contrast between that past and the states accessible afterward gives the thermal arrow somewhere to start. The origin of the special past remains open. Experiments have also found particle transitions whose rates differ when their sequence is reversed. Those rates enter particle physics. To understand the warming cobalt we still need to know which end of its history contained the freshly cooled sample.[6]

## How a difference became a gradient

In 1992 George Smoot and his colleagues reported the small departures from uniformity in the oldest light. The receivers aboard the Cosmic Background Explorer compared patches of sky sixty degrees apart. The team subtracted the large pattern due to our own motion and excluded the bright band of the Milky Way. Adding the maps from paired receivers kept a signal that disappeared when one map was subtracted from the other. On patches ten degrees across, the temperature varied by about thirty millionths of a kelvin.[7]

A slight excess of mass attracts its surroundings. In the early plasma, radiation pressure pushed the ordinary matter outward each time gravity compressed it. When the universe cooled enough for electrons to bind to nuclei, light could travel far between collisions. Gas could now fall into concentrations of dark matter. Tom Abel, Greg Bryan and Michael Norman followed one such cloud in a simulation. Falling gas heated as it collided. When a collision set a hydrogen molecule rotating or vibrating, the molecule could release the energy in a photon that escaped the cloud. More gas could fall. By the time roughly a hundred solar masses had gathered in the central region, its own gravity overcame its pressure.[7][8]

Once the gas could cool, the slight departure from uniformity fed its own growth. Each parcel that fell in increased the pull on the next. A surplus of matter supplied the hydrogen. A slight excess in one place gave the gas a direction to fall. A cold sky gave the heat of that fall somewhere to go. The handedness of the weak force will matter again inside stars, where a proton has to change into a neutron for ordinary hydrogen burning to begin. The claim that assembly takes the route with the greatest sustained rate of entropy production turns on what each route can carry. A cloud that traps its heat develops pressure against further infall. One that radiates can keep drawing gas down. To rank their rates takes both the energy escaping per second and the entropy carried by the radiation.[2][7][8][9]
