# Chapter 7 — Self Reproduction

A link broke in the wall on Francisco Varela, Humberto Maturana and Ricardo Uribe’s computer. Inside the wall a catalyst helped make new links from material passing through. If a replacement reached the gap in time, the wall closed. If the catalyst escaped first, the parts dispersed. The program gave each piece rules for what it could do when it met another.[1]

The researchers called this relation *autopoiesis*. The catalyst makes links, and the joined links keep the catalyst where it can make more. Through this loop a system produces the parts that keep the system organized. Fresh material must still come in and waste leave.[1]

## A machine that went home for a charge

In Bristol, W. Grey Walter built a machine that could find a light. His mechanical tortoise carried a photoelectric eye, a motor and a battery. A lamp and contacts marked a charging hutch. When well charged the tortoise turned away as it neared the light. When its battery ran low the same light could draw it far enough into the hutch to make contact. Once charged, it could leave. A change in the battery altered what the machine did with a signal from the eye.[2]

The steering responded to light. The charge on the battery altered how it responded. Together those two levels brought the machine home when it needed power. The charging contacts could do little for a tortoise that had lost a wheel.[2]

Norbert Wiener called the study of such control *cybernetics*. In a 1943 paper Arturo Rosenblueth, Wiener and Julian Bigelow described what can happen when a patient with a cerebellar disorder lifts a glass to his mouth. A correction can carry the hand too far the other way. With each swing the hand travels farther, until the water spills. Feedback can steady the glass or magnify a tremor, depending on when and how strongly the hand responds.[2]

The tortoise could recharge. It could never make the wheel that brought it home. In Varela’s model the action returned to the part doing the regulating. The wall confined the catalyst that made the next piece of wall. Increase the time it takes to replace a link and more catalysts should escape. Shorten it and more boundaries should close. Here the feedback loop makes the parts that keep it working, the feature that makes it autopoietic.[1][2]

## One becomes two

Finding one boundary intact on ten visits gives a different history from finding ten daughters on the last visit. For one cell to become two, each daughter must receive the equipment to carry on the work.

In marine algae collected in Japan, Kyoko Hagino’s team saw a small body of bacterial origin beside the chloroplasts. Later researchers used X-rays to examine cultured cells at different stages of division. The body, now called a nitroplast, divided in step with its host. Each daughter received a copy. Some of the proteins that copy needed came from genes in the host nucleus.[3]

The alga captures sunlight and fixes carbon. Its partner spends some of that carbon obtaining usable nitrogen. Consider the same light, seawater and total cell volume shared in different proportions. Give too little room to the partner and a shortage of nitrogen holds up the host’s growth. Give too much and there is too little host to feed the partner. At either extreme more investment in the abundant service buys little. The fastest balanced growth should come near the division of space at which the two supplies keep pace. Francisco Cornejo-Castillo and colleagues predicted a whole-cell to partner radius ratio of 2.47 in related partnerships. The mean across 167 measured pairs was 2.33.[3]

A daughter that inherits the nitrogen fixer can put its share of sunlight to work in water too poor in usable nitrogen for the host alone. A daughter that misses it must find nitrogen elsewhere. In a culture that supplies usable nitrogen, feeding the partner still costs carbon. One cultured strain lost its partner and kept growing.[3]

The light falling on a patch of sea sets a limit for all its cells. Coordinated division can bring them closer to that limit if sunlight still goes unused and the daughters survive. If one alga shades another, its gain can come at its neighbor’s expense. An entropy budget for the patch must also count energy stored in biomass or carried out by the current.[3]

In what sense does life reproduce *to consume free energy*? Every daughter that inherits this partnership can draw on sunlight where the shortage of available nitrogen would otherwise stop its growth. A cell that lives for one season spends the gradient for one season. A lineage can keep drawing on it after every original cell has died, and can spread to sunlit water beyond the reach of the parent. Selection needs no foresight to produce that effect. A lineage whose daughters can feed in the next patch leaves descendants there.[1][3]

The word *to* makes a claim we can check. Start with the same supply of light and nutrients and follow what happens to it over several generations. New cells lock up some energy as biomass. Some light passes through the water unused. Once the surface fills with cells, a daughter may shade a neighbor. The proposed energetic advantage of reproduction is the extra flow a multiplying lineage can maintain across that whole history. A count of daughters gives us the number to follow. The energy left in the water and the material they export would tell us how much of the gradient they used.[3][4]
