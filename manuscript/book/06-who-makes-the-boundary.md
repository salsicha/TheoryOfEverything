# Chapter 6 — Who Makes the Boundary?

*Part II · Humans*

A link broke in the little wall on Francisco Varela, Humberto Maturana and Ricardo Uribe’s computer. Inside the wall was a catalyst. Material could reach it there, and in its presence new links could form. If one of them replaced the broken link before the catalyst escaped, the wall could close again. Too large a break, or too long a wait, and the parts dispersed. The investigators had given the program rules for local encounters. They had not assigned a repairman to the hole.[1]

Why include such a model in a history of people? A person alive now consists of material different from much of the material that composed the child. To account for that continuity we need to explain which organization survives the replacement of its pieces. A list of the pieces present on Tuesday can be perfectly accurate and tell us very little about how there could be a living person on Wednesday.

The researchers gave a name to the relation they had modeled. In *autopoiesis*, a network of processes continually produces components of the organization that makes those processes possible. The catalyst helps make links. Joined links keep the catalyst in a region where its work can have a local effect. Precursor materials still come in and spent material can leave. A circle in the dependencies is compatible with a steady traffic of matter and energy across the boundary. A system closed to that traffic would soon use up what it needed.[1]

## A machine that went home for a charge

In Bristol, W. Grey Walter built a machine that could find a light. His mechanical tortoise carried a photoelectric eye, a motor and a battery. A lamp and contacts marked a charging hutch. When well charged the tortoise turned away as it neared the light. When its battery ran low the same light could draw it far enough into the hutch to make contact. Once charged, it could leave. A change in the battery altered what the machine did with a signal from the eye.[2]

A light-seeking machine with no second response might spend its life with its nose against a lamp. Walter set one loop of activity within reach of another. The steering responded to light. The battery’s state altered the behavior of the steering. The two levels did different jobs. Together they gave the machine a way to acquire energy at a time when it needed it. Remove a wheel and the hutch could still be shining when Walter came back to find the machine stranded on the floor.[2]

This was territory that Norbert Wiener called *cybernetics*. A result returns to influence the action that produced it. In a 1943 paper, Arturo Rosenblueth, Wiener and Julian Bigelow described a patient lifting a glass to his mouth. A correction to one side could prompt a larger correction to the other, until the movement sent the water spilling. The hand had a target and information about its error. The timing and size of the returning corrections decided whether it reached the target. Connecting output to input can disturb a system as well as steady it.[2]

Varela’s model makes a prediction at the instant the first link breaks. Two events can come next under exactly the same supply of material. A replacement can close the gap, or the catalyst can escape through it. Which usually happens first depends on the time it takes to make and fit a link compared with the time it takes the catalyst to drift away. Speed up escape without changing repair and more boundaries should fail. Speed up repair and the catalyst gets another turn at the material arriving from outside. This is how cybernetic feedback can become autopoiesis. The process that the wall keeps going makes the next piece of wall.[1][2]

## One becomes two

A single boundary that lasted a long time would give a microscopist repeated chances to see it. A population of boundaries poses another question. To produce two viable daughters, the system must produce enough of the relevant parts, distribute them, and leave each part of the division a means of continuing the work. One structure surviving ten inspections and ten structures surviving one inspection each could leave the same tally on a careless observer’s sheet.

Kyoko Hagino’s marine algae supply a case where the difference became visible. In cells collected in Japan her group could see a small body of bacterial origin beside the chloroplasts. Later researchers used X-rays to examine cultured cells at different stages of division. The small body, now called a nitroplast, divided in step with the host. Each daughter received a copy. Some of its working proteins came from genes in the host nucleus. A record of inheritance had to follow components into both compartments.[3]

The alga captures sunlight and fixes carbon. Its partner spends some of that carbon obtaining usable nitrogen. Consider the same light, seawater and total cell volume shared in different proportions. Give too little room to the partner and a shortage of nitrogen holds up the host’s growth. Give too much and there is too little host to feed the partner. At either extreme more investment in the abundant service buys little. The fastest balanced growth should come near the division of space at which the two supplies keep pace. Francisco Cornejo-Castillo and colleagues predicted a whole-cell to partner radius ratio of 2.47 in related partnerships. The mean across 167 measured pairs was 2.33.[3]

Proportions tell us what could work. The order of events tells us how the arrangement lasts. A daughter that inherits enough of both partners can make another pair. A daughter that misses the nitrogen fixer must find nitrogen elsewhere. When division keeps the partners together, their descendants can put additional light to work on each succeeding day that nitrogen would otherwise have limited growth. Supply usable nitrogen from outside and the cost of feeding a partner remains while much of the advantage disappears. One cultured strain did lose its partner and continued to grow.[3]

This is the energetic case for the assembly. The finite supply of light caps what all the cells in a patch can capture. Coordinated division can bring the patch closer to that cap over several generations, provided cells survive, light still goes unused, and the new growth does more than displace another organism. To rank entropy production across the patch, follow the sunlight and the energy retained or exported as biomass for the same interval. The observed proportions test a prediction about a bottleneck. They also tell us which bottleneck to change to make a different arrangement pay.[3]

For a person, the hierarchy extends farther. Organs regulate the supply available to their cells. People secure food, and communities teach children how to get it. These arrangements produce different sorts of continuities. A person can learn a practice without copying a genome, and a workshop can train a replacement worker without producing a replacement workshop. Whenever we ask what reproduces, it pays to identify the next individual that could do the same work on its own terms.

Walter put a mirror in front of the tortoise. The interaction of the lamp and the eye made the machine move before its reflection. A patch of white could do much the same. It had found the consequence of its own behavior in the world, but how could anyone tell whether it knew that this consequence was its own? A naturalist who made a rule about his own memory gives us a better place to look.
