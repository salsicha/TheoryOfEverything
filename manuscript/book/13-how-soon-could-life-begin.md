# Chapter 13 — Did Life Begin as Soon as Earth Could Support It?

*Part IV · Paradox*

In Western Australia a crystal survived the destruction of the rock in which it formed. Rivers carried it away. It ended up among other grains in the Jack Hills. Simon Wilde and his colleagues found that part of this zircon was about 4.4 billion years old. The team could extract two pieces of information from the same grain. Uranium and its decay products recorded the time since the crystal formed. Its oxygen isotopes suggested that material in its ancestry had interacted with liquid water at low temperature. A grain that had outlasted a landscape still contained evidence that water had touched it.[1]

Liquid water on a young planet sets an early date for one condition life needed. An ocean could still lose its first chemical products to an impact. The stronger possibility is that Earth spent geological ages becoming habitable and became inhabited in the first interval when a lineage could survive. Once water, raw material and a lasting source of chemical energy came together, the wait for life would have been measured by reactions and generations. How could so long a preparation end in so short a change?[1][2]

Oleg Abramov and his colleagues tried to date a particular starting line. For how long after Earth formed would repeated impacts have interrupted the survival of the molecules that might take part in an RNA world? In a study published in 2026 they combined a history of impacts with a thermal model. They identified favorable conditions for persistent prebiotic RNA chemistry around 4.33 billion years ago. Cool conditions at one site between strikes, lasting relief from disruption and the first successful line of descent are three different events. The team calculated a date for the conditions. It did not find a molecule of that age.[2]

A team led by Edmund Moody ran the argument backward from things alive now. Genes inherited in common by very distant branches of life can be compared. By anchoring portions of their history to independently dated events and making assumptions about rates of change, the team estimated that the last universal common ancestor lived roughly 4.2 billion years ago. One of its reported intervals ran from 4.33 to 4.09 billion years. This was already a population with a history. Lineages that died out without leaving living descendants disappear from this particular reckoning. In Western Australia again, Tara Djokic and colleagues have described evidence for microbial communities at hot springs about 3.48 billion years ago. Those rocks attest to occupation of a particular environment. Its first settlers could already have had an immensely long ancestry.[3]

The zircon, the thermal model and the family tree put water, favorable conditions and an already evolved population on the young Earth. It is tempting to subtract the two central estimates and give life 130 million years to get started. At the older end of that interval is a temperature calculation. At the younger end is a population that already had ancestors. Neither clock dates the first generation. The dates leave room for life to have taken the first opening. The argument that it did turns on what happened inside that opening.[1][2][3]

## When reactions begin to leave descendants

A calculation that builds a present-day cell by drawing its entire sequence from a sack of letters gives a wait longer than the age of Earth. At a vent, the next reaction starts with what the last one left. The rock supplies some compounds and excludes others. Its pores bring molecules together and retain some of the products. These are the material and Platonic constraints we met at Lost City. A bond that will form and a bond that never will have different consequences long before there is a cell to inherit either.[4]

Wim Hordijk, Mike Steel and Stuart Kauffman asked what adding to that inventory could do. In one model, chains could contain two kinds of building block. The researchers fixed the probability that any given chain could catalyze a given reaction, then allowed successively longer chains. They looked for a set whose members could be made from the starting materials and whose reactions all had catalysts available within the set or its food supply. Allow one more position in a chain and the number of possible chains of that length doubles. In one series of simulations, sets of mutually supporting reactions were likely by the time the allowed length reached thirteen. The important result was a property of the whole collection. New combinations could supply a catalyst that several otherwise incomplete routes had lacked.[4]

The model counts workable sets. In a stream of real water a set also has to get started and outrun its losses. A catalyst can survive one flushing of a pore and vanish in the next. When a network spends some of the stream’s energy replacing its own catalysts, it can outlast the molecules with which it started. For heredity there is one further crossing. An arrangement must pass to descendants capable of doing the same work, with differences that can themselves be inherited. If the average number of viable successors per founding unit stays below one, successive generations dwindle on average. Above one, some families can escape extinction and carry their chemistry into other pores. The first such family could still fit inside a pore. What Earth had gained was a line of descent on which natural selection could act.[4]

At Scripps, Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce put a clock on part of that transition. Two RNA catalysts helped make one another. In the system they studied the population could double in about an hour. When several pairs competed for a common supply, new combinations arose and some displaced their parents. A difference in how well a combination used the available material could now have descendants of its own. The researchers supplied the starting catalysts. Their hour measures amplification in their apparatus. Early Earth also had to make the first pair, or some quite different chemistry that served the same purpose.[4]

Why expect even the first workable combination to turn up promptly? A field of mineral pores gave the same restricted chemistry many starts. Each additional kind of molecule could supply a missing reaction for several sets at once. Each additional pore offered another place where one set might begin operating before a current dispersed it. If attempts are independent and roughly steady, doubling the number of equally well supplied pores halves the expected wait for the first successful start. A common impact can cut all those attempts short. Cooling and the retreat of frequent impacts changed both how many places qualified and how long each one had.[2][4]

A family that barely replaces itself can take many generations to grow. A combination that almost never forms can leave one pore empty for ages. Across a field of well-supplied pores, chemistry would have had many chances to start at once. For life to have taken hold in the first habitable interval, the wait for one start that left descendants had to be short compared with the life of the habitat. Once its descendants occupied fresh surfaces, some of the energy that would have left the vent in unreacted compounds could pass through living chemistry. A date for the founding and a measurement of what flowed out of the vent would tell us different things.[2][3][4]

## One season on Daphne Major

On the island of Daphne Major there is a case in which biologists actually knew which animals were present before the change. Peter Boag and Peter Grant had measurements of Darwin’s finches. During the drought of 1977 food grew scarce. Among the seeds that remained were large, hard ones. Large birds, in particular males with large beaks, survived disproportionately well. A change in the distribution of beak sizes could occur in a season of hunger. No finch had to wait for an appropriate beak to turn up by chance that year. Individuals with different beaks had already hatched. The drought changed what each kind could eat.[5]

On Daphne Major the investigators could put times to both parts of a change. The birds with large beaks were already there when the supply of soft seeds failed. The distribution of survivors shifted in a single drought. On the early Earth the corresponding interval starts when a habitat can support a lineage and ends when one establishes. The evidence for either date is much coarser than a fieldworker’s record of a bad season.[1][2][3][5]

Someone who needs billions of years of evolution to look at a zircon is more likely to find herself on a world where life started early. David Kipping allowed for that bias. In 2025 he asked how the estimate of an already living ancestor about 4.2 billion years ago changed the odds. With habitability beginning about 4.4 billion years ago, his calculation favored fast over slow abiogenesis by roughly thirteen to one. In that comparison “fast” is measured against a planet’s habitable lifetime. The result gives a quantitative reason to take a rapid origin seriously. Whether the first lineage appeared in the very first suitable interval is a finer question than those dates resolve.[1][3][6]

The rocks can put the first-window explanation at risk. Date an enduring supply of water, building material and chemical energy independently of any trace of life. Estimate how long it offered the reactions a chance to leave descendants. A well-preserved record of habitable sites that remained sterile far longer than the chemistry required would put a second long wait after Earth became habitable. A trace of life within the first such interval would put the delay where the Jack Hills grain first led us to look, in the making of a habitable planet.[1][2][3][4][6]
