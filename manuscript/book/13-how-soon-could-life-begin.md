# Chapter 13 — How Soon Could Life Begin?

*Part IV · Paradox*

In Western Australia a crystal survived the destruction of the rock in which it formed. Rivers carried it away. It ended up among other grains in the Jack Hills. Simon Wilde and his colleagues found that part of this zircon was about 4.4 billion years old. The team could extract two pieces of information from the same grain. Uranium and its decay products recorded the time since the crystal formed. Its oxygen isotopes suggested that material in its ancestry had interacted with liquid water at low temperature. A grain that had outlasted a landscape still contained evidence that water had touched it.[1]

I argue that life emerged on Earth as soon as it could. Most of the waiting went into making a place where chemistry could keep what it had made. A hot surface can cool enough for water to collect and still suffer impacts that destroy a succession of fragile products. A single cool afternoon on the young Earth would scarcely help the next afternoon's chemistry. The relevant opening was the first place that remained cool and supplied with chemical energy long enough for products from one round to take part in the next. The zircon puts water into this history. It leaves the duration of that first opening to be found.[1]

Oleg Abramov and his colleagues tried to date a particular starting line. For how long after Earth formed would repeated impacts have interrupted the survival of the molecules that might take part in an RNA world? In a study published in 2026 they combined a history of impacts with a thermal model. They identified favorable conditions for persistent prebiotic RNA chemistry around 4.33 billion years ago. Cool conditions at one site between strikes, lasting relief from disruption and the first successful line of descent are three different events. The team calculated a date for the conditions. It did not find a molecule of that age.[2]

A team led by Edmund Moody ran the argument backward from things alive now. Genes inherited in common by very distant branches of life can be compared. By anchoring portions of their history to independently dated events and making assumptions about rates of change, the team estimated that the last universal common ancestor lived roughly 4.2 billion years ago. One of its reported intervals ran from 4.33 to 4.09 billion years. This was already a population with a history. Lineages that died out without leaving living descendants disappear from this particular reckoning. In Western Australia again, Tara Djokic and colleagues have described evidence for microbial communities at hot springs about 3.48 billion years ago. Those rocks attest to occupation of a particular environment. Its first settlers could already have had an immensely long ancestry.[3]

Put the three dates in order. A rock records water early. A calculation allows fragile chemistry to persist while Earth is still young. The family tree points back to a population that had already acquired a considerable inheritance by about 4.2 billion years ago. That order is what we would expect if cooling took up most of the delay and life occupied the first enduring opening. Subtracting the two model dates would yield 130 million years between unlike events. Neither endpoint is a sighting of the first living thing. The historical claim is stronger than these clocks can establish on their own.[1][2][3]

## What a cell would have to find

Against what expectation is it rapid? A calculation that builds a present-day cell by drawing its entire sequence from a sack of letters gives a wait longer than any plausible life of Earth. The calculation has thrown away the chemistry before it begins. Certain bonds form more readily than others. A mineral surface can bring reactants together. A difference in chemical potential can drive a reaction that would otherwise be scarce. A product that stays in a pore changes what can react there next. Reusing an intermediate turns a calculation about guessing many successive parts afresh into one about what that intermediate makes accessible. These are the material and Platonic constraints we met at Lost City. Some can be read off a reaction diagram before anyone has detected the intermediate itself.[4]

Follow two parcels with the same chemical supply into pores at the same temperature and flow. A parcel may leave with most of its hydrogen still available. A mineral may already use up much of that supply. A third route opens if a reaction makes a catalyst that sticks to the wall. The next parcel encounters a surface the first one helped to build. When catalysts are replaced faster than they are swept away or destroyed, many parcels can react during the life of the same pore. A lineage that makes more catalysts and occupies fresh surfaces can draw on a larger share of the continuing flow. Where the mineral reaction already exhausts the feed, copying has a cost and little left to gain. The largest sustained rate could belong to the mineral there.[4]

Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce observed part of this competition in the laboratory. Pairs of RNA catalysts made more of one another. When different pairs shared a supply, new combinations arose and some came to dominate. Which combination prospered depended on what else was consuming the same materials. The researchers began with catalysts designed for the task. Early Earth had to produce its first competent chemistry. The result shows what can happen *after* that difficult step and why measuring a candidate on its own can give the wrong forecast.[4]

Why expect the first enduring habitat to have produced life quickly? Each working pore offered repeated opportunities. To estimate a waiting time, we need two independent numbers. How often did the available chemistry make a combination capable of taking the next step? What fraction of those combinations lasted long enough to leave descendants? If those events were rare and independent at a steady rate, doubling the number of equally well supplied pores would halve the expected wait for the start of a successful lineage. Its first division would still take as long. An impact that emptied all the pores together would impose a common deadline for that division.[2][4]

My claim is that once Earth had enough connected, supplied pores, their combined rate of producing a lasting lineage was high, and living populations sustained more chemical conversion than minerals and unassisted reactions could sustain on the same feed. Dating a lineage tests the timing. Measuring the chemicals entering and leaving, including what remained stored in new material, tests the energetic reason offered for it.[2][3][4]

## One season on Daphne Major

On the island of Daphne Major there is a case in which biologists actually knew which animals were present before the change. Peter Boag and Peter Grant had measurements of Darwin’s finches. During the drought of 1977 food grew scarce. Among the seeds that remained were large, hard ones. Large birds, in particular males with large beaks, survived disproportionately well. A change in the distribution of beak sizes could occur in a season of hunger. No finch had to wait for an appropriate beak to turn up by chance that year. Individuals with different beaks had already hatched. The drought changed what each kind could eat.[5]

The two histories belong on the same page for a particular reason. The finch researchers could separate the appearance of a useful difference from the moment the difference paid. The dates for earliest life give us neither event with anything like that precision. But there too we must allow time for the production of the ingredients and then ask how fast events proceeded once a productive route became available. A swift response can be the last part of a long preparation. It can also conceal failures on other planets where an apparently similar preparation never led to a living thing.

There is one more bias. An observer who needs billions of years of subsequent evolution is more likely to find herself on a planet where life got going early. David Kipping calculated how Earth’s chronology changes the odds of a fast origin when the time needed for observers counts too. Under his assumptions the early start favors rapid origins. A single inhabited planet offers limited leverage. An independent origin elsewhere would do more, especially if we could measure when suitable conditions first arose there.[6]

The prediction concerns the interval between two events one could, in principle, date separately. Use rocks and reaction rates to establish when a habitat could retain the intermediates through successive cycles, sustain their supply and support copying faster than loss. Use an independent record to look for life. I expect the first persistent lineage in the first interval long enough for those steps to finish. A well-preserved history of habitable settings with the requisite flows and chemistry persisting for geological ages before life began would count against that expectation. The argument about early Earth is that the planet took time to cool and life took the first opening it got. How early could another planet have offered one?
