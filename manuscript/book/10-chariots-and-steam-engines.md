# Chapter 10 — Chariots and Steam Engines

At Knossos a clerk scratched twenty-two pairs of new wheels into clay, then entered the odd wheel left over. He recorded their supplier as *ko-ki-da*. Another tablet records a delivery going the other way. Triopas, as the museum renders his name, received a chariot on wheels, a horse and a cuirass. The palace could give him an outfit ready to use.[1]

A horse kept eating when a broken wheel took its chariot out of service. The spare in the clerk’s account could get the team moving again. The tablet preserves something a wheel in a grave seldom can. Someone knew what the store held and could issue its contents to the next driver.[1]

A light vehicle could carry people at the speed of a horse team across suitable ground. Its fuel grew on land that had to feed the animals between journeys too. A palace collecting produce from many settlements could keep horses and people to care for them. It could also decide who got to travel that fast. The clerk wrote down Triopas’s name.[1]

Horse-drawn vehicles left traces in steppe graves early in the second millennium BCE. The Cretan tablets come from a later palace economy. The finds trace the spread of animals, equipment and knowledge through many hands. A team could go only where the wheels could travel and the horses could feed at the other end.[2]

## Coal below the Irish Sea

At Saltom on the English coast, the surviving stonework records the circle the horses walked. Carlisle Spedding began sinking the pit in 1729. Beneath the Irish Sea he had to lift both water and coal. Walking around a pole, the horses turned a drum to wind up the coal. The steam engine’s beam went up and down, a motion suited to the pump rods. It could keep the water down while miners filled the next load for the horses to wind.[3]

The trouble started when water entered faster than a mine owner could afford to lift it. Another man or horse could have been hired. Each needed food or wages whatever the price the coal fetched. Small coal of little value to a distant buyer lay close to the boilers. Once an owner could afford the engine and its first firing, a portion of each load could pay, in fuel, for bringing up the next. An engine that wasted most of the heat in its coal still left the mine owner more coal to sell than he had been able to reach before.[3][4]

An owner could burn a heap of coal beside the shaft. Burning a share under the pump instead opened a seam that could feed household fires for years, provided the extra coal reached buyers and displaced little output from other pits. At Saltom the owners built a harbor beside the shaft. Storms damaged it. The miners sent coal they had just raised back underground, took it through the cliff to Ravenhill, wound it up a second shaft and sent it by wagonway to Whitehaven.[3]

Henry Beighton’s 1717 drawing of the engine at Griff in Warwickshire shows a difficulty inside the cylinder. Steam entered beneath the piston. A jet of cold water condensed it, lowering the pressure so the atmosphere could push the piston down. The next charge of steam met metal the last stroke had cooled. Insulation around the cylinder would still leave cold water striking its walls. Run a pipe to a separate cold vessel and the steam can condense while the cylinder stays hot.[4]

James Watt’s 1769 patent opens with those two requirements. Keep the cylinder as hot as the steam entering it and the separate vessel cold. The older machine suggested both the design and a test a builder could name in advance. Lift the same weight of water through the same height and compare the fuel bills. A lower bill might make a deeper seam worth pumping. To learn whether the district burned more fuel, the owner would have to follow the coal out of the gate.[3][4]

## Three hundred carts

In October 1877 Tang Tingshu went ashore at Lutai on his way to inspect the Kaiping coalfield. His steamer had brought him from Tianjin. Upstream the river twisted under too many bridges to make a good freight route. At the pits, miners told him of seams disappearing into mud and water. Coal worth 2.7 taels of silver a ton at the pit mouth cost another 2.2 taels to cart to Lutai. Tang reckoned he would need three hundred large carts a day. He could not get them.[5]

The Kaiping miners had as much reason to want dry seams as the men at Saltom. Shallow workings still paid for men’s labor. An engine pumping deeper workings would cost money every day while the extra coal waited for carts. This was a *metastable* arrangement. A bigger operation might pay its way once built, yet buying a pump alone could ruin the first man to try it. With a railway as well as machinery, Tang reckoned he could land coal in Shanghai for about four taels a ton.[5]

Tang’s figures let a reader forecast part of his investment. A railway alone could carry away the shallow output while the deep seams stayed wet. A pump alone would fill the yard. To sell his projected tonnage Tang needed drainage and cheap bulk freight together. Which he could build first also depended on what the authorities would allow.[3][4][5]

Officials objected to the longer line. Tang got a canal and a short horse railway. Engineers laid light rails but built bridges strong enough for locomotives. Permission for locomotives came sooner than expected, and the light rails proved a poor saving. In 1883 a visiting American consul saw steam pumps below ground, men working the softer rock by hand, and a replacement winding engine under construction in the company shops. The account explains why this Chinese operator kept some hand labor and when he invested in steam. Kaiping in 1877 leaves open what other operators could have afforded in 1712.[4][5]

Steam had also reached China aboard vessels backed by British state and commercial power during the First Opium War. Treaties imposed after a war helped determine who could bring machinery where. At Kaiping a Chinese operator chose and financed the equipment on different terms. The steamer could deliver Tang to Lutai. It took a route beyond Lutai to make an engine pay for itself at the mine.[5][6]

The clerk at Knossos kept an odd wheel in his count. At Kaiping the company could build a winding engine to replace the one it had worn out. Both records let us see what was ready for a second journey.[1][5]

At either mine, the coal seam contained chemical free energy before the pump arrived. Water, fuel for the boiler, workers who could repair the engine and a route for selling the coal determined whether a new arrangement could reach it. A chariot needed horses fed and wheels replaced just as a steam engine needed its coal brought up. A machine could spread only as fast as people could assemble the conditions for using it.[1][3][5]
