# Chapter 8 — Chariots and Steam Engines

*Part III · Technology*

The clerk at Knossos had twenty-two pairs of new wheels to enter, and one wheel left over. He scratched the extra wheel into the clay. The man who had supplied the consignment went into the account as *ko-ki-da*. On another tablet a scribe recorded a delivery going the other way. A man whose name the museum renders as Triopas received a chariot on wheels, a horse and a cuirass. One man disappears from the record after he delivers the wheels. The other appears when someone issues him the equipment. Between them stood an institution that could collect workshop output in pairs and hand out a vehicle ready to use.[1]

Archaeologists who recover an axle or the impression of a wheel can reconstruct some of what a carriage did. The tablet records something harder to recover from a grave. Someone needed to know how many wheels the palace had. A horse standing idle kept eating. An outfit that lost a wheel represented an investment in animals, equipment and people that could no longer travel together. The clerk’s odd wheel had value even if nobody had occasion to use it that day. A workshop, a store, a record and a way of getting a spare to its owner extended the useful life of every vehicle they served.[1]

Horses had supplied muscle before there were chariots. What a light wheeled vehicle offered was a way to carry people at the speed of a team across suitable ground. The energy for the journey came from plants. The cost included land that could feed the horses between journeys and men and women whose labor could be spared to care for them. A polity collecting produce from many settlements could shoulder these costs. Possession of horses and vehicles in turn gave some people a reach few of their subjects could match. It matters who got the vehicle. The clerk recorded Triopas’s name.

Horse-drawn vehicles left traces in steppe graves early in the second millennium BCE. The Cretan accounts belong to a later palace world. Between the two lies enough time for knowledge, animals and equipment to have passed through many hands. A map of finds shows where evidence survives. It cannot give us a single inventor, much less credit every victory of every chariot-owning king to the chariot. Ground a horse could cross, a road a wheel could take and a place to feed animals at the other end all restricted where ownership could become power. The technology that spread included arrangements for meeting those conditions.[2]

## Coal below the Irish Sea

At Saltom, on the English coast, the horse that outlasted the arrival of the steam engine had an advantage easy to see from the surviving stonework. It walked in a circle. Carlisle Spedding, who began sinking the pit in 1729, had to raise coal and water from beneath the Irish Sea. Horses walking around a pole turned a drum and wound up the coal. The beam on the new engine went up and down. That motion suited the pump rods. The engine could keep pumping while men filled the next load. For much of the work these were complementary machines. A better pump gave the horses more to wind.[3]

The trouble started when water entered faster than a mine owner could afford to lift it. Another man or horse could have been hired. Each needed food or wages whatever the price the coal fetched. Small coal of little value to a distant buyer lay close to the boilers. Once an owner could afford the engine and its first firing, a portion of each load could pay, in fuel, for bringing up the next. An engine that wasted most of the heat in its coal still left the mine owner more coal to sell than he had been able to reach before.[3][4]

How could a steam engine get coal burned faster? A heap beside the shaft could burn where it lay. Put part of it under the boiler, drain a seam, and there could be fires in hundreds of homes for years to come. The difference would show up in the fuel burned across the district over the life of the mine. If they merely replaced fires fed from a neighboring pit, the ledger at Saltom would overstate the gain for the district. New customers and a way to reach them mattered. The owners built a little harbour beside the shaft. Storms damaged it. The men started sending the coal they had just raised back underground. They took it through the cliff to Ravenhill, wound it up a second shaft and sent it along a wagonway to Whitehaven. The detour in the prologue bought the extra coal a market.[3]

The engine itself contained a second contradiction. A drawing Henry Beighton published in 1717 shows the engine at Griff in Warwickshire. On each cycle the Newcomen engine admitted steam beneath the piston. A jet of cold water condensed it there, lowering the pressure so the atmosphere could push the piston down. The next admission of steam met metal the last stroke had just cooled. An engineer could make a prediction from that sequence. Insulating the cylinder would keep heat from escaping through its walls. It would still leave cold water striking the inside. Could the steam be condensed somewhere else? A pipe to a second, cold vessel would let the cylinder stay hot and still give the piston the pressure drop it needed.[4]

The first two provisions of James Watt’s 1769 patent lay out those two temperatures. The cylinder should stay as hot as the steam entering it. The separate vessel should stay cold. An engineer who had never seen Watt’s design could have deduced that arrangement by following a charge of steam through the older one. The calculation would also have picked a measure of success in advance. Lift the same weight of water through the same height and compare the fuel bills. A lower bill could make deeper seams worth pumping. To learn whether the district now burned more coal, an owner would have to follow the coal out of the gate.[3][4]

## Three hundred carts

Why did this arrangement first find so many buyers in England? In October 1877 Tang Tingshu left Tianjin by steamer to inspect the Kaiping coalfield. He went ashore at Lutai. The river farther on twisted under so many bridges that it would make a poor freight route. In the pits the miners told him about coal seams that disappeared into mud and water. Tang did the account. Coal worth 2.7 taels of silver a ton at the pit mouth cost another 2.2 taels just to cart it to Lutai. His projected output would need three hundred large carts a day. He could not get them.[5]

Tang had found a Chinese mine at which flooding also made an engine desirable. He had found a reason to keep paying people to work shallow seams. A new pump would cost its owner money every day while the extra coal waited for carts. In the language of physics the older arrangement was *metastable*. A larger system might support itself once built. Changing just one piece could impoverish the first person to try. Tang wanted a railway as well as machinery. He reckoned that with rail he could land a ton in Shanghai for about four taels.[5]

Tang’s figures let us make a forecast before learning what he built. Follow a ton of coal from the seam to the buyer. The daily deliveries can rise only as far as drainage, winding, freight and sales will all permit. A pump alone could leave coal piled beside the shaft. Freight alone could take away the shallow output while the deeper seams stayed wet. To sell the projected tonnage the company needed drainage and a cheap bulk route together. The quickest way to start hauling and the route with the greatest eventual capacity might be different. Tang wanted a railway. Permission to build it was one of the constraints on what could go first.[3][4][5]

What he got at first was a canal and a short horse railway. Officials had objected to his longer line. The engineers laid light rails and built bridges strong enough for locomotives. When permission came sooner than expected, they had reason to regret saving money on the rails. By 1883 the American consul who visited could see steam pumps below, men working the softer rock by hand, and the replacement for a winding engine under construction in the company shops. The mine now had people who could make its next machine. Kaiping tells us why particular Chinese operators employed human labor for as long as they did and how that choice changed. An account from 1877 cannot settle what every coalfield in China could have done in 1712.[4][5]

Steam also travelled to China on vessels backed by British state and commercial power during the First Opium War. The terms on which people encountered a technology could include a treaty imposed on them. To say that an assembly conquered the world can obscure both the people who imposed those terms and the people who chose, financed and altered machinery for purposes of their own. Tang had taken a steamer on the first leg of his survey. It was the journey he made after getting off it that determined what steam could do for his coalfield.[6]

At Knossos a clerk made sure the odd wheel would still be in the count after the wheel maker had gone. At Kaiping there was a shop where someone could build the next winding engine. The reach of either technology depended on such people. Their work explains how a vehicle or an engine could continue to spread long after its first owner had used it up.
