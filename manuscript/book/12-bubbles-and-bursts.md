# Chapter 12 — Bubbles and Bursts

On April 2, 2002, Cogent Communications bought part of PSINet out of bankruptcy. The price was $9.5 million in cash, with certain liabilities assumed. The sale included customers’ contracts and the right to exchange traffic with other networks. Cogent also took on obligations to pay for circuits and space in other companies’ buildings. Customers still expected their packets to reach the other end. A bankruptcy judge could approve the transfer of the business. Keeping those connections working took equipment, power and agreements with the people at the far end of the wires.[1]

Cogent described two ways of reaching a customer. For its own network, it had bought long-term rights to unlit strands of glass between cities and around metropolitan rings. The industry called those strands *dark fiber*. Cogent installed the equipment to send light through them. Some of the customers it acquired from PSINet sat beyond that network. To reach them it paid other carriers for working circuits. Both customers could have bought a service called internet access. They put quite different costs on the provider.[1]

The distinction matters to the history of the dot-com bubble. Investors could lose the money they had put into a network while its cable remained in the ground. A successor with rights to use a strand could connect two cities without raising money to dig that route again. A strand that bypassed the customer’s town offered no such saving. Even where the route was right, someone still had to light the fiber, arrange an exchange of traffic and pay to connect the building.[1][2]

## What the boom left behind

An entrepreneur setting out before the construction boom would have had to reckon with the cost and delay of the missing routes. After the boom, another entrepreneur could rearrange pieces laid down by firms with quite different customers in mind. Two networks in the same building could begin exchanging traffic. A service in one city could reach readers in another at the cost of sending more data along an existing route. Traffic, the price of capacity and the cost of the last connection still decided whether the change was worth making.[1][2]

This is the claim about *conformational entropy space*. In chemistry, the conformations available to a molecule are physical arrangements with probabilities that depend on its surroundings. A network also admits different arrangements of existing parts. Here the arrangements include lit and unlit routes, exchange points, servers and the contracts that let one operator reach another. The analogy identifies something one could count after specifying the equipment, demand and budget. It supplies no thermodynamic entropy in joules per kelvin for a list of business opportunities.[2][3]

## What a click could pay for

Google’s early advertising offers a second date. Advertisers could buy text ads through its self-service system in 2000. At first they paid for the number of times their advertisements appeared. In the first quarter of 2002 Google began offering AdWords on a cost-per-click basis. A reader might receive a page of search results, read it and leave. An advertiser paying by the click owed money when the reader followed the advertised link. Google could record that event and bill for it.[4]

A request for a page used bandwidth and server time. The click had value because an advertiser expected some clicks to become sales. An auction gave different advertisers a way to say what reaching a particular audience was worth to them. Later, a publisher could give Google space on a page and receive a share of the advertising fees. The same search and billing equipment could finance the next page served to a reader. In its public offering documents Google described nearly all its net revenue as coming from advertising.[4]

Human attention is scarce. A search reveals something about what a person wants at the moment an advertiser can reach them. The gap between the expected value of that contact and the cost of delivering it is what the book calls *free energy in attention space*. Electricity supplies the physical free energy in the server. Revenue pays for more machines and more electricity. Clicks, dollars and kilowatt-hours belong in separate columns if we want to follow how one changed the others.[1][4]

The audience also helps determine what the network will carry. A reply gives another reader a reason to open the page. Repeated across millions of exchanges, decisions about what to say become signals used to rank the next reader’s feed. The resulting order changes which posts a writer can expect anyone to see. That is downward causation at the level of a medium. Each round of communication helps make the conditions for the next, and advertising puts a price on keeping the rounds coming.[4][5]

By 2025 the two ends of that circuit appeared in the same company’s accounts. Meta earned most of its revenue from advertising. During the year it spent $69.69 billion on property and equipment, including servers, data centers and network infrastructure. The accounts cover several uses, AI among them. A sale of access to someone’s attention can now help finance a building where models learn from the language people have put online. The light in the fiber, the money for the building and the sentences available for training came from different sources. They had to arrive together for that use to pay.[6]

Cogent’s accounts document cheaper ways to assemble some routes. Google’s accounts document a way to earn money from delivering an advertisement. The records do not identify a particular search as having traveled over a particular strand of former bubble infrastructure. What connects the histories is a change in what an enterprise had to build for itself and what it could earn once it had built the rest. A cable laid for customers who never came could serve a customer its first owner had never considered. Whether a building full of computers can leave the same kind of inheritance will depend on which parts survive its first business.
