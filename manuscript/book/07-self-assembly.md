# Chapter 7 — Self Assembly

Bartosz Grzybowski, Howard Stone and George Whitesides floated little magnetized disks on liquid and set a magnet turning beneath them. The disks spun. They also moved across the surface and settled into patterns, holding their distance from one another while the liquid around them kept moving. The researchers could change the pattern by changing the speed of the magnet. They had supplied one drive to the whole dish. No one had to steer the individual disks into position.[1]

Each disk changed the conditions its neighbors encountered. The magnet drew the disks toward the center of the apparatus. Their spinning stirred the liquid, giving rise to a repulsion between neighbors. Where these effects balanced, the disks could keep a working distance even while the fluid dissipated energy. Measuring that distance as the drive changed gave the researchers more to explain than a photograph of the finished pattern.[1]

## Which arrangements does a drive favor?

Nikolai Perunov, Robert Marsland and Jeremy England asked what could bias the rearrangement of matter under repeated driving. A system can reach the same final arrangement by several histories. Along those histories it can absorb different amounts of work from the drive and release different amounts of heat. The likelihood of undoing a change also matters. In their calculations, different drives could favor different outcomes. This is the point of *dissipative adaptation*. To predict what accumulates, one needs to know both how a configuration forms under the drive and how readily it comes apart.[2]

Alfred Lotka asked a related question about living things. Where available energy goes unused, an organism that can tap it can gain an advantage. Howard Odum and Richard Pinkerton later considered the rate at which a system delivers useful work. Run some devices very gently and little energy is wasted on each turn, yet very little work gets done per hour. Run them close to a standstill under a heavy load and useful output again falls. The *maximum power principle* directs attention to the operating point at which work delivered per unit time peaks.[3]

A watt at a useful load and a watt lost to friction both appear on an energy account. Only one of them drives that load. To compare two routes by entropy production we need to follow both flows, including where their heat ends up and at what temperature. A machine that delivers the most power to a pump today could be tapping a reservoir that empties tomorrow. The book’s stronger claim concerns the fastest route to the highest rate the whole arrangement can sustain.[3]

## What the finished structure does to its parts

Denis Noble found a concrete meaning for *downward causation* in models of the heartbeat. The behavior of channels in a cell membrane depends on the voltage across it. The flow of ions through the channels helps produce that very voltage. Hold the voltage fixed from outside and one can study what the channels do at a chosen value. Let it vary again and the state of the cell as a whole changes what each channel does next. The influence runs both ways through physical interactions one can measure.[4]

The wall of a model cell in the previous chapter supplies a simple *work-constraint cycle*. An intact wall holds a catalyst near its supply. The reactions it makes possible can produce more wall material. The existing constraint directs a flow, and some of the resulting work renews the constraint. In the rotating dish the experimenter pays for the motion of the magnet. The disks arrange themselves, but spinning them has yet to build a motor that will keep them spinning.[1][4]

Adrian Bejan’s *constructal law* asks how the shape of a system carrying a current can change to improve access to that current. A broad channel carries a long-distance flow with little resistance. Smaller branches reach sites the broad channel would never visit. With a fixed amount of material, spending it on the trunk leaves less for the branches. The resulting prediction concerns geometry under a stated limit. One can compare the flow through two arrangements using the same material and the same driving difference.[5]

## What becomes possible next

Assembly theory asks how many joining steps an object requires when a part already constructed can be used again. Finding many copies of a complicated object adds another question about the history that kept producing it. Stuart Kauffman’s *adjacent possible* looks forward from that point. Once a part exists in quantity, some arrangements become reachable in a step that previously required a whole sequence. A working membrane already made by a parent changes what its daughter has to build for herself.[6]

Together these accounts suggest a way to predict change before seeing its result. Begin with two structures reachable from the same parts. Ask which one can form before the drive changes, which one survives disturbances, and how much of the available gradient each can keep using. Then ask what new part or constraint each would leave behind. The rotating disks let an investigator vary the drive and predict a new spacing. For a cell, the harder prediction is whether any of the work the structure does will make another structure able to do it again.[1][2][4][6]
