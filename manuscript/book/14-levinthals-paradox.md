# Chapter 14 — Levinthal’s paradox

Christian Anfinsen and his colleagues unfolded ribonuclease, an enzyme that cuts RNA. They broke four bonds that held the folded chain together. Every amino acid was still present, in the same order. Given RNA, the chain could no longer cut it.[1]

Eight sites on the chain could pair to form the four bonds. The first site has seven possible partners. The first site left over has five, the next has three and the last has one. That gives 105 pairings. The team let the bonds form again in a solution that still favored an unfolded chain. The resulting mixture recovered about one percent of the original activity.[1]

When the solution allowed the chain to fold as the bonds formed, the enzyme recovered its ability to cut RNA. Even a scrambled chain could regain activity if its bonds could exchange partners under conditions favoring the native fold. Before the fourth bond closed, the sequence and the surrounding water had already favored some pairings.[1]

## What did the count leave out?

There are many more ways to bend the backbone than to pair those eight sites. In 1968 Cyrus Levinthal asked how proteins could reach their working shapes in the time they took. For an illustrative count, give a chain of a hundred units three settings at each unit. That gives about 5 × 10⁴⁷ arrangements. Even at a trillion trials a second, inspecting them all would take about 1.6 × 10²⁸ years. That figure is a search time only for a chain obliged to inspect complete shapes one after another.[2]

A bend can bring two parts of a chain into contact. If they hold, other sites come within reach. Burying some groups in the interior changes the free energy of the surrounding water as well as the chain. Several partial folds can lead to the same contacts. Some contacts trap the chain until they break. A drawing gives each arrangement one place on the page. A molecule spends much longer in some arrangements than in others.[3]

Robert Zwanzig, Attila Szabo and Biman Bagchi modeled a chain that could make wrong turns. Even a modest energetic preference for local structure that fitted the final fold cut the predicted search time. This is a folding *funnel*. Several routes approach the same small set of states. Which contacts really help a given protein, and which trap it, have to be worked out for that sequence in its surroundings.[3]

## The time it takes to cross

Hoi Sung Chung and his colleagues attached fluorescent dyes to individual proteins. When two parts of a chain approached, the balance of light from the dyes shifted. From the arrival times of the photons the team could estimate how long a molecule waited and how long it spent crossing into the folded state once a successful crossing began.[4]

For a small protein domain called WW, the folding rate corresponded to a wait of about a hundred microseconds. For GB1, under its test conditions, the wait was about a second. Yet the crossing itself took about two microseconds for WW, and the team put an upper bound of ten microseconds on the crossing for GB1. The slower protein spent most of its second on the starting side.[4]

Waiting for a habitat and founding a lineage inside it likewise take separate clocks, a distinction the next chapter follows from Earth to other stars. Counting possible shapes cannot date the origin of life. Nor does a short crossing in a laboratory protein tell us how long the first living chemistry spent waiting for one.[3][4]

Anfinsen’s enzyme could cut one strand of RNA, release the products and take up another. If the chain wore out in its tube, the reaction lost a catalyst. A cell with access to the same feed could spend some of it replacing the worn chain. Its daughters could carry the same capacity into fresh fluid. Folding shortens the time it takes to get a working surface. Replacement and division determine how many working surfaces keep drawing on a gradient after the first one is gone. A bare mineral surface exposed to the same current can catalyze reactions too.[1][4][5]

If life sustains the highest accessible rate of entropy production, its advantage over bare mineral should survive a comparison at the same inflow. That requires counting the energy in new membranes, replacement catalysts, cells and unspent products carried out of the habitat. A thriving population could export much of the chemical energy it took in. If the bare surface dissipates more of the same supply in the same time, the proposed advantage of life fails the test. The dyes told Chung when a molecule folded. What leaves the habitat would tell us how much of the gradient living chemistry dispersed.[3][4][5]
