# Chapter 2 — From Hydrogen to Gold

*Part I · Physics*

Ian Roederer and his colleagues found gold in the light of a star. In the ultraviolet spectrum of HD 222925 an absorption line appeared where gold atoms should remove light. By matching the line to laboratory data, the team could estimate how much gold the star’s atmosphere contained. The star had inherited a sample of the gas from which it formed. What chain of events could give a cloud of gas gold to pass on?[1]

Start with hydrogen. The simplest nucleus is one proton. An electron passing it feels an electrical attraction. It can speed up on the approach and slow down again on the way out. To stay bound, the pair has to lose energy. A photon can carry it away. Quantum confinement sets how close the electron can settle. Squeezing its range of positions eventually costs more kinetic energy than it earns in electrical binding. The resulting atom has a characteristic size and a definite binding energy. It takes 13.6 electron volts to free an electron from hydrogen’s ground state.[2]

For a long time the young universe undid this work about as fast as it could do it. Radiation could strip newly bound electrons from nuclei. As the universe expanded it cooled. At an age of roughly 380,000 years enough electrons remained bound that light could travel a great distance through the gas. We can still detect radiation from that interval. The electric force had been there all along. What had changed was how long the product could survive.[2]

## Where a cloud begins to burn

A dark line cuts across the narrowest part of an hourglass in an image from the James Webb Space Telescope. Above and below it, dust glows in infrared light. Behind it lies the young object called L1527. The line marks a disk seen almost edge on. Light from the forming star escapes above and below the disk and illuminates cavities in the surrounding cloud. Those grains of dust were there before L1527 could burn hydrogen. Earlier stars had supplied their heavier elements. The cloud that gave rise to our Sun began with much the same inheritance, including the carbon now growing in a field of wheat.[3]

A cloud of gas has several ways to remain a cloud. Random motions of its particles provide pressure. Turbulent motions and magnetic fields can help support it too. If a region comes to contain enough mass within a small enough volume for gravity to overcome support, gas begins to fall inward. That changes the pressure. Material gains speed as it falls, collides and heats up. The tendency that started the collapse is producing a tendency that can resist it.[3]

The gas has to get rid of energy if it is to settle into a tighter arrangement. Radiation carries some away. Once the center becomes dense enough to trap radiation, the temperature rises and a compact object can persist. Rotation matters along the way. Gas carrying angular momentum cannot all fall straight to the middle. A disk gives material a way to orbit while angular momentum moves outward and some material moves in. Some also leaves in outflows. The cavities visible around L1527 are part of that history. A picture of a star forming includes matter that had to get out of the way.[3]

Here gravitational energy comes before nuclear energy. A protostar can light up the dust around it while still depending on contraction and infall for much of its luminosity. To sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion the core has to reach temperatures and densities at which enough nuclei react. Two positively charged nuclei repel each other. Gravity confines a large supply of them for a long time. Their thermal motions and quantum tunneling give some encounters a route across the barrier. Once enough reactions occur, the nuclear binding energy released can replace energy radiated away at the surface.[4]

A brown dwarf follows much of the same course. It can radiate the heat of contraction, and some brown dwarfs burn deuterium for a while. There is too little mass for sustained ordinary hydrogen burning. The Sun crossed that threshold. How long could it have kept shining at its present brightness on gravity alone?[3][4]

## Neutrinos from the Sun’s core

In Italy the Borexino collaboration looked for one of the first products of fusion in the Sun by putting a vessel of exceptionally clean liquid under the rock of Gran Sasso. Sunlight would have been a nuisance. So were cosmic rays and traces of ordinary radioactivity. The signal they wanted was a faint flash from an electron disturbed by a passing neutrino. Nearly every neutrino passed through the entire apparatus without interacting. A radioactive impurity in the liquid, on the other hand, was already inside the shield.[4]

Deep in the Sun, heat strips electrons from hydrogen atoms. Their nuclei can approach closely enough to fuse. Across the main chain, four hydrogen nuclei supply the matter for one helium nucleus. The decrease in mass releases energy. Near the start of the chain two protons can make a deuterium nucleus, a positron and a neutrino. The neutrino carries news of the reaction straight out of the core. The energy that works its way outward through hot, opaque matter takes a much longer route. In 2014 the collaboration reported the low-energy signal from that first reaction. To separate it from other flashes the team had to account even for two background events arriving close enough together to look like one. They identified the solar signal from the distribution of energies across many flashes.[4]

The Sun’s mass and radius give an estimate of the gravitational energy it released in contracting. Divide that energy by the power it radiates today. Even a generous estimate buys about thirty million years at today’s luminosity. Spread the same allowance over a billion years, and contraction alone can supply only a few percent of that power on average. The calculation fixes the mass and the final radius. A model that gets more energy by shrinking to a much smaller object owes us an explanation for the radius we see.[3][4]

The neutrinos identify what supplies most of the Sun’s power today. It comes from a chain of reactions that begins when two protons fuse. A second series using carbon, nitrogen and oxygen as catalysts runs in the very same core and contributes a much smaller share. Borexino eventually detected neutrinos from that series too. The Sun uses both routes at once. Its composition and core temperature set their relative rates. If a core releases heat too fast for the overlying gas to carry it away, expansion cools it and checks the burning. If the output falls, contraction can heat it again. Confinement and the escape of radiation set the rate the assembly can maintain.[3][4]

Over a billion years, hydrogen burning can sustain a flow of radiation from this mass of gas that contraction to the observed radius could never match. Contraction uses up its supply of energy much sooner. To go from radiated power to entropy production one must also know the spectrum of the light and where it is absorbed. And to give the Sun the highest rank among all the ways its parent cloud could have evolved, one would have to compare fragmentation into several stars, disk losses and outflows under the same initial conditions. The neutrinos give us the rates for the fusion reactions in the star that actually formed.[3][4]

## The limit of fusion

Where did the Sun get the carbon it uses as a catalyst? Carbon was already in the cloud when our star formed. An earlier generation of stars made it. As stars exhaust the hydrogen in their cores, some contract and get hot enough to burn the helium they have made. Three helium nuclei can combine in stages to form carbon. If a carbon nucleus captures one more helium nucleus, it can become oxygen. Both reactions release energy. Their relative rates help determine how much carbon survives the burning. Stellar winds and explosions return some of that material to the gas from which later stars and planets form.[4][5]

In a sufficiently massive star, later rounds of burning can build nuclei up to the iron group. That is where a furnace running on fusion meets a limit. Beyond the peak in binding energy per nucleon, fusing successively heavier nuclei costs energy overall. To produce gold in quantity takes another set of conditions. It also takes earlier nuclei on which the next process can work.[5][6]

On August 17, 2017, gravitational-wave detectors registered the inspiral of two neutron stars. Astronomers found light from the merger and took spectra of the expanding debris. Darach Watson and his colleagues identified strontium in those spectra. The timing gave the element an address. A fresh source of neutron-capture elements had appeared at the site of the collision.[6]

In neutron-rich ejecta an existing nucleus can capture neutrons faster than it can decay. A neutron carries no electric charge to repel it at the surface of a positively charged nucleus. In subsequent beta decays a neutron becomes a proton, advancing the nucleus to a different element. The neutron supply, the seed nuclei and the time available before the ejecta thin out restrict which chains can run. Such chains can reach gold. Strontium was the element identified in the merger spectrum. The gold line was in the atmosphere of HD 222925. The two observations tell us where a process operated and what earlier generations left behind, without assigning that star’s gold to a particular collision.[1][6]

The passage from hydrogen to gold has several sources of power. A cloud contracts under gravity. A star can burn fuel built by an earlier round of fusion. In a merger, previously assembled stellar remnants supply unusual matter and an expanding flow. A heavier nucleus can require an investment from its surroundings. A theory that ranks assemblies by the energy they release has to count the history of the whole system. Gold sitting in a cool atmosphere has finished the part of that history we can read from its spectrum.[1][5][6]

## What a planet inherits

In a wheat field near Ciudad Obregón in Mexico, Rut Sanchez-Bragado and her colleagues compared the carbon in ripe kernels with carbon from different parts of the same plants. Two carbon isotopes occurred in slightly different proportions in the ear and in the shoot below it. From the proportions in the ripe grain, the team estimated that the green ear itself supplied about 70 percent of the carbon compounds that filled the kernels in their plots. A wheat plant takes carbon dioxide from the air and uses sunlight to build sugars. Some of that carbon ends up in the starch we eat. Earlier stars supplied the carbon nuclei. Fusion in the Sun supplies the energy for the plant to assemble them into food.[5][7]

The Atlantic seafloor inherited those elements too. Water containing oxygen made in stars seeps into iron-bearing rock. As iron in the rock reacts with the water, hydrogen gas forms and returns to the sea in the vent fluid. A microbe can draw energy from the meeting of that fluid with seawater even where sunlight never reaches. The next chapter follows an expedition that found animals living at just such an outlet.[8]
