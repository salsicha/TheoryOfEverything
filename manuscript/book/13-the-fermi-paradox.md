# Chapter 13 — The Fermi Paradox

*Part IV · Paradox*

The piece of information that put Voyager 1 outside the Sun’s bubble came from something that happened on the Sun. A solar outburst sent a disturbance outward. When it reached the gas around the spacecraft, the electrons oscillated. Don Gurnett and his colleagues could read the density of the gas from the frequency. There were too many electrons there for the thin plasma they expected inside the bubble. Working back through the observations, the team concluded that Voyager had crossed the heliopause in August 2012. A craft launched in 1977 had reached interstellar space. It had come nowhere near another star.[1]

For the people who built it there was a substantial reward. An instrument smaller than a human passenger could send home information unobtainable from Earth. Nobody had to breathe on the way or eat at the destination. The question Enrico Fermi raised at lunch in Los Alamos in 1950 is usually retold as a question about visitors. If worlds like ours are common and the Galaxy has been here so long, where is everybody? The colleagues who recalled the conversation decades later differed on details. They agreed on the force of the question. A great deal of time appears to have gone by in which somebody could have arrived.[2]

How much time was there, though, and for what journey? A star can light up once gravity brings enough hydrogen to suitable conditions for fusion. A planet with an ocean, exposed rock and chemicals capable of feeding a metabolism needs a different inventory. Generations of stars made and dispersed heavier elements. Planet formation had to incorporate them into bodies on which water could persist, rock could react and the products could remain separated long enough to do work. At Lost City a vent supplied one side of a chemical exchange and seawater supplied the other. Detecting oxygen, carbon or iron in a star establishes none of these arrangements on its planets.[3]

Fermi’s lunch companions could give the Galaxy billions of years. How many of those years included planets with the lasting chemical gradients cells could use? Such worlds may have appeared comparatively late. Metal-rich stars of the broad group astronomers call Population I could have had an advantage. Even on such a world the small machines of the first cells and the large assemblies required for astronomy occupy different positions in the history. A short wait for life could coexist with a long wait for a species that builds radios. Even a civilization old enough to send a craft would need a reason to send a passenger.

## Five old planets

A star in the old thick disk of the Milky Way puts pressure on the first half of the answer. Kepler measured repeated losses of a little of its light. Tiago Campante and colleagues inferred five planets crossing the face of Kepler-444, all between Mercury and Venus in size. A different part of the light pattern betrayed oscillations of the star itself. Those gave the team an age of about 11.2 billion years, with an uncertainty of about a billion. All five planets travel close to the star. The measurement establishes the antiquity of small planets. It gives no inventory of an ancient ocean, vent or cell.[3]

Population I covers a range of ages. Some stars in an old metal-rich cluster have already become white dwarfs. The proposal that no relevant star could yet have reached the end of its life therefore cannot rest on a population name. We need a more demanding comparison. Given rocks and water on an old planet, how much usable chemical difference could persist, for how long, and with what means of renewal? Early, well-supported evidence of life in an ancient system would speak directly against a recent threshold. An old system with five hot little planets refutes a much easier claim, that early stars could form no little planets at all.[3]

## What a journey pays

Now consider the traveler’s account. On a living planet it takes work to keep a body at a suitable temperature, to obtain food and to replace what wears out. A society draws on an inheritance of organisms, air, water, soils and people who know what to do when something goes wrong. An interstellar passenger must carry or rebuild enough of that inheritance for the length of the voyage. Finding the same minerals at the destination that could be obtained at home offers little return to the travelers who pay. Observations have a different account. Gurnett’s team could get its result back across the boundary by radio. The spacecraft never had to come home.[1]

Voyager’s engineers have faced a smaller version of this choice. On September 26, 2024, they sent Voyager 2 a command to turn off its plasma science instrument. Its orientation in interstellar space had left it with few opportunities to make a useful measurement. Keeping it on would bring forward the day when another instrument had to go dark. By then a radio command took about nineteen hours to reach the craft. The plutonium aboard released heat at a rate set by its decay whichever instrument the team chose. On the same spacecraft, with the same remaining electrical supply, the expected observations per watt gave the engineers a reason to make one choice. Heat production alone could scarcely distinguish them.[1]

What changes when the destination is another star? Give a society the same industrial budget and the same years to spend it. It can put instruments to work at home, dispatch a probe, or use some of its launch capacity to keep passengers alive. If a detector can make the required observation unattended and send it home, the probe can devote more of a given payload to that job. It must still repay a launch and years of waiting for an answer. To favor the probe on energetic grounds one would have to show that the information or work it delivers brings more power into use across both sites than the same investment at home. The return to knowledge and the return in heat can rank these journeys differently.[1][4]

A dying home star could reverse the advantage of staying put. So could a probe able to build descendants from material at the destination. One launch might then establish a second place of manufacture, itself able to launch others. If replacements took longer to build than the machines lasted, the expansion would stop at the first destination. The forecast turns on a competition between construction and loss that can be stated without knowing what anybody on another planet would admire.[4]

The silence itself needs a scale. Radio searches listen in particular directions, frequency bands and intervals. A signal might have passed Earth before the receivers were built. Another might still be on its way. Jason Wright and colleagues calculated how much of one specified space of possible signals several searches had sampled. The answer was a tiny fraction. Detecting a persistent transmitter in a band under close observation and ruling out visits by small dormant machines are very different undertakings. Calling both results silence assigns our receivers powers they have yet to earn.[5]

Three discoveries would change this argument in different ways. An old inhabited planet would lengthen the time available for life. A population of old living planets with few signs of technology would put more of the delay between cells and engineering. A machine of extraterrestrial origin would settle the feasibility of probe travel for at least one builder, while leaving its builders’ taste for settling elsewhere quite open. The assembly account gives us reasons to search for particular missing stages. It can live with more than one answer, but it ought to lose a piece of its explanation with each adverse discovery.

The first traveler we know crossed into interstellar space because people wanted to learn something about the place where the Sun’s influence changed. To tell that it had arrived they needed a measurement of the gas and an event on the Sun. The makers and their emissary got what they came for while remaining on opposite sides of the boundary.
