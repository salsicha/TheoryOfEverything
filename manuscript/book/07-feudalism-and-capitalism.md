# Chapter 7 — Feudalism and Capitalism

*Part III · Technology*

Bishop Fulbert of Chartres ended his letter to the duke of Aquitaine by explaining its brevity. His church and city had burned. Rebuilding occupied him. He looked for help from God and from the duke. William had asked him what a vassal owed a lord. Fulbert had answered at some length about the duties on both sides before mentioning the fire.[1]

The letter dates from 1020. A faithful man had to guard his lord’s person, interests and secrets and give counsel and aid. A lord owed his man good faith on the same points. The request at the end turned a formula into a question with a material answer. Whose resources would be available when Chartres had to rebuild? The answer was different for a bishop able to write to a duke and a laborer on land held by either of them.[1]

Fealty made service the price of a claim on someone else’s help. In that respect it resembles a labor union. A worker who pays dues can call on a fund that no worker could supply alone. Fulbert could call on the duke after a fire. A peasant who owed compulsory labor faced a different prospect. The lord to whom he might appeal for protection also had the power to exact the work. Who could make good on a promise mattered as much as what each person formally owed.[1]

This is the question of *historical materialism* at the scale of a burned city. How did the people who fed, housed and protected one another reproduce the conditions of their existence? The participants occupy positions defined in relation to one another. The distinction becomes practical when a harvest, a payment or a day of labor is due. A lord depends on people whose work he can command. Their dependence on his protection can give him the means to exact more service. The two sides can reproduce the relation for a long time while having opposing interests within it. The return that sustains an arrangement need not be voluntary or just.[1][2]

## Twenty-two pieces of land

In 1349 the English Crown faced a labor shortage after the plague. It ordered eligible workers to accept service at customary rates and threatened employers who offered more. A landlord facing an uncut crop had reason to bid against the next landlord. As a group they had reason to want bidding stopped. The Crown’s order brought those interests together in a document that exists because the old terms could no longer simply be assumed.[2]

Put the two offers beside the same standing crop. At the customary wage a landlord who could still command enough hands could bring it in. If the workers stayed away, bidding more could get the grain under cover before the weather turned. A higher bid raised the district’s harvest only if it brought an idle worker to a field or moved one where the impending loss was larger. Moving a reaper from an equally urgent crop next door could change two landlords’ incomes without saving a single extra sheaf. The shortage had made a different bargain possible. Who turned up, where they had come from and how many days remained until the grain spoiled determined what the bargain could produce.[2]

At Walsham in Suffolk in October 1453, Alice Chaucer divided 387 acres of arable demesne into twenty-two parcels let at fixed annual rents. On land once farmed for the lord, the tenants would arrange the sowing and harvest. A poor crop left them owing rent. Alice retained a claim on what the land produced while transferring a large part of the job and risk of producing it. Those twenty-two grants tell us which operation had ceased to reproduce itself. The manor no longer needed to organize this labor directly in order for its owner to receive income.[2]

The leases changed who had the means and reason to act first. The alternatives on those same acres were to assemble labor under the lord’s direction or to let households organize it for a return they could retain above the rent. Where a tenant could take a dry afternoon to harvest without waiting for the steward, leasing could save grain in a narrow weather window. Where one steward could move teams quickly among fields, division could cost part of the crop. The grants record which arrangement took over at Walsham. Harvests, seed reserves and labor days over successive seasons would decide which could sustain the larger harvest under the same weather and workforce. A landlord could also prefer the rent simply because the tenant bore the risk.[2]

In December 1690 the Massachusetts Bay authorities faced the unpaid bills of a war. They authorized up to seven thousand pounds in paper bills for creditors willing to take them. The colony’s own receivers would accept the paper in payment. A man who had supplied provisions could now pass a bill to a neighbor who owed the colony a tax. The tax gave the neighbor a reason to accept it. A worn bill could even be replaced bearing the same number and value. What survived the exchange was a claim on future payments. War had created a bill the colony could settle by making its power to tax transferable. People there already reckoned debts in pounds. The order documents how particular money came to circulate, one place where war, protection and public revenue gave a slip of paper its value.[3]

## Forty francs in Lyon

A much later dispute came before Lyon’s labor council. Mademoiselle Pommet wanted forty francs for weaving. Her employer, Madame Vieux, acknowledged the debt. Illness had used up her savings, other creditors had claims on the workshop’s earnings, and a relief fund held the remainder. The printed report established both that Pommet had a valid claim and that she had to wait for the money.[4]

Karl Marx described the reproduction of a system in which one person sells labor and another can buy it again after selling the product. To see where this circle might break, follow Pommet home. A weaver must eat while she makes the next length of silk. Every day of waiting advanced the workshop credit out of resources she needed for herself. The existence of a market for silk settled very little about whether those forty francs could reach her on time.[4][5]

Ten years before the case, Lyon’s weavers had negotiated schedules of rates with merchants. The workers counted warp threads and passes of the shuttle to distinguish a fine, laborious cloth from a coarser one. Merchants present at one meeting had lacked authority to bind their colleagues. Representatives did meet and sign a schedule. After an insurrection the minister of war declared it void. A customer who bought a yard of silk could easily mistake its price for something in the cloth. The arithmetic, the authority to sign and the power to revoke the schedule all had a hand in what the price could be.[4]

Marx called this tendency for relations among people to appear as properties of things *commodity fetishism*. Niklas Luhmann supplies another part of the account. A recognized communication changes what someone can claim next. The council could establish Pommet’s claim. It could not put cash into Vieux’s till. Joined through feedback, the two accounts explain why a promise can redirect actual resources and why its authority can survive a failure of payment. What appears in public as the price of silk conceals who is bearing the delay.[4][5]

In 1871 a speaker in the House of Lords described a device that could overcome a similar obstruction. Union secretary Robert Applegarth used monthly branch reports to find a town short of carpenters and another with carpenters out of work. A common fund could buy a rail or steamboat fare. Before the report arrived, an unemployed man had to search at his own expense or wait. Once the vacancy was known, one journey could bring an idle pair of hands to timber already waiting for work. If the vacancy would otherwise have gone unfilled for weeks, the largest sustained output of the two routes could come from the one that first spent money on a fare. If another carpenter was already on his way, the fund might chiefly change who earned the wage.[6]

The three old dialectical expressions have identifiable jobs in this history. The dependence of lord and vassal, or employer and weaver, gives substance to the *interpenetration of opposites*. A sufficiently large loss of workers changing what one could demand illustrates *quantity into quality*. Wage labor loosens the personal tie of service, then people selling labor separately assemble a common protection through dues. The old claim to protection returns, this time as a collective claim workers can make on one another. In dialectical terms that recovery in a changed form is a *negation of the negation*.[2][5][6]

What could be predicted before looking at a lease or a union report? Harvest dates, the time to fill a vacancy and the cost of getting a worker there tell us when a different bargain could sustain more work. A harvest can feed a town whose workshops draw on further fuel. Grain left in the field can also feed organisms and release heat. The highest-rate claim concerns both histories, including what becomes of the grain. The wage bargain has a more immediate test. Workers have a material reason to renew it while wages arrive and buy what their households need. However much silk a workshop can sell, a weaver who has to wait for her forty francs has reason to look elsewhere.[2][4][6]
