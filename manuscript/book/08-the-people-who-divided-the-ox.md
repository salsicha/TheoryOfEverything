# Chapter 8 — The People Who Divided the Ox

*Part II · Humans*

The first cut exposed white fat. The second cut did too. By the third, Richard Lee could see that the people who had spent days disparaging his Christmas ox knew more than they had been telling him. He put the thickness of the fat at two inches. Even then, the man with the knife insisted the animal was a wreck. Then the people standing around it began to laugh.[1]

Lee had bought the big black animal from a Herero friend, Yehave, for twenty pounds. He was studying how much food people obtained at /Xai/xai in the Kalahari. He kept his own provisions to avoid throwing off the count. At Christmas he intended to contribute something. By his calculation his purchase would feed some hundred and fifty guests. Ben!a had wanted to know what he expected them to eat from such a beast. Tomazo even took him to see a smaller, fatter cow. The owner declined to sell.[1]

After the feast, Lee asked for an explanation. A hunter who brought home a large animal could start to imagine that the people he fed worked for him. Disparagement was one way to keep a hunter from building authority on somebody else’s need for supper. The camp ate for two days. Lee’s money had bought the ox. He had spent several uncomfortable days learning what else the purchase would buy.[1]

This was a deliberate practice, explained by the people who carried it out. It suggests one answer to a much larger historical question. How could a species capable of aggression make it profitable for people to put a catch within reach of others? The energy in a carcass can feed a child whose parent had an empty morning. Whether people can count on that meal depends on who divides the meat, what rights others can assert, and what happens to someone who tries to turn a temporary surplus into permanent command.

Among Ju/’hoansi groups described by Lorna Marshall, the first division could be the responsibility of the owner of the arrow that had brought the animal down. Arrows circulated among people. Identifying the best hunter was therefore a poor way to find the person in charge of a particular meal. Other records from the region describe concentrated ownership of borrowed implements and hunters left with little of what they obtained. Ownership can spread risk or concentrate power. The outcome depends on who can lend on what terms.[1]

## What selection would require

*Self-domestication* is a claim about inheritance. Richard Wrangham proposes that the ability of people to act together against a habitually aggressive member could reduce the reproductive advantage of explosive aggression. On one account, coalitions among our ancestors sometimes killed such men. A group can plan violence while its members grow less prone to lash out on impulse. Those are different behaviors, with potentially different effects on who has surviving children.[2]

Three pieces would have to hold for this to amount to biological evolution. Individuals would have to differ in relevant dispositions. Some of the difference would have to be inherited. The social response would have to alter reproductive success often enough, over enough generations, to change the distribution. The feast demonstrates a mechanism for teaching restraint in a matter of days. It leaves us with no count of prehistoric sanctions and no measure of a change in gene frequencies. Repeated learning within childhood can also yield adults practiced at restraint.[2]

Near Lake Eyasi in Tanzania an older child sometimes took a nursing mother’s infant while the women dug for tubers. The diggers tapped rocky ground around a stalk. Some holes went down more than a meter. At the roasting fire a child might have nothing to put on the scales. Kristen Hawkes, James O’Connell and Nicholas Blurton Jones watched who did what at the digging site. Without that observation it would be easy to conclude that the person who freed the digger’s hands had contributed nothing to the meal.[3]

These cases give cooperation a material advantage without assuming that a camp acts like a single person. Sharing risk can pay. Care can put a food source within reach. The people who do the caring still need to be fed. Children can spend years acquiring hard skills if someone bears the cost when novice efforts yield little. The young also learn how to teach, how to receive criticism, and when a real claim on others will be honored. An advantage that returns to those people can support another generation able to get food the same way.[3]

## Three dates, three changes

At Jebel Irhoud in Morocco human remains lay with flints that had once been heated. Minerals in the flints accumulated a record of radiation after their last sufficient heating. Measurements on the material helped the investigators date the associated fossils to about 315,000 years ago. The faces have features recognizable in our species. The braincases retain older features. An excavator can put an age on that combination. The deposits cannot tell us who held a baby while its mother dug.[4]

A jaw from Misliya Cave in Israel has been assigned an age much older than the dispersal which supplied most of the ancestry of living people outside Africa. The exact early dates are debated. The broader distinction stands. People could cross a boundary long before one of the populations that crossed it expanded over a much larger area. Evidence also points to an expansion in the range of African habitats people used around 70,000 years ago. Rainfall affected which routes could be crossed and when. Treating the entire continent as a holding place until a particular kind of human mind appeared would miss that history inside Africa.[4]

The farming transition came much later, roughly twelve thousand years ago in some regions. At Dhraʿ in the Jordan Valley, purpose-built granaries had raised floors before there is clear local evidence of domesticated crops. A floor that keeps part of a harvest available for another season changes what a community can do next. Someone has to be able to claim a share when the store is opened. The investigators found the floor. The distribution rule has left a poorer record.[5]

Anatomical change, wider dispersal and grain storage mark three different gains. Self-domestication could have contributed to the ability to cooperate across the first two intervals. It could hardly by itself have made a dry corridor passable or grain storable. The human achievement was cumulative. People came to adulthood inside arrangements for feeding and teaching them, then could alter some of those arrangements for children who followed. Social organization had become one of the conditions under which human beings evolved.

By the end of Lee’s feast the ox was gone. What could still operate at the next feast was an understanding of who owed what to whom. A much later record from France puts such an understanding in writing. It is a letter about the duties that a man owes his lord. At the end the author brings up a fire and lets his lord draw the inference.
