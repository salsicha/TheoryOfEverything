# Epilogue — What the Heat Meter Read

In January 2016 the computers and equipment in a building in Mäntsälä, Finland, used 3,411 megawatt-hours of electricity. In steady operation that much electrical work ultimately gives off about 4.58 megawatts of heat, day and night. The meter on the heat-recovery connection registered 663 megawatt-hours of waste heat supplied by the site, or 0.89 megawatts recovered on average. Farther along the pipe, the town’s heat pump delivered 1,042 megawatt-hours of heat to the network. The pump had its own electricity bill. In July the building used more electricity and the town took less heat. Three meters followed the same machines from a paying job to a warm house.[1]

When Yandex chose the site it wanted good fiber links, roads by which equipment could reach the building, and cold air to cool the machines. It was Nivos, the local utility, that saw a second customer. Air from the computers warmed water to about 35–40 degrees Celsius. The town needed roughly 55–60. Nivos bought 331 megawatt-hours of electricity for its pump that January to lift the temperature. The 663 megawatt-hours were the part of the site’s heat that reached the connection. More heat escaped by other routes.[1]

This one building gives us a heat meter. The International Energy Agency gives us the scale of the electrical supply. In 2025 the world’s data centers used an estimated **485 terawatt-hours**, an average draw of about **55 gigawatts**. Their electricity use rose 17 percent in a year. At AI-focused data centers it rose **50 percent**. The first number includes other kinds of computing. The last gives a sense of how fast the demand for AI is changing the load. In steady operation nearly all the electrical work consumed at a site eventually reaches its surroundings as heat. The Finnish records show what became of a measured share.[1]

What was gained by putting so many computers behind one set of doors? A large calculation can be divided among many chips up to the point where one needs a result from another. Add a faster chip and it may spend its newly liberated minutes waiting. In the TPU v4 system Norman Jouppi and his colleagues built, operators could reconfigure the connections among 4,096 chips. Small mirrors in optical switches shifted the paths along which the chips exchanged results. A person changing the connections could alter which jobs made good use of the entire installation without moving a single chip. An instruction copied from one place to another had changed how much work a fixed supply of equipment could perform.[2]

Many useful AI systems have precisely this combination of demands. Training takes repeated work on large collections of examples. Serving a trained model takes power again whenever someone wants an answer. An idle model has no occasion to answer anybody. Extra time spent sampling and checking alternatives can improve a response until the budget runs out or another try brings no better result. A community that can make enough use of those answers to pay for the chips, network and electricity has a reason to build another center. Under such conditions AI and its buildings are natural products of a history that has already supplied electric grids, communication, accumulated records and customers. The leap from answering questions to pursuing purposes of its own still requires a system that can set, model and correct those purposes.[2][3]

At another data center Dan Fuenffinger saw a controller lower the temperature of the cooling water. That would usually make the plant consume more power. In winter it could use colder air outside, and the change saved power. Operators had been carrying out a program’s suggestions themselves. They asked to let it make adjustments because the useful recommendations were taking up so much of their shifts. The system that followed received readings every five minutes. Operators retained authority to take over. A computation had made enough difference to the people running computers that they changed who, or what, got to turn the controls.[4]

The company compared the cooling electricity needed per unit of cooling with its historical baseline. Its reported improvement grew from about twelve percent to about thirty percent over nine months. These are gains in the cooling plant. Whether the improvement reduced total demand at the power station depended on who bought the capacity it freed. An empty rack can remain empty. An operator with another paying customer can fill it. To get from a saving to an account of expansion, there has to be somebody at the other end of that transaction.[4]

This returns us to the mine. Burning a little coal under a pump gave workers a route to much more coal. A mine could keep buying pumps because people wanted the extra output and some of the new fuel could keep the next pump going. A Finnish town had a reason to keep a data center’s heat-recovery system running even when it had no interest in buying the computations. In both cases a use that had hardly figured in the original device could extend the life of the larger arrangement. On a cold day a buyer was waiting at the end of the pipe.

## From bare rock to a building full of computers

At the Apollo 15 landing site, astronauts put probes into the lunar soil. Heat from the Moon’s interior was crossing each square meter at about 21 milliwatts. At that local rate it would take roughly **220 square kilometers** of ground to carry the power drawn by the Finnish building. The Sun heats the lunar surface by day and the surface radiates to space. The probes isolated a smaller current that keeps coming up from below even through the lunar night.[5]

Life found flows of energy in places the Sun never reaches. Beneath the ocean floor, microbes can live on the chemical energy left in buried organic matter. James Bradley and his colleagues estimated a median power of **1.5 × 10⁻²⁰ watts** per methane-producing cell there. These living microbes give us a comparison for an early way of making a living without oxygen. The power available to the first cells on Earth is unknown. Now count the cells. Across the ocean sediments the team studied worldwide, three kinds of microbial metabolism together used an estimated **37.3 gigawatts**, enough to match about **8,100** buildings drawing power at the Mäntsälä rate. Reproduction matters to the argument. A reaction yielding almost nothing per cell can sustain a substantial current across a planet once enough cells carry it out.[6]

Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace put a guinea pig in a cotton-lined basket and measured the ice it melted. After five hours and thirty-six minutes they removed the animal, let the basket cool and collected the water. They repeated the trial with the same guinea pig. Their two weighings worked out to about 400 grams of ice melted in ten hours. The animal gave off about **3.7 watts** of heat in the apparatus. It kept making heat as it breathed. A single building at Mäntsälä gives off as much heat as about **1.2 million** such animals under those conditions.[1][7]

On Béchervaise Island in East Antarctica, Adélie penguins bring fish and krill back to their chicks. Darren Southwell and his colleagues used thirteen years of records to estimate how much food the colony needed. Averaged over the breeding season, the food eaten by its 1,836 breeding pairs supplied about **190 kilowatts**. It would take about twenty-four colonies feeding at that rate to match the power entering the Finnish building.[1][7]

For a society we can keep the territory fixed and change the year. In the historian Paul Warde’s account of England and Wales in 1560, feeding draught animals took about as much energy as burning firewood. Food for people, fodder, wood, coal, wind and water together supplied an average of **2.0 gigawatts**. By 1950 the same territory used **194 gigawatts**. Coal alone supplied about 166. Total use rose nearly a hundredfold, and use per person rose sevenfold. A farm had to grow another crop to feed its teams. A mine with a pump and a railway could send out fuel accumulated over geological time.[8]

How much entropy did these flows produce? A watt tells us how much energy moves each second. Its effect depends on the form in which it arrives and where it ends up. If 4.58 megawatts of heat pass from rock at 320 kelvins to rock at 300, the transfer produces **955 watts per kelvin** of entropy. If the same power arrives as electricity and the computers dissipate it into surroundings at 300 kelvins, the rate is **15,300 watts per kelvin**. Electricity can do work before it becomes heat. A patch of ground supplied with both the original heat current and the additional electricity would produce about **16,200 watts per kelvin** through those two processes.[5]

One Finnish building drew as much power as about 7,200 people with their share of the farms, workshops and households of 1560. It would take about 440 such buildings to match the whole territory’s energy use that year, and about 42,000 to match it in 1950. Look at the coal alone. Hold the temperatures fixed and the entropy produced by the flow of coal heat rises more than eight hundredfold. What changed was the amount of fuel people could bring to the fire. The world’s data centers in 2025 already drew about 27 times the entire 1560 supply of England and Wales, and just over a quarter of its 1950 supply.[1][8]

I argue that AI is now one of the means by which the universe maximizes its rate of entropy production. A resistor on the Finnish connection could have released just as much heat. What it could never have done on its own was give a customer a reason to order another rack of resistors. An answer worth buying can earn the cost of another computation. A program that frees cooling capacity can make room for those computations in the same building. When those uses bring new generators and more buildings into service, computation changes how much energy the wider society can draw. The prediction concerns that whole society over time. It calls for counting the fuel saved at the town boiler alongside the power bought at the data center, and comparing the result with what people would have done with the same resources without AI.[1][2][4][5]

Heather Graham asked researchers to risk an answer before turning over the labels on her samples. The meters at Mäntsälä offer the same opportunity. Which month, January or July, sent more server heat into town? The electrical meter points to July, when the servers drew more power. Follow the pipe to the people who wanted the heat, and the answer is January. An argument about the rate for a whole world has to follow the energy at least that far.[1][9]
