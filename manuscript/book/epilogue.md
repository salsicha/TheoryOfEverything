# Epilogue — What the Heat Meter Read

In January 2016 a heating company in Mäntsälä, Finland, received 1,042 megawatt-hours of heat from a pump connected to a data center. In July it received 752. A person holding the data center’s electricity bills would have predicted the months the other way round. The building used 3,411 megawatt-hours of electricity in January and 4,034 in July. Jani Laine and his colleagues obtained both sets of records. The customers who wanted heat and the customers who wanted computing had different uses for the building and kept different hours.[1]

When Yandex chose the site it wanted good fiber links, roads by which equipment could reach the building, and cold air to cool the machines. The suggestion of selling heat came from Nivos, the local utility. The town already had pipes that carried hot water to customers. Air drawn past the computers could warm water to about 35–40 degrees Celsius. The network wanted roughly 55–60. Nivos supplied the heat pump to take the last step. In the January account the pump itself consumed 331 megawatt-hours of electricity. Some of the heat counted at the outlet came from that extra purchase. The difference matters to the customer paying for it.[1]

What was gained by putting so many computers behind one set of doors? A large calculation can be divided among many chips up to the point where one needs a result from another. Add a faster chip and it may spend its newly liberated minutes waiting. In the TPU v4 system Norman Jouppi and his colleagues built, operators could reconfigure the connections among 4,096 chips. Small mirrors in optical switches shifted the paths along which the chips exchanged results. A person changing the connections could alter which jobs made good use of the entire installation without moving a single chip. An instruction copied from one place to another had changed how much work a fixed supply of equipment could perform.[2]

Many useful AI systems have precisely this combination of demands. Training takes repeated work on large collections of examples. Serving a trained model takes power again whenever someone wants an answer. An idle model has no occasion to answer anybody. Extra time spent sampling and checking alternatives can improve a response until the budget runs out or another try brings no better result. A community that can make enough use of those answers to pay for the chips, network and electricity has a reason to build another center. Under such conditions AI and its buildings are natural products of a history that has already supplied electric grids, communication, accumulated records and customers. The leap from answering questions to pursuing purposes of its own still requires a system that can set, model and correct those purposes.[2][3]

At another data center Dan Fuenffinger saw a controller lower the temperature of the cooling water. That would usually make the plant consume more power. In winter it could use colder air outside, and the change saved power. Operators had been carrying out a program’s suggestions themselves. They asked to let it make adjustments because the useful recommendations were taking up so much of their shifts. The system that followed received readings every five minutes. Operators retained authority to take over. A computation had made enough difference to the people running computers that they changed who, or what, got to turn the controls.[4]

The company compared the cooling electricity needed per unit of cooling with its historical baseline. Its reported improvement grew from about twelve percent to about thirty percent over nine months. These are gains in the cooling plant. Whether the improvement reduced total demand at the power station depended on who bought the capacity it freed. An empty rack can remain empty. An operator with another paying customer can fill it. To get from a saving to an account of expansion, there has to be somebody at the other end of that transaction.[4]

This returns us to the mine. Burning a little coal under a pump gave workers a route to much more coal. A mine could keep buying pumps because people wanted the extra output and some of the new fuel could keep the next pump going. A Finnish town had a reason to keep a data center’s heat-recovery system running even when it had no interest in buying the computations. In both cases a use that had hardly figured in the original device could extend the life of the larger arrangement. On a cold day a buyer was waiting at the end of the pipe.

Does it follow that the data center maximizes the rate of entropy production? Follow one unit of heat. Transferring it from a hot body to a colder body increases their combined entropy by an amount that depends on both temperatures. Discharging heat straight to cold outside air produces more entropy in that transfer than passing the same heat to water that is already warm. The warm water, in turn, can heat a house. A faster flow across a fixed temperature difference produces entropy faster. Recovering heat changes the places and temperatures at which those transfers happen. Electricity used by the heat pump entered somewhere else. Gas the town used to burn could stay unburned. A meter can count megawatt-hours accurately while leaving the entropy produced across the whole system undetermined.[1][5]

The strongest claim of this book is that reality proceeds toward energy extraction at the greatest sustained rate available under its constraints. It describes a selection process each time work done today changes which routes can be used tomorrow. An assembly that gains access to more energy gains an advantage when some of the extra flow can renew it, multiply it or build the next assembly. Higher levels can therefore open sources that their parts could scarcely touch. The claim makes a demand on anyone who invokes it. Specify an alternative people could actually have built, with the same jobs to run and homes to heat, over the same winters. Count the electricity, the displaced boilers, the temperatures and the work required to build and renew each arrangement. A community could keep choosing a route that delivers its answers on time while a feasible rival produces more entropy. That outcome would preserve the explanation of its choice and count against the proposed maximum.[5]

Heather Graham started with specimens whose labels she knew and asked other people to tell her which showed evidence of life. What they did before she disclosed the labels mattered. They had to choose a sign of repeated construction while a wrong answer was still possible. We know who built the Finnish installation. What is still open is whether we can account for its form well enough to say in advance where another like it will earn its keep, where a smaller device will do the work, and which arrangement will use the larger flow. For a start, a town needs a heat customer. In July the computers worked harder. In January the town drew more from those same machines and sent it down the pipes to people’s homes.[1][6]
