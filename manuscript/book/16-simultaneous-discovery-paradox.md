# Chapter 16 — Simultaneous discovery paradox

One Thursday in May 1905, Albert Einstein offered Conrad Habicht four papers in exchange for his friend’s dissertation. The fourth was still rough. It concerned moving bodies and what physicists meant by space and time. Around the same month Henri Poincaré wrote to Hendrik Lorentz after missing him in Paris. He had been working through Lorentz’s equations. In a second letter he corrected his argument. He could now show why a number Lorentz’s calculation had left adjustable must equal one.[1]

Poincaré presented his result in Paris on June 5. The editors of *Annalen der Physik* received Einstein’s paper on June 30. Twenty-five days separated the presentation and the submission. How had two men come to the same rule by different routes that spring? In 1887 an optical measurement still left room for more than one answer.[1][2]

## Turning the stone

In Cleveland, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley floated a slab of stone on mercury. On it sat mirrors and a device that split a beam of light. The beams followed perpendicular paths, rejoined and produced bright and dark stripes. Turning the stone exchanged the paths’ directions relative to Earth’s motion. If Earth moved through a stationary medium that carried light, the stripes should shift. The team marked sixteen positions for a reading. Stopping at a mark strained the apparatus. By the time the stripe held still, a change in temperature could move it again. They learned to take the readings as the stone turned. One revolution took about six minutes.[2]

They expected a shift of about four tenths of a stripe spacing and saw a small fraction of it. In 1889 George FitzGerald pointed out that motion through the ether might shorten a solid body along its direction of travel. An arm of the instrument could then shrink by just enough to cancel the expected delay. The Cleveland reading still allowed a stationary ether and a single time common to everyone.[2]

Other instruments failed to reveal the expected motion, among them a charged condenser Frederick Trouton and Henry Noble had suspended so it could turn. In 1900 Poincaré worked out how observers moving together could synchronize distant clocks by exchanging light signals. Four years later, in St. Louis, he described the time those clocks would show. That year Lorentz published equations relating measurements in a moving system to those in one at rest. They contained a scale factor. His model of an electron gave him a reason to set it to one. Any new account now had to fit the lengths, the clocks and the electrical experiments together.[1][3]

The measured properties of moving electrons still fitted more than one proposed shape. Poincaré demanded something else of the equations. Convert a measurement from one laboratory to a second, then from the second to a third. A direct conversion from the first to the third must give the same answer, or the reported length of a ruler would depend on who forwarded the figures. Rotating the laboratories also had to leave the rule intact. Together those requirements fixed the scale without a choice about the forces inside an electron.[1]

## Two ways to set a clock

Einstein’s magnet posed another problem. Move it past a conducting loop and a current flows. Move the loop past the magnet in the opposite direction at the same relative speed and the same current flows. Textbook accounts assigned different causes to the two results. Einstein wanted the same laws for both observers and a common speed of light in a vacuum. He had to tell his readers how separated observers could synchronize two clocks. Send a light signal from A to B and reflect it straight back. Set B at reflection to the midpoint of the departure and return times measured at A. Observers travelling with the clocks can use the rule.[1]

Now let an observer on the ground watch the signal pass between two clocks on a moving platform. On the outward leg the light must catch the front clock. On the return leg the rear clock comes toward the light. The traveller sets the clocks by dividing the round trip equally. The ground observer assigns unequal times to its two legs. Both can keep Einstein’s rule for setting clocks and both can measure the same speed of light. What they have to give up is agreement on which events at different places happened at the same time. A clock’s position along the direction of travel enters the equation for its time.[1]

The spare factor also turns up in Einstein’s paper. It could stretch or shrink a ruler laid *across* the direction of travel. Convert the length from one observer’s frame to the other and back. The two factors must multiply to one or the first observer gets back a different ruler. Reverse the direction of travel. A sideways ruler has the same length whether the laboratory moves to the right or the left, so the two factors must be equal. The only positive value left is one. Lorentz had obtained that value the year before. Einstein’s ruler and Poincaré’s successive transformations showed why whole classes of measurements required it.[1]

Here *quantity turned into quality*. One more failed attempt to detect motion through the ether might have called for another adjustment to the apparatus. Requiring one rule that preserved light speed, worked with the clocks people could set and returned the ruler at its original length changed what could be deduced. For a given speed, axes and units, the conversion had no scale left to adjust.[1][2][3]

The work that had taken a generation could now pass between desks. A physicist could use the Cleveland readings without floating another slab on mercury. Lorentz’s equations were in print. Once the remaining choice came down to a short calculation with a ruler, two people working from those results had reason to reach the same number promptly. The May letters put both men at work before either June appearance.[1][2][4]

Their disagreement survived where the constraints ran out. Poincaré could retain an ether with a true time the moving clocks did not show. Einstein took the readings as time in the clocks’ own frame. An earlier group with the same measurements, clocks and equations and years of fruitless work would count against discovery at the first opportunity. The June records give us a date by which each man had reached the result. Comparing its entropy production with rival research would require both the energy those routes consumed and the work their results made possible.[1][2][3][4]

In 1909 Einstein calculated fluctuations in radiation and obtained a wave-like term and a term associated with discrete exchanges of energy. Both survived in the same calculation. In dialectical language, recovering features of two accounts that had seemed to exclude each other is a *negation of the negation*. This was a different problem from the moving clocks of 1905, with a similar obligation to preserve measurements both accounts had explained.[5]

In 1977 a navigation-satellite team switched its on-board clock from quartz to full-power cesium operation after fifteen days. Tracking measurements from Panama showed a net gain against ground time of about thirty-eight microseconds a day, close to the prediction from motion and gravity together. A ground crew could keep correcting an accumulating error. Building the rule for drift into the service spared some of those corrections. Other clock errors still required calibration.[6]

The engineers could predict the sign and size of the drift before switching on the cesium clock. To find the energy consequences we would have to follow what customers did with the service. The narrower prediction of June 1905 can be checked on paper. Send a ruler’s length through the conversion and back. Whatever each man thought about the ether, the length had to come back unchanged.[1][6]
