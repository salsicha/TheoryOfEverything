# Chapter 4 — From hydrogen to gold

The gold in Ann Nguyen and Ernst Zinner’s spectrum came from under the grain. They had spread dust from the meteorite Acfer 094 on a gold mount in their St. Louis laboratory. One grain, half a micrometer across, had caught their attention in a scan with an ion probe. Its ratio of oxygen-17 to ordinary oxygen-16 was nearly three times the solar value. When they turned an electron beam on it to identify the mineral, the instrument picked up the mount as well.[1]

Most of the grains had oxygen like the Sun’s. Nine carried the distinctive isotope ratios of other stars. One was pyroxene. Two were olivine, a mineral common in Earth’s mantle. Some dust had survived the birth of the Sun with a record of its earlier home intact. The gold under the grain posed a different question. A star can shine for billions of years making helium, then make the oxygen in a grain like this one. What would it take to put gold in the same cloud?[1][4][5]

## The bond that cooled the gas

Two hydrogen atoms in the early gas could collide and fly apart again. To stay bound they had to shed energy. A free electron gave them a route. One atom could catch it and release a photon. If the second atom reached it before light stripped the extra electron away, the two atoms could bind and eject an electron. A later collision could set the new molecule rotating. When the molecule dropped to a lower rotational energy, a photon could carry the difference out of the cloud.[2]

Only certain energies and arrangements allow the atoms to stay bound. That restriction holds wherever hydrogen meets hydrogen, a constraint in what the book calls *Platonic space*. A molecule that survived a collision could shed energy as light and take up heat in another collision. The escaping light carried entropy into colder space. Even a small fraction of hydrogen bound into molecules gave a much larger mass of gas a way to lose heat and settle inward. A bond between two atoms had changed how much gas gravity could gather into a star. That passage from one scale to the next, with entropy leaving in the light, is the *hierarchical entropy rejection funnel*. By the time the core grew hot enough for fusion, the molecules at its center had come apart. The mass they had helped gather stayed together.[2][3]

The first stars, called Population III, had almost nothing but hydrogen and helium to work with. Their gas held no gold. A star enters the long part of its life called the main sequence once its core can sustain hydrogen fusion. Four hydrogen nuclei can become one helium nucleus through a series of reactions. The difference in mass supplies the energy that the star radiates. Beneath Gran Sasso in Italy, the Borexino team caught neutrinos from the first step of this process in our own Sun, where two protons meet. The Sun has been drawing on its hydrogen for some 4.6 billion years.[2][3][6]

## What a star can return to space

When the Sun exhausts the hydrogen in its center, it will still have hydrogen farther out. The helium core will contract under its own weight. Hydrogen will burn in a shell around it, and the outer layers will expand into a red giant. The surface will cool and redden even as gravity heats the center enough for helium to burn. There, two helium nuclei can join for a moment. If a third strikes before they break apart, the three can form carbon. Some of the carbon captures another helium nucleus and becomes oxygen.[3][4]

A star of the Sun’s mass stops there. Its core never gets hot enough to burn carbon. A wind carries away its envelope and the exposed core cools as a white dwarf. Most of the carbon and oxygen it made stay inside. In somewhat heavier giants, rising gas brings freshly made carbon up into the envelope, where the wind can carry it away. How much material escapes a star this way? In the cluster Messier 4, Jason Kalirai’s team measured white dwarfs whose parent stars began with about four-fifths of the Sun’s mass. Two-thirds of that mass still sat in the remnants. Only the third they had lost was available to another cloud.[3][4]

In a much more massive star the weight on the center raises the temperature, and fusion spends the larger fuel supply in a fraction of the time. A massive star born alongside a Sun-like neighbor can burn through carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon while its neighbor is still burning hydrogen. Each stage leaves material that the next, hotter stage can use. The sequence builds an iron-rich core. At that point further fusion can no longer supply the power the star radiates. As more ash lands on the core, it can grow too heavy to support itself and collapse. Some collapses drive a supernova that scatters oxygen, magnesium, silicon and iron into surrounding gas. Depending on the mass that remains, the center can become a neutron star or a black hole.[3][4]

## How a cloud acquires gold

Two massive stars in orbit can each leave a neutron star. Some of these pairs spiral together as gravitational waves carry away orbital energy. When they collide, some of the matter thrown clear is so rich in neutrons that an atomic nucleus can capture several before it has time to decay. As neutrons in the growing nuclei turn into protons, new elements form. This is the rapid neutron-capture process, and it can reach gold. In 2017 astronomers followed the fading light of a neutron-star merger. Darach Watson and his colleagues identified strontium in the expanding debris. They had caught freshly made heavy material on its way back out into space.[5]

How early could that material reach another star? With the Hubble Space Telescope, Ian Roederer’s team split ultraviolet light from an old Milky Way star, HD 222925, into its component wavelengths. For a given amount of hydrogen, the star held about one-thirtieth as much iron as the Sun. At a wavelength of 267.6 nanometers some light was missing. Gold atoms had absorbed it. Even gas that poor in iron had received gold before the star formed.[5]

## What a planet inherits

Astronomers call the old, generally metal-poor stars Population II. Here a *metal* is any element heavier than helium, oxygen as much as gold. Planet building was already under way in that old population. In the light of a different metal-poor star, Kepler-444, Tiago Campante and his colleagues found five planets smaller than Earth crossing its face. From the star’s oscillations they estimated its age at about eleven billion years. The transits gave them the planets’ sizes. A later spectrum of the star itself showed magnesium, silicon, iron and even yttrium, another element built by neutron capture. The planets’ gold content remains unknown.[6]

The Sun belongs to Population I, the generally younger, metal-rich population. By the time it formed about 4.57 billion years ago, its cloud had collected debris from many earlier stars. In primitive meteorites, Mario Fischer-Gödde and his colleagues measured gold in the rock itself. Earth could inherit gold only after an earlier source had made it and the debris had reached the solar system’s birth cloud. Some clouds met that condition long before ours did. Whether they could also build a seafloor that supplied hydrogen to an ocean depended on what else they held.[1][5][6]

Some grains reached the young solar system intact. Others condensed there as the gas cooled. Katharina Lodders calculated which solids could persist in a gas of solar composition at one ten-thousandth of a bar. A magnesium-rich form of olivine began to condense at about 1,350 kelvin. Water ice first became stable near 180 kelvin. Across that gap some oxygen could travel in solid grains while water remained in the gas. A disk gathering material into planets could sort those two supplies of oxygen long before an ocean lay against a rock.[1][7]

Dust also changed what happened to the energy streaming past it. A grain could absorb visible starlight, warm up and radiate the energy in many lower-energy infrared photons. The outgoing light carried more entropy, while the grain could keep its mineral structure.[1][7]

Earlier stars supplied the carbon in a grain of wheat. Near Ciudad Obregón in Mexico, Rut Sanchez-Bragado and her colleagues traced what our own star did with that inheritance. They compared carbon isotopes in ripe kernels and in the green parts of the plants. In their plots the ear itself supplied an estimated seventy percent of the carbon compounds that filled the grain. Sunlight gave the plant the energy to take electrons from water and use them to turn carbon dioxide into carbohydrate. After dark, the plant could draw on energy stored during the day.[8]

## Where rock makes hydrogen

On the Atlantic floor, rock can supply electrons in the dark. Tectonic movement brought mantle rock within reach of seawater at the Lost City hydrothermal field. As water worked into the rock, iron in minerals such as olivine gave up electrons. Hydrogen in the water accepted them and paired off as dissolved hydrogen gas. Deborah Kelley’s team measured concentrations of up to fifteen millimoles of hydrogen per kilogram of fluid coming back out. Seawater on the other side of a vent wall supplied dissolved inorganic carbon.[9]

The fluids could meet at the same temperature and still have a chemical difference capable of doing work. Down an open crack, much of the hydrogen might reach the sea unspent. At a mineral wall that helps hydrogen give up electrons to carbon dioxide, some of the same flow can go into making a carbon compound. If assembly favors the route with the highest sustained rate of entropy production, the comparison turns on how much fluid passes, the heat it releases and the products it carries away.[9][10]

The two olivine grains in St. Louis show that this family of minerals already existed before Earth did. Where an ocean reaches fresh, iron-bearing olivine, each parcel of seawater can renew a chemical gradient. A rocky planet could do that much before it had a single cell.[1][9][10]
