# Chapter 4 — From hydrogen to gold

Ann Nguyen and Ernst Zinner aimed an electron beam at a speck of meteorite dust half a micrometer wide. Gold showed up in the spectrum. It came from the mount on which they had spread the dust in their St. Louis laboratory. What had drawn them to this grain from the meteorite Acfer 094 was its oxygen. For every atom of ordinary oxygen-16, they had found nearly three times as much oxygen-17 as they expected from material formed with the Sun.[1]

Most of the grains clustered around the solar proportions. Nine stood out. Stars change the proportions of oxygen isotopes as they age, and dust that condenses around them can carry that record away. The nine grains had reached the solar system already made. Grain one was a pyroxene. Two of the others were olivine. On parts of the Atlantic floor, seawater reaches mantle rock rich in those same minerals. The clouds that made the first stars contained scarcely any of the oxygen or silicon needed to make either mineral. Yet those stars already owed their existence in part to a chemical bond.[1][2]

## The bond that cooled the gas

Two hydrogen atoms in the early gas could collide and fly apart again. To stay bound they had to shed energy. A free electron gave them a route. One atom could catch it and release a photon. If the second atom reached it before light stripped the extra electron away, the two atoms could bind and eject an electron. A later collision could set the new molecule rotating. When the molecule dropped to a lower rotational energy, a photon could carry the difference out of the cloud.[2]

Only certain energies and arrangements allow the atoms to stay bound. That restriction holds wherever hydrogen meets hydrogen, a constraint in what the book calls *Platonic space*. A molecule that survived a collision could shed energy as light and take up heat in another collision. The escaping light carried entropy into colder space. Even a small fraction of hydrogen bound into molecules gave a much larger mass of gas a way to lose heat and settle inward. A bond between two atoms had changed how much gas gravity could gather into a star. That passage from one scale to the next, with entropy leaving in the light, is the *hierarchical entropy rejection funnel*. By the time the core grew hot enough for fusion, the molecules at its center had come apart. The mass they had helped gather stayed together.[2][3]

In a stellar core hot enough to sustain hydrogen burning, four hydrogen nuclei can become one helium nucleus through a chain of reactions. The products have less mass and the difference supplies the star with energy. Beneath Gran Sasso in Italy, the Borexino team recorded neutrinos from the first step of the main chain in our Sun, where two protons meet. The Sun can keep radiating for billions of years on its nuclear fuel. A brown dwarf too light to sustain that chain radiates away the heat of contraction and dims.[3]

When a star uses up the hydrogen in its core, the helium left there can contract and heat up. In a hot, dense enough core two helium nuclei can form beryllium-8, which almost always falls apart before a third reaches it. On the rare occasions when a third arrives in time, the three can form an excited carbon nucleus. Most of those fall apart too. A small share shed energy and settle into carbon-12. Some of that carbon captures another helium nucleus, making oxygen-16. The rates of those two reactions help decide how much carbon survives alongside the oxygen. The cloud that formed our solar system inherited both.[3][4]

The gold beneath Nguyen and Zinner’s sample took a different route. Successive stages of fusion in massive stars can reach the iron group. Beyond the peak in binding energy a star cannot keep powering itself by fusing ever heavier nuclei. Where enough free neutrons reach existing nuclei in quick succession, some nuclei can grow much heavier. Subsequent radioactive decays turn neutrons into protons, changing the element. A neutron-star merger in 2017 left a trace of strontium in the light from its expanding debris. Ian Roederer’s team found gold in the atmosphere of a different, ancient star. These observations catch heavy elements at two points in their history, in material on its way out and in material another star inherited. They leave open which event supplied the gold on the laboratory mount.[5]

## What a planet inherits

A star can make an oxygen nucleus deep in its interior. To make a grain of rock, the oxygen must reach a place cool enough for chemical bonds to last. Stellar winds and explosions scattered material enriched in oxygen, magnesium, silicon and iron. In cooler gas atoms could keep their outer electrons. The nuclei determined which elements were present. The electrons determined how those elements could bind to one another.[1][4][6]

Some grains reached the young solar system intact. Others condensed there as the gas cooled. Katharina Lodders calculated which solids could persist in a gas of solar composition at one ten-thousandth of a bar. A magnesium-rich form of olivine began to condense at about 1,350 kelvin. Water ice first became stable near 180 kelvin. Across that gap some oxygen could travel in solid grains while water remained in the gas. A disk gathering material into planets could sort those two supplies of oxygen long before an ocean lay against a rock.[1][6]

Dust could also change what happened to the energy streaming past it. A grain that absorbed visible starlight warmed up and radiated the energy in many lower-energy infrared photons. The energy spread over more photons and carried more entropy away. Meanwhile the grain could keep its mineral structure. The nine grains on the gold mount preserve oxygen from an earlier generation of stars in just such solid material.[1][6]

Earlier stars supplied the carbon in a grain of wheat. Near Ciudad Obregón in Mexico, Rut Sanchez-Bragado and her colleagues traced what the Sun’s light did with that carbon. They compared the proportions of carbon isotopes in ripe kernels and in the green parts of the plants. In their plots they estimated that the ear itself supplied about seventy percent of the carbon compounds used to fill the grain. Sunlight let the plant take electrons from water and use them to reduce carbon dioxide to carbohydrate, releasing oxygen. After sunset, the plant can still use oxygen to break down carbohydrate and obtain energy for growth. Part of a day’s light has become a chemical difference that can do work at night.[7]

## Where rock makes hydrogen

On the Atlantic floor, fresh rock can supply electrons in the dark. Tectonic movement along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge brought mantle rock within reach of seawater. In its olivine, four oxygen atoms surround each silicon, and magnesium or iron can occupy sites between those groups. Iron in those sites can give up an electron as the rock reacts with water. Hydrogen in the water accepts electrons and pairs off as dissolved hydrogen gas. Some iron stays in the altered rock in a more oxidized state. Where the new minerals take up the iron in its original state, less hydrogen forms. Even rocks with ample iron can send quite different fluids back to the sea.[8]

At the Lost City hydrothermal field, Deborah Kelley’s team measured what came back out. Hydrogen concentrations ranged from less than one to fifteen millimoles per kilogram of vent fluid. The fluid was alkaline and poor in carbon dioxide. On the other side of a chimney wall, seawater supplied dissolved inorganic carbon. The two fluids could reach the same temperature and still hold a chemical energy gradient. Hydrogen could donate electrons and, under suitable conditions, carbon dioxide could accept them. A difference in acidity across a mineral barrier could help drive that transfer. Part of the energy released could then go into making a new carbon compound.[8][9]

In a sealed pocket, a reaction spends the difference that drives it and slows as the products accumulate. At an open vent, water reaches fresh rock, carries hydrogen upward and meets more seawater. Flow can sweep the products away. How much of the gradient gets used depends on the route the water takes. Hydrogen that passes straight through a crack can reach the sea with much of its chemical energy still available. Along a wall that catalyzes electron transfer, some of the same hydrogen can react on the way. The book proposes that assembly favors the route with the highest sustained rate of entropy production. The hydrogen concentration at the inlet gives only part of that account. How much fluid passed, how much heat escaped and what left in the products matter too.[8][9]

The two olivine grains on the gold mount tell us how early this family of minerals existed. At Lost City, water that has passed through iron-bearing rock delivers hydrogen to a pore in a white chimney. Seawater reaches the pore from the other side. A reaction there can draw on the difference, and the next parcel of water can renew it. A rocky planet could do that much before it had a single cell.[1][8][9]
