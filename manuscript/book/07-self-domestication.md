# Chapter 7 — Self Domestication

/Twi had killed three people. Then his own community killed him. Richard Lee recorded the account among the Ju/’hoansi of the Kalahari. After /Twi died, the women as well as the men publicly took responsibility for his death. Lee described their agreement as rare. In a feud, a bereaved family had another family to hold responsible. Here a whole community claimed the deed.[1]

During his fieldwork in the 1960s, Lee asked people about killings they remembered from the years before he arrived. He recorded twenty-two homicides in the Dobe and Nyae Nyae regions between 1920 and 1955. Fifteen belonged to feuds. One series of deaths stretched over twenty years. Lee wanted to understand how people who had lost relatives could ever bring such a sequence to an end.[1]

The anthropologist Richard Wrangham saw an evolutionary question in accounts like these. What happened to the family line of a man who might once have got his way by force? In Wrangham’s *execution hypothesis*, early human communities sometimes killed their most persistently aggressive members, particularly men who used threats and violence to dominate others. If this happened often enough, people less inclined to lash out could leave more descendants. This is the proposed course of *human self-domestication*. A rule people enforced together could change what the children born under it inherited.[2]

## Who lived to raise children

The proposed history extends back some 200,000 years. Its claim is that repeated penalties for dangerous aggression changed who survived to raise children. An oral history can document an incident within living memory. To carry the argument back through those millennia takes separate evidence of inherited variation, its consequences for reproduction and the physical changes that followed.[1][2][4]

A sudden attack in anger and a killing a community decides on in advance call on different capacities. Wrangham calls the first *reactive aggression* and the second *proactive aggression*. A tendency to fly at someone who stood in the way could become costly in a group quite capable of planned violence. The people who killed /Twi displayed the second capacity. Lee’s account tells us how many people /Twi had killed. What he felt at the time of each killing is lost to us.[1][2]

A death could end a danger that day. Its effect on the next generation depended on when it happened. A man with grown children might already have left as many descendants as his neighbors. A man who died younger lost the chance to father more children and to help the ones he had survive. If habitual aggressors left fewer surviving descendants on average, inherited differences that made it easier to hold back could become more common in sons and daughters alike. The children would still have to learn what their neighbors expected.[2][3]

Lee found out how fast that learning could happen. For a Christmas feast at /Xai/xai he bought a black ox big enough to feed about a hundred and fifty people. For days his hosts told him the animal was too thin. When the hide came off, Lee could see two inches of fat. Later Tomazo explained why people belittled even a good kill. A man who supplied meat might begin to think the people eating it ought to do as he said. They ate Lee’s ox for two days. By the end of the feast he had a better idea of his standing.[1][3]

Wrangham’s account makes a further claim about obedience. If defying a collective judgment also carried a cost, the more pliant members of a group might have left more children. Descendants could then be both milder in a quarrel and more subservient to the group. Those traits can come apart. A calm person can refuse an order. An angry person can obey through fear. What became advantageous would depend on whose anger drew a sanction and whose orders the others enforced.[2][3]

## Why selection on temperament could change the face

Early in an embryo’s development, a band of cells forms along the back of the structure that will become the brain and spinal cord. These are the *neural crest cells*. Some move into the developing head and give rise to much of the bone and cartilage of the face, including the jaws. Other neural crest descendants, from farther down the body, help form the adrenal medulla, the core of each adrenal gland above the kidneys. Cells there release adrenaline during a sudden threat. The face and part of the machinery that readies a body for a confrontation thus have a common developmental origin.[4]

Adam Wilkins, Wrangham and W. Tecumseh Fitch began with a puzzle about domesticated mammals. Breeders could favor animals that tolerated handling and find that the jaws changed too. The three researchers proposed a mild reduction in the contribution neural crest cells make to particular tissues. Fewer cells might reach a site, or the cells that arrived might multiply less. A smaller contribution to the adrenal medulla could dampen the rush of adrenaline under threat. If the same inherited changes also reduced growth in the face, selection for a milder temperament could bring a smaller jaw or a narrower face with it. Applied to humans, the proposal explains how neighbors who cared what a dangerous man did could have influenced, over many generations, how the faces of their descendants grew.[2][4]

Matteo Zanella and colleagues could watch part of that process in cells from living people. They studied neural crest cells derived from donors who differed in the number of copies of a stretch of chromosome 7. A gene in that stretch, *BAZ1B*, helps regulate other genes active as the face develops. In a dish the team watched cells move into a gap. How quickly they closed it depended on the gene’s activity and the genetic background of the cells. Here was a way an inherited difference could alter the movement of cells that help build a face. Whether the same differences made ancient people more willing to submit to others remains an open question.[4][5]

## The width across the cheekbones

On the human skull known as Jebel Irhoud 1, from Morocco, part of the cheek arch is missing. Heated flints found with other remains at the site date an early human occupation there to about 315,000 years ago. Robert Cieri and his colleagues faced the same missing piece at Qafzeh in Israel. For both skulls they estimated facial breadth from the widest span of the arches that survived. They also measured the height of the upper face, from the root of the nose to the upper jaw between the front teeth.[6]

Of the skulls in the group older than 80,000 years, seven provided a measurement across the cheekbones. Their average was 145.6 millimeters. Thirty-four in the later fossil group averaged 139.0. The later faces were narrower on average, though the small early sample varied enough that the difference in width was statistically uncertain. Height told a clearer story. The upper face grew shorter over time, and the brow ridges projected less. A later study by Mark Hubbe and Katerina Harvati also found relative narrowing of the upper face when they compared three-dimensional skull shapes along the lineage leading to living humans.[6]

Cieri’s team proposed changing sensitivity to androgens, hormones that influence growth, as one possible explanation for the brow and upper face. The work a jaw does matters too. Across the much later transition to agriculture, David Katz and colleagues found modest differences in skull shape consistent with the softer diets of farming populations. Bone preserves the outcome of growth. To trace a narrower face to self-domestication would take evidence that the inherited changes affecting facial growth also affected responses to provocation, and that those differences mattered to who left descendants.[4][6]

Lee could see what yielding to another person meant when there was meat to divide. The owner of the arrow that brought down an antelope had a right to distribute the meat. Hunters lent and traded arrows, and women as well as men could own them. If the owner was away, the hunter saved a share of dried meat for that person. Honoring such a claim could feed a household on a day when its own hunt had failed. People had good reason to keep the arrangement going, even when that meant handing over meat they had brought home themselves.[7]

At the Christmas feast, Lee’s hosts made sure that buying the ox gave him no authority over the people who ate it. In the account Lee recorded of /Twi, a community claimed authority over a man’s life. If that power repeatedly changed who lived to raise children, its effects could outlast anyone’s memory of a particular killing. Children could be born more ready to yield. Whom they learned to obey would depend on the people who raised them.[1][2][3]
