# Prologue — The Sample Without a Name

Heather Graham sent the samples without their names. One contained material from the Murchison meteorite. Another came from bay sediment. Graham knew which was which. The people who would put them through a mass spectrometer had to give their answer before she told them.[1]

Ordinarily a biologist gets to know where to look. A green film on a rock lies just where light and water meet. A bottle in a brewery comes with a fair presumption about yeast. An instrument sent to another planet might draw a sample through a tube from a place nobody has ever seen. It might find organic molecules. So did the people who examined Murchison after it fell in Australia. Carbon chemistry runs well beyond the reach of anything known to be alive. What, then, could the instrument recognize?[1][2]

Stuart Marshall, Cole Mathis and their colleagues proposed looking for evidence of work done more than once. In outline their procedure was simple. Break a molecule into fragments. From the pattern of fragments, estimate how many distinct joining operations it would take to build the original. In counting those operations, allow a piece already built to be used again. A molecule containing the same section four times should get credit for the fact that there is a shorter route to it. So should a molecule whose parts can themselves be made from common smaller parts. The least number of steps under the specified rules is its molecular *assembly index*. The team calibrated their estimate against molecules of known structure. The instrument measured fragments. The shortest route was something they inferred.[1]

A handful of identical beads can make a long string. Its length says little about the difficulty of making it. The case that interested the team was a substance whose construction still took many different joins after every allowance for reuse, and which turned up in sufficient numbers to measure. One occurrence leaves a wide choice of explanations. To account for a population of copies, it pays to look for something that can turn out another when fresh material comes along. In the laboratory we have a familiar candidate in a cell with the machinery to produce a particular molecule.[1][2]

When Graham disclosed the labels, the result had an awkward feature worth taking on a space mission. In the mixtures examined, the high end of the estimated scale belonged to material of biological origin. Yet some samples touched by life never reached it. The bay sediment was one. Some Scotch whisky also failed to cross the study’s threshold. A distiller starts with work done by living things and selects what will come over in the still. The instrument gets the contents of the bottle at the end. It cannot recover every stage in the bottle’s history.[1]

Graham’s question has a second part. If an investigator can recognize what has been built, could she also say why it took shape *when it did*? What if the same process works at quantum, chemical, biological, social and technological scales? There were stars before planets, living things before engines, and engines before anyone had a reason to build a computer. Each took time to form. The question of this book is what set the pace.[1][2]

A mine on the Cumberland coast offers a hint. A steam engine pumped water out of a shaft and gave miners access to a coal seam. Some of the coal could fire the boiler. More coal came within reach of people able to build another engine. The interesting quantity was never the amount a single boiler could burn in an hour. It was the coal that could now come to the surface over the life of the mine. The owners even paid to send it underground a second time. We will come to the reason for that journey.[3]

That is the argument of this book in its most concrete form. A difference in temperature, height or chemical composition can supply work. Access depends on how the parts fit. A drawing of a pump can specify a relation between a piston and a rod that holds wherever the pump is built. I call such relations *Platonic*. The drawing can cross an ocean. Someone at the other end still has to supply a cylinder, a shaft worth draining and enough fuel for the first stroke. Every assembly has both aspects. A relation limits what the parts can do together. Matter supplies the means of doing it.

My thesis is that the universe races to dissipate available energy at the greatest sustained rate its conditions allow. In that sense I argue that energy always follows the steepest accessible gradient. A temperature difference can be large across a barrier that admits almost no flow. What matters is how fast each physically buildable route can actually produce entropy over the time it takes to form and operate. Energy can pass through several routes at once. The claim concerns their combined rate, including the work spent building a faster route. The second law requires nonnegative total entropy production for a macroscopic isolated system. Maximization across physics, chemistry, biology, society and technology is the wager of this book.[3][4]

On this account life appeared as soon as Earth could sustain it. Later, communities able to command fuel built machines that used it much faster than unaided bodies could. Two physicists arrived at the same relation between time and place in the same month once the instruments and mathematics were ready. Today people buy electricity to run AI, and some of its results bring more machines into use. A town that buys the heat from a roomful of computers will let us put numbers on what a piece of bare rock can do once the machines arrive.

Marshall’s index belongs to a particular set of rules for joining parts. There is no accepted scale on which a photon, a village and a computer can all be ranked by putting them through the same instrument. Graham’s procedure offers a discipline that travels better. Say what evidence the proposed history should leave. Risk an answer before turning over the label.
