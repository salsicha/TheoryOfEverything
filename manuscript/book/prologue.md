# Prologue — What If Life Is Everywhere?

Heather Graham sent the samples without their names. One came from the Murchison meteorite, another from bay sediment. Graham knew which was which. The people who would put them through a mass spectrometer had to give their answer before she told them.[1]

On Earth a biologist gets clues that an instrument on another world would lack. The green film lies where light and water meet. The bottle of whisky came from a distillery. A meteorite can contain organic molecules, too. What could the instrument recognize if all it got was the contents of a tube?[1][2]

Stuart Marshall, Cole Mathis and their colleagues broke molecules into fragments and used the patterns to estimate how many distinct joins it would take to build each molecule. A piece built once could be used again. They called the shortest count under those rules a molecular *assembly index*. A molecule that still took many different joins, and occurred in enough copies to measure, suggested that something had made it repeatedly. The high scores in the team's sample set came from material of biological origin. Some material that life had touched scored low. The bay sediment was one example. Distillation had left little in the whisky to tell the instrument about the yeast.[1][2]

Suppose life is everywhere it can get started. Then Graham's question acquires a second half. An instrument might tell us that living things made a sample. What law would make it inevitable that, on a suitable world, there would be living things there to make it? We know one inhabited planet. Taking widespread life as our starting assumption lets us ask what else we ought to find if the assumption is right.

When I wrote a Sudoku solver, the first empty square looked like a reasonable place to start. Six digits might fit. The program could fill half the board before finding out that its first move had been wrong. A few rows down, another square already had an answer. Here are two rows from one puzzle. Farther down the grid, the setter has put a five in the third square of row seven.[3]

~~~text
Row 1     8 2 ·  |  9 · 4  |  7 1 6
Row 4     7 1 6  |  8 · 3  |  9 · 4
~~~

The first row lacks a three and a five. The five in row seven rules out a five in the third column. Write three beside the two. The gap between nine and four must now take five. Follow that five down the column to row four. Its middle gap must take two, leaving five for the gap at the right. One entry several rows away settled four moves. No one had to try them out.[3]

We can give the same puzzle to a reader who has never seen its solution. The restrictions are there before anyone sees them. This is what *Platonic space* means in this book. The requirement that two fives cannot share a column holds on paper, on a screen and on the next copy of the puzzle. A list of all the digits one could write in every square makes the search look enormous. The four squares we just filled had one continuation between them. Other grids still demand guesses. Which grid we start with matters.[3]

A rock at the bottom of the Atlantic presents a harder question. Its pores have shapes. The molecules passing through them can make only certain bonds. Some products wash out. Others stick long enough to react with the next parcel of water. Two products that survive separately can do something together that neither could do alone. To keep doing it, they need fresh material. At a hydrothermal vent, water carrying hydrogen meets seawater of a different composition. Their meeting can supply chemical work day after day. Heat and spent material can leave the pore.[4]

Call this a *hierarchical entropy rejection funnel*. The four entries in the puzzle show what a constraint can eliminate. Crossing out a digit tells us nothing about heat. The pore adds the missing physics. A local arrangement can keep its order while energy disperses and entropy is exported to its surroundings. It can also leave behind a catalyst that changes which reaction comes next. The hierarchy lies in that inheritance. An atom binds, a molecule folds, a cell puts folded molecules to work. Each step starts with parts that an earlier step has already supplied. No step has the whole universe of conceivable arrangements open to it.[4][5]

At Saltom on the Cumberland coast, men sank a shaft toward coal while water entered the workings. An inch of water was a nuisance until it covered the working face. Then a seam was out of reach. Burning some coal in a steam engine could lift the water, put miners back at the face and bring far more coal to the surface. The owners even paid to send coal underground a second time. The reason for that journey will matter when we compare the pump with the other investments a mine could make.[6]

The book argues that a gradient and a narrowing set of physically accessible routes do more than permit change. Once the prerequisites hold, the next assembly forms by the quickest feasible route to the greatest rate of entropy production the system can sustain. That makes two predictions for Saltom. How soon could each available pump begin work? Over its working life, how much more coal could it get burned, counting fuel and time spent building it and the means to get coal to buyers? A quick start can buy a poor pump. The fastest start and the highest sustained return have to be checked separately.[5][6]

On that account a planet might take ages to cool and still acquire life in the first interval in which its chemistry could leave descendants. A cell would face another puzzle every time it made a protein. At the far end of the book, a computer connected to a Finnish town's heating system will give us a meter with which to ask what another level of assembly has done to the flow of energy. Before there were oceans, shafts or cells, what supplied the first differences that anything could exploit?
