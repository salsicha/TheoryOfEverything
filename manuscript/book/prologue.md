# Prologue — Assembly Theory and the Search for Life Beyond Earth

Heather Graham sent the samples without their names. One contained material from the Murchison meteorite. Another came from bay sediment. Graham knew which was which. The people who would put them through a mass spectrometer had to give their answer before she told them.[1]

Ordinarily a biologist gets to know where to look. A green film on a rock lies just where light and water meet. A bottle in a brewery comes with a fair presumption about yeast. An instrument sent to another planet might draw a sample through a tube from a place nobody has ever seen. It might find organic molecules. So did the people who examined Murchison after it fell in Australia. Carbon chemistry runs well beyond the reach of anything known to be alive. What, then, could the instrument recognize?[1][2]

Stuart Marshall, Cole Mathis and their colleagues proposed looking for evidence of work done more than once. In outline their procedure was simple. Break a molecule into fragments. From the pattern of fragments, estimate how many distinct joining operations it would take to build the original. In counting those operations, allow a piece already built to be used again. A molecule containing the same section four times should get credit for the fact that there is a shorter route to it. So should a molecule whose parts can themselves be made from common smaller parts. The least number of steps under the specified rules is its molecular *assembly index*. The team calibrated their estimate against molecules of known structure. The instrument measured fragments. The shortest route was something they inferred.[1]

A handful of identical beads can make a long string. Its length says little about the difficulty of making it. The case that interested the team was a substance whose construction still took many different joins after every allowance for reuse, and which turned up in sufficient numbers to measure. One occurrence leaves a wide choice of explanations. To account for a population of copies, it pays to look for something that can turn out another when fresh material comes along. In the laboratory we have a familiar candidate in a cell with the machinery to produce a particular molecule.[1][2]

When Graham disclosed the labels, the result had an awkward feature worth taking on a space mission. In the mixtures examined, the high end of the estimated scale belonged to material of biological origin. Yet some samples touched by life never reached it. The bay sediment was one. Some Scotch whisky also failed to cross the study’s threshold. A distiller starts with work done by living things and selects what will come over in the still. The instrument gets the contents of the bottle at the end. It cannot recover every stage in the bottle’s history.[1]

Graham had asked how much construction an unknown sample required. Her instrument could give the same answer for a molecule assembled in an hour as for one whose last reaction took a thousand years to occur. That leaves a question for a book that begins with the search for life. Could a planet spend ages preparing the conditions for life, then become a living world in the first interval those conditions held?[1][2]

At Saltom on the Cumberland coast, men sank a shaft toward coal while water came into the workings. Two rates decided how far they could go. So long as a pump lifted water faster than it came in, the men could work below. If the inflow overtook the pump, the water would rise. An inch at a time would do it. Once the water covered the working face, that coal was out of reach. A steam engine could lift the water and give the men back a place to stand and cut coal. Some of the coal they raised could fire its boiler. The owners even paid to send coal underground a second time. We will come to the reason for that journey.[3]

A difference in temperature, height or chemical composition can supply work. Whether it will turn a pump depends on how the parts fit. The piston has to travel far enough to give the rod its stroke. A builder can copy that relation from a drawing and build a pump on the other side of an ocean. This is the *Platonic* aspect of an assembly. The builder still has to supply a cylinder, a shaft worth draining and fuel for the first stroke.

At Saltom, burning some coal at the mine could get more coal burned across the district. The first shovelful fired the boiler. The pump opened a seam. How much sooner could a small engine start work than a large one, and how much coal could each bring to market over its working life? Count the years spent building each one and the coal that actually burns after it leaves the mine. The fastest start and the greatest sustained rate of burning might belong to different engines. If the universe takes the quickest feasible route to the greatest sustained rate of entropy production, the engine that wins on both counts should be the one that gets built. A district that could afford both and consistently chose the slower, lower-output route would be evidence against that prediction. The second law gives a direction to the change. To predict a maximum takes a comparison like this one.[3][4]

An extra inch of water could be an inconvenience on one shift and put a seam out of reach on the next. Dialectics calls such a change *quantity into quality*. On the early Earth, the threshold went the other way. Water had to cool, rock had to expose fresh surfaces, and reactions had to supply products that survived long enough to take part in others. A fresh current could wash away everything the last one had made. Once some of that chemistry could use the current to produce descendants, a new generation could go to work on the water that came through next.[4]

The same prediction puts the long wait for life in the time Earth took to become habitable. Once the rock, water and chemical energy could support reactions that left descendants, a lineage should have taken hold on the much shorter clock of the chemistry. A planet that stayed sterile through one suitable habitat after another would have a different history. The age of the oldest known fossil alone cannot tell the two histories apart.[4]

The hard part is to recognize the threshold before we know the outcome. A miner could measure the inflow and the capacity of a pump before risking the next shift. Can we use the rocks to date a habitat where chemistry could sustain heredity, then ask independently how soon life appeared there? Graham made her colleagues answer before she turned over the labels. We owe her the same discipline.[1][3][4]
