# Chapter 12 — Levinthal’s Paradox

*Part IV · Paradox*

Christian Anfinsen and his colleagues took apart an enzyme that cuts RNA. They could tell whether it was working by giving it RNA and measuring what it cut. The enzyme, ribonuclease, consisted of one chain folded back on itself. Four bonds braced parts of the chain together. The researchers broke those bonds and unfolded the chain. It lost its ability to cut. The amino acids were all still there, in the same order.[1]

Eight sites along the chain could pair to make the four braces. Take the first site. It has seven possible partners. Take the first site left over. It has five. Then three, then one. That makes 105 ways to fasten an object that has only four fastenings. The group let the bonds form again while the solution still favored an unfolded chain. The resulting mixture had about one percent of the activity of the original enzyme. Much of the material had closed up wrong.[1]

Change the order of events and the result changes. When the chain had the freedom to fold and the bonds could form in those conditions, the material recovered its ability to cut RNA. Even chains with scrambled bonds could regain activity when the bonds could exchange partners under conditions that favored the native fold. No one took hold of the eighth site to tell it where the third one lay. The sequence and its surroundings gave some arrangements an advantage before all four fastenings closed.[1]

## What did the count leave out?

The 105 pairings make a modest problem beside the number of ways the backbone can bend. Cyrus Levinthal asked in 1968 how a protein could reach a working shape in the time it actually took. Take a deliberately simple count. Give a chain of a hundred units three possible settings at each unit. That gives three to the hundredth power, about 5 × 10⁴⁷ arrangements. Even at a trillion complete trials a second, inspecting them all would take about 1.6 × 10²⁸ years. These are invented settings and a generous clock. The count exposes an assumption about how the chain spends its time.[2]

A part of the chain that folds back can make contacts that tend to hold it there. Parts already joined can bring other residues within reach. Water is a participant too. Burying some groups in the interior changes the free energy of the chain and the surrounding water. Different partial folds can lead to the same set of contacts. There are also arrangements from which the only way forward involves giving up contacts and trying again. The rules for movement give different statistical weights and different lifetimes to drawings that looked equivalent on paper.[3]

Robert Zwanzig, Attila Szabo and Biman Bagchi stripped the problem down to ask how much bias it would take. In their model each step could still go the wrong way. A modest energetic preference for locally correct structure was enough to cut the predicted search time drastically. The modelers had supplied both the moves and the bias. A chemist studying an actual sequence has to find out which contacts matter and whether an early contact helps or traps the chain. This is the physical meaning of a folding *funnel*. Many routes can give access to a much smaller set of states, with different chances of getting there and different costs for getting back out.[3]

## The time it takes to cross

Hoi Sung Chung and his colleagues attached fluorescent dyes to individual protein molecules. When two parts of a chain came closer together, the balance of light from the dyes changed. The researchers recorded the photons and used their arrival times to separate two intervals that an ordinary folding measurement runs together. How long does an unfolded chain wait before making a successful crossing? Once it sets out on that crossing, how long until it arrives?[4]

One of the proteins, a small domain called WW, folded at a rate of roughly ten thousand times a second under the conditions studied. Another, GB1, folded about once a second under its test conditions. Yet the actual crossing took about two microseconds in WW. For GB1 the team could put an upper limit of ten microseconds on it. The long wait in the second case had not bought a correspondingly long journey. A molecule could spend nearly the whole interval moving among shapes that belonged to the starting state.[4]

That distinction carries back to the young Earth. The time it took to supply a cool, persistent chemical habitat belongs to one question. How long a working lineage took to get started inside such a habitat belongs to another. Protein folding shows why the sheer number of shapes cannot settle either question. It also shows why the existence of a reachable destination cannot fix an arrival date. Two chains with short crossings kept very different clocks.[3][4]

The folded enzyme can sit in Anfinsen’s tube after it has equilibrated with its surroundings. Give it fresh RNA and a folded molecule can cut many successive molecules. In a cell, a whole system supplies material, uses products and replaces catalysts that wear out. Outside a cell, a mineral surface can also catalyze a reaction and a current can carry its products away. What would give a living assembly the advantage? If its share of the flow can feed descendants, the descendants can put more catalytic surfaces to work on the next parcel of water. The comparison turns on how much usable chemical energy each route discharges per unit time once the work of keeping the catalysts in operation is counted.[1][5]

The book’s claim is that life can sustain the highest accessible rate of entropy production along such a gradient. To choose between the mineral surface and a population feeding on the same inflow, we would need the composition of the outflow as well. Carbon carried away in a product may have plenty of chemical work left in it. A population could spread quickly and carry most of that capacity out with its cells. A persistent mineral route that dissipated more under the same constraints would count against the proposed optimum. The dyes told Chung when a chain folded. To tell whether life has taken the fastest route for dispersing an energy supply, we have to account for what leaves the habitat.[3][4][5]

A chain in a terrestrial laboratory can settle the question of how long one folded molecule takes to appear. If the same narrowing operates wherever planets supply suitable chemistry, a different question comes into view. How long would it take for us to hear from another world on which it worked?
