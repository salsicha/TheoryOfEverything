# Chapter 1 — From Nothing to Energy

In 1714 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz asked why there was anything at all. Earlier in the same short work he had explained how one living thing could come from another. A father could account for a son. Add the grandfather and the explanation went back a generation. Carry it back as far as one liked and the family still existed. What accounted for the existence of the whole series? Leibniz thought the answer had to be something whose existence required no further cause. He called it God.[1]

His question gives nothing an apparent advantage. A universe with matter in it seems to require an explanation. An empty space looks cheaper. Take away its matter, though, and someone could still say how far one end was from the other or how long a light signal would take to cross it. Even the sentence “once there was nothing” gives nothing a date and puts it at one end of a history. Leibniz wanted to know why there was a history at all.[1][2]

## What the empty apparatus held

Steve Lamoreaux could vary the distance between a metal-coated plate on a torsion pendulum and a curved surface facing it. The apparatus operated in a vacuum. As he brought the surfaces closer, an electrical feedback system had to exert a different force to hold the pendulum still. The signal varied with the separation. He reported the measurements in 1997.[3]

The calculation of the Casimir force starts with two surfaces, a distance between them and an electromagnetic field. Moving a surface changes the conditions the field must satisfy. Pumping air from the apparatus helped make a delicate measurement possible. The space he emptied still had properties he could change and a force he could measure. Even assigning a system zero energy requires saying which system it is and what sets the zero.[3]

Absolute nothingness would be a much stronger proposition. It would exclude matter, fields, relations and the possibility of their taking form. If that exclusion held without exception, one thing about reality would be settled absolutely. Nothing could ever come about. The book starts from a different premise about certainty. Some outcomes are open in reality itself. This is *ontological uncertainty*. Our ignorance about a result already fixed would leave the result as definite as ever.[2]

On that premise, absolute nothingness cannot exist. Its permanence would settle the very possibility of change. A field, even in its lowest-energy state, already gives us something whose arrangement can affect the work it takes to move a surface. Lamoreaux could bring the surfaces together or draw them apart. Why do so many changes in nature have a recognizable direction?[2][3]

## The air in Joule’s second vessel

In an experiment he reported in 1844, James Prescott Joule joined two copper vessels with a stopcock. He compressed air into one, pumped the other nearly empty and immersed both in the same bath of water. When he opened the stopcock, air rushed into the second vessel. His thermometer detected no net change in the temperature of the bath. Yet something had been spent. The pressure difference could have driven a piston. Closing the valve and opening it again would give him no second rush of air.[4]

Consider one collision in the ordinary mechanical description of the gas. Reverse the velocities just after the molecules collide and they retrace their paths. To send the whole gas back through the stopcock would take an extraordinary coordination of those paths. At equilibrium, a molecule of an ideal gas in two equal vessels has an even chance of being on either side. The chance that ten particular molecules are all on the original side is one in 1,024. Every additional molecule halves that chance again.[4][5]

Time itself has no preferred direction. Joule broke the symmetry between the two ends of the gas’s history when he confined almost all the air to one vessel. Once he opened the valve, the gas spread into the far more numerous arrangements available across both vessels. Its entropy rose. That direction of change is its *thermodynamic arrow of time*. Calling something the first event in the universe already assumes we know which way to read its history. Joule had a pump to account for the low-entropy end of his experiment. What could account for ours?[4][5]

Julian Barbour, Tim Koslowski and Flavio Mercati approached that question with a calculation. They followed a thousand particles attracting one another under Newton’s law of gravity. The equations gave neither direction of time a preference. The group passed through a point of minimum overall size. Following its history away from that point in either direction, the researchers found close pairs forming as other particles drew far apart. Their measure of clustering fluctuated but grew on both sides. Each side had a distinguishable direction of development. What they had calculated was the growth of gravitational structure. Explaining the entropy increase in our universe still requires an account of its remarkably smooth early distribution of matter.[5][6]

The loss of symmetry between the two temporal directions belongs beside two other asymmetries in the universe. The weak interaction distinguishes left from right. More matter than antimatter survived in the observable universe. In the thermal case, a low-entropy condition at one end of our history distinguishes the direction in which energy disperses. Each calls for its own physical explanation. In Joule’s apparatus the pressure difference tells us which way the air will go. The width of the opening helps determine how fast. In a cylinder of water heated from below, the water can change how much heat flows through it by organizing itself into a current.[4][5][7]
