# Chapter 1 — From Nothing to Energy

In Vienna, Olaf Nairz, Markus Arndt and Anton Zeilinger brought two silicon edges closer together in the path of a beam of molecules. Each molecule was a cage of seventy carbon atoms. With the edges 1.4 micrometers apart, a laser scanning the beam farther along found the molecules in a narrow band. The team reduced the gap to about a twentieth of that width. Fewer molecules got through. Those that did reached farther to either side.[1]

A strain gauge helped the researchers set the gap between the edges. A laser 1.33 meters beyond the slit ionized molecules as they passed through its light. By moving the laser across the beam and counting the ions at each position, the team could map where the molecules went.[1]

What did the narrower opening fix? Every molecule that passed through it had to be within the gap. Preparing a quantum wave within that narrow strip takes a range of sideways momenta. Farther along the beam, those momenta give the molecules a wider spread of arrival positions. Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle puts a lower bound on the product of the spreads in position and momentum along the same direction. Even the most precise preparation the theory permits leaves a spread in at least one of them.[1][2]

The team put the width of its detector’s response at ten micrometers, with a margin of half a micrometer. Better calibration could shrink that margin. This is *epistemic uncertainty*. It concerns how well someone knows a quantity. Even with an exactly calibrated detector, the narrow slit would still produce a spread. A complete quantum description predicts the distribution of possible results. On the reading of the uncertainty principle developed here, those alternatives belong to the molecule itself. Which outcome it will realize remains open even to someone who knows the preparation completely. This is *ontological uncertainty*. What would it take to empty the world of even that possibility?[1][2]

## What the empty apparatus held

The same principle has a consequence for energy. Consider a particle held near a resting position by a force like that of a spring. Confining it closer to the center lowers the energy stored in its displacement, but the wider spread in momentum raises its kinetic energy. Let it spread farther and the cost in displacement grows. The lowest-energy state strikes a balance. It has a definite energy above the bottom of the spring’s potential even though neither position nor momentum is perfectly sharp. This residual energy is called *zero-point energy*.[3]

A free quantum field can be described as a collection of such oscillators. Its vacuum is the state in which every mode has its lowest energy. There are no particles of that field present, yet measurements of the field over a small region can still give a spread of readings. The surfaces around it also matter. Steve Lamoreaux put a metal-coated plate on a torsion pendulum in a vacuum and brought a curved conducting surface close to it. An electrical feedback system held the pendulum still. As the gap changed, so did the force the system had to exert. Lamoreaux could pump the air out of the apparatus and still measure a consequence of what bounded that space.[3][4]

## Why there cannot be absolutely nothing

In 1714 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz asked why anything exists at all. A father can account for a son, and a grandfather for the father. Carry the explanation back as far as one likes and it still assumes the existence of the family. Leibniz wanted a reason for the whole series. A physicist who accounts for a force by starting with a quantum field still faces Leibniz’s question. Why is there a field to begin with?[4][5]

The uncertainty principle applies to a physical state that already exists. The argument of this book is that the indeterminacy it reveals is fundamental to existence itself. A physical possibility, on this view, is something real about the world. In Vienna, narrowing the gap changed how likely a molecule was to arrive near the edge of the beam. Those possibilities had measurable consequences. They would still have belonged to the preparation if the scientists had put their detector away.[1][2][6]

Absolute nothingness would exclude matter, fields, and even the possibility that either could take form. Even a world empty of particles in which a field *could* take form would already possess a real possibility. Remove that as well and one fact about reality would be settled without qualification. No physical outcome could occur. If indeterminacy is fundamental, such a complete determination is impossible. On that premise, **absolute nothingness cannot exist**.[2][5][6]

What does take form depends on the conditions. In Vienna two silicon edges narrowed the range of positions and widened the range of sideways momenta. In Lamoreaux’s vacuum the separation between two surfaces set the force his pendulum registered. A difference can also supply the work that produces a new arrangement. Heat a layer of water from below and the water itself can begin to turn.[1][4][7]
