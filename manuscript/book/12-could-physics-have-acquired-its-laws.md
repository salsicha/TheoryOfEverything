# Chapter 12 — Could Physics Have Acquired Its Laws?

*Part IV · Paradox*

In 1997 Adam Riess worked out how much matter the universe contained and got a negative number. In his notebook the answer came to minus 0.36 in units of the density required to make a particular model of the universe flat. His stars had exploded billions of years before any human being saw them. The negative sign came from a choice he had made at his desk.[1]

The explosions were type Ia supernovae. Astronomers could estimate how bright each had been from the way its light faded. Measure how faint it appears here and you have a distance. Measure how far its light has shifted toward the red and you have information about how much the universe has expanded during the journey. Riess had asked how much matter, through its gravitational attraction, would have slowed that expansion enough to fit the distances. He had set the cosmological constant to zero. The explosions came out too far away. A negative density was the calculation’s way of saying that the assumed slowing had the wrong sign. His team and a second team found evidence that expansion had been speeding up.[1]

A positive cosmological constant can account for that acceleration. Represent it as an energy density of empty space and an awkward problem appears. We can infer a value small enough that matter gathered into galaxies before accelerated expansion came to dominate. We have no generally accepted derivation of that value from the underlying physics. This is one of the sharpest instances of *fine-tuning*. It is tempting to read an unlikely number straight off the page. To call it unlikely, though, one must say which values could have occurred and how often. Expressing one measured density in different units can make its number as small or large as one likes.[1][2]

Steven Weinberg asked how large a positive vacuum energy could be before matter failed to gather into galaxies. Raphael Bousso and three colleagues later asked which values would give an observer access to the greatest total production of entropy. They varied the positive vacuum energy and held the other cosmological parameters fixed. Most of the entropy in their tally came from something quite ordinary. Dust grains absorbed starlight and returned the energy as more numerous, lower-energy infrared photons. The measured cosmological constant fell within the range their calculation favored.[2]

Where did the difference between the model universes come from? Around the observed value, the entropy produced per unit time in a given volume expanding with the matter changed relatively little. Change the vacuum energy and you change how much of that matter can ever exchange signals with an observer. Integrating over a larger accessible region can give a larger total even when each equal parcel works at much the same rate. This calculation gives a reason an observer might find a particular range of values. For the constants themselves to be assembled, something in the early universe must be capable of changing state.[2]

## A field that holds its history

We have a modest precedent. In the Standard Model the state of the Higgs field helps set the masses of the W and Z particles. Physicists detected a short-lived excitation of the field in 2012. The excitation decayed. The relation that gives those particles their masses endured. A property that might have been entered once and for all beside the name of each particle instead depends on the state of something they share. The model still takes parameters as given. The precedent concerns the kind of explanation available when a research program succeeds.[3]

The proposal in this book goes further. If there is genuine ontological uncertainty, an apparent choice among numbers written at the beginning could be a history in which some relations became definite together. Constraints would narrow the physically accessible combinations. Symmetry breaking could give a region one definite state out of several the equations permit. Material shaped by the new state could interact with its surroundings in ways that renewed the conditions for the same relation to operate elsewhere. A self-renewing organization of that kind would be the cosmic counterpart to autopoiesis. In its strongest form the proposal says that the effective constants we inherit belong to arrangements that sustain the highest accessible rate of energy extraction. A number inked into a table has no metabolism. The work would have to be done by fields or other physical degrees of freedom whose relations the number expresses.

How much of this chain do we know how to build? Robson Christie and colleagues considered a field that could occupy either of two states during expansion. As a barrier grew, some regions in their calculation could remain in the higher-energy state. Interactions with the environment could suppress subsequent escape. Information about which state the region occupied had spread beyond the region, a process called *decoherence*. This gives an intelligible way for a physical history to persist. The calculation supplied the possible states and the rules of interaction at the outset. The observations available today leave open even whether our own electroweak vacuum is metastable.[4]

Take two regions of the same model with the same starting matter, coupling to the environment and available field states. From the barrier and the fluctuations one could estimate how often a region enters each state and how long it stays there. Now follow equal initial portions of matter for equal intervals on an internal clock. Which state lets them dissipate more free energy? Does that flow in turn help keep the field there or establish the same state nearby? A high barrier could preserve the slower state for the entire interval. Finding that result would favor an explanation by trapping. To explain a fine-tuned constant by maximum entropy production, the faster route must have a physical way to prevail.[2][4]

## A clock inside the experiment

There is a prior difficulty in saying the laws accumulated *over time*. Where did the time for their accumulation come from? In Turin, Ekaterina Moreva and her colleagues assigned one of two photons the job of a clock. They prepared the photons so that their polarizations were entangled. One set of measurements tested a property of the pair as a whole. It stayed the same through a joint transformation. Another set sorted observations of one photon by the reading of the other. Against different clock readings the partner showed different polarizations. Both accounts could come from the same pair.[5]

Don Page and William Wootters had proposed the principle behind this demonstration. A system can have a stationary description as a whole and still contain a physical clock correlated with changes in the rest. One need not give an observer outside the pair a watch in order to define the internal question. Moreva’s team certainly used laboratory time to prepare photons and read instruments. Its experiment gives an example of what *time from within* might mean. It supplies neither a birth date for the universe nor an explanation for our ability to remember yesterday.[5]

Did time emerge from symmetry? A symmetry means some change of description leaves specified relations intact. The pair retained a global property under a transformation while an internal comparison disclosed change. A broken symmetry could also distinguish alternatives which older rules treated alike. Deriving the *direction* of time demands something more. The gas that spreads across an empty vessel started confined to one end. A camera can record its spreading because it began with room to acquire a record. In cosmology an account of the arrow must explain the exceptionally low entropy of the early universe, including its gravitational state. Writing down a rate of energy extraction already presupposes a clock by which rates can be compared.[5][6]

These difficulties give the fine-tuning proposal a research task. Derive an observable relation between *dimensionless* quantities from a mechanism that limits which ones can form together. Specify what would keep the relation in force, how rival states would dissipate energy under the same conditions, and what trace a rival history would leave. A theory that can accommodate every possible measurement afterward has gained little on the original list of constants. A theory that tells an astronomer where two measurements must agree before she takes the second has taken a risk.

Riess could cross out a premise in his notebook because the numbers on the sky had refused to meet it. A history of laws should one day put an observer in a position to do the same.
