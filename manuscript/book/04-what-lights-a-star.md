# Chapter 4 — What Lights a Star?

*Part I · Physics*

A dark line cuts across the narrowest part of an hourglass in an image from the James Webb Space Telescope. Above and below it, dust glows in infrared light. Behind it lies the young object called L1527. The line marks a disk seen almost edge on. Light from the forming star escapes above and below the disk and illuminates cavities in the surrounding cloud. The place that looks darkest in the picture is where much of the work is going on.[1]

A cloud of gas has several ways to remain a cloud. Random motions of its particles provide pressure. Turbulent motions and magnetic fields can help support it too. If a region comes to contain enough mass within a small enough volume for gravity to overcome support, gas begins to fall inward. That changes the pressure. Material gains speed as it falls, collides and heats up. The tendency that started the collapse is producing a tendency that can resist it.[1]

The gas has to get rid of energy if it is to settle into a tighter arrangement. Radiation carries some away. Once the center becomes dense enough to trap radiation, the temperature rises and a compact object can persist. Rotation matters along the way. Gas carrying angular momentum cannot all fall straight to the middle. A disk gives material a way to orbit while angular momentum moves outward and some material moves in. Some also leaves in outflows. The cavities visible around L1527 are part of that history. A picture of a star forming includes matter that had to get out of the way.[1]

Here gravitational energy comes before nuclear energy. A protostar can light up the dust around it while still depending on contraction and infall for much of its luminosity. To sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion the core has to reach temperatures and densities at which enough nuclei react. Two positively charged nuclei repel each other. Gravity confines a large supply of them for a long time. Their thermal motions and quantum tunneling give some encounters a route across the barrier. Once enough reactions occur, the nuclear binding energy released can replace energy radiated away at the surface.[2]

An object with too little mass follows part of the same history and goes no farther. A brown dwarf can form in a collapsing cloud and radiate heat. Some can burn deuterium for a time. It lacks the mass for sustained ordinary hydrogen burning. Put the mature star and the brown dwarf side by side and the difference may look like one of brightness. Follow the fuel and there is a difference in what each object can keep doing. The threshold separates two futures that gravity alone did not specify.[1][2]

## Light under a mountain

In Italy the Borexino collaboration looked for one of the first products of fusion in the Sun by putting a vessel of exceptionally clean liquid under the rock of Gran Sasso. Sunlight would have been a nuisance. So were cosmic rays and traces of ordinary radioactivity. The signal they wanted was a faint flash from an electron disturbed by a passing neutrino. Nearly every neutrino passed through the entire apparatus without interacting. A radioactive impurity in the liquid, on the other hand, was already inside the shield.[2]

Near the start of the Sun’s main fusion chain two protons can make a deuterium nucleus, a positron and a neutrino. The neutrino carries news of the reaction straight out of the core. The energy that works its way outward through hot, opaque matter takes a much longer route. In 2014 the collaboration reported the low-energy signal from that first reaction. To separate it from other flashes the team had to account even for two background events arriving close enough together to look like one. There is more than one way to make a detector light up. The shape of the distribution mattered more than any particularly handsome event.[2]

What has been assembled? First, gas under gravity made a site at which further kinds of binding could occur. Nuclear reactions then helped sustain the conditions that made them possible. Pressure can support a star against contraction. A change in the core temperature can alter the reaction rate, expansion and subsequent cooling. This is a form of regulation with no separate governor and no blueprint for making a daughter star. Calling it self-regulation tells us how one star lasts. Calling it reproduction would oblige us to find an offspring and a mechanism of inheritance.[1][2]

Fusion does more than keep a light on. A succession of stars has altered what subsequent clouds can contain. Hydrogen burning builds helium. Later stages in suitable stars build carbon and oxygen and other nuclei. Stellar winds and explosions can return material to space. The next cloud to collapse then inherits chemical options beyond those open in a universe made chiefly of hydrogen and helium. Different elements have different histories. The energetic advantage of fusing light nuclei does not extend up the entire periodic table. Neutron capture and violent stellar events have their own roles in the heavier inventory.[3]

This gives *higher assembly* a precise meaning in the history of a planet. A mineral that includes oxygen was out of reach until something made oxygen nuclei. Water could condense only after there was both oxygen to bind and an environment cool enough for the resulting molecules to collect. An iron-bearing rock and a liquid ocean together can drive chemistry neither can carry out alone. The early enrichment of a cloud did not ordain the formation of a particular ocean. It changed which oceans were possible.

A statement that stars favor greater extraction can be tested at the right level. A self-gravitating body that ignites has access to a store of nuclear energy a lighter neighbor cannot sustain. It can process material and radiate for far longer than contraction alone would allow. The fact that a very massive star can use its fuel faster and die sooner is a reason to specify the time interval. Power today, total energy released, and the capacity left for the next generation can give different rankings. The camera at L1527 and the detector under Gran Sasso are valuable because they measure parts of those histories independently.[1][2]

One parcel of the next history begins when seawater gets into exposed rock on the floor of the Atlantic. The water comes back out warm and rich in hydrogen. In the absence of daylight there are still animals to be found where it emerges.
