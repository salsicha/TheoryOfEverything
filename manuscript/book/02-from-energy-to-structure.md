# Chapter 2 — From Energy to Structure

Stephan Weiss and Guenter Ahlers tilted a cylinder of water. Its bottom plate was warmer than its lid. Thermometers at different heights around the wall told them which side the warm water was climbing and where the cold water came down. Sometimes a single circulation roll filled the cylinder. Sometimes one roll sat above another. A small tilt favored the single roll. The water carried a little more heat that way.[1]

Give a layer of still water a warm bottom and a cool top and it can carry heat by conduction alone. In water well above its temperature of maximum density, a parcel warmed from below expands and becomes buoyant. As it rises it can lose heat to its surroundings and lose its advantage. Viscosity also resists the motion. Increase the temperature difference far enough, at a given depth, and the upward motion grows before those losses can stop it. Cooler water descends to take the parcel’s place. A layer that had transported heat while standing still can now transport it by circulating.[1][2]

Something in the whole arrangement determines what each parcel can do. The water must stay inside the vessel. What goes up must leave room for what comes down. The depth helps set how far a warm parcel can travel before it gives up much of its heat. These are constraints in what the book calls *Platonic space*. They rule out whole classes of motion. There is room for a returning current in a roll. There is no way for all the water in a closed cylinder to rise at once.[1][2]

A single five on a Sudoku board can do a similar piece of reasoning for the solver. A row lacks a three and a five. One of its two empty squares already has a five in its column. That square must take the three, which settles the other square too. Several choices disappear before anyone tries them. The water has something the paper lacks. A maintained temperature difference supplies the work, and the motion of one parcel helps set the conditions for the next.[2][3]

## What keeps turning and what stays frozen

The convection roll is a *dissipative structure*. It maintains its form while heat passes through and the water continually loses mechanical energy to friction. Bring the plates to the same temperature and the circulation runs down. If the original temperature difference returns, another roll can form. Its rising side may be on the opposite wall.[1][2]

Now let water give up heat to surroundings cold enough for ice to grow. A small ordered cluster can melt back into the liquid. If the cluster grows large enough under the prevailing conditions, adding molecules to the crystal lowers the free energy enough to pay the cost of maintaining a surface between ice and water. Freezing releases latent heat, which the surroundings must take up for freezing to continue. Once formed, a grain of ice can keep its lattice in a cold vessel whose temperature is the same at both ends.[4]

This is *conservative self-assembly*, also called static self-assembly. A crystal can persist under equilibrium conditions. The convection roll had to earn its continued motion from a flow. Both depend on allowed arrangements of the parts. Cooling and continued heating select quite different relations among the same water molecules. Even a snow crystal with six equivalent directions of growth has a record of local conditions in the unequal lengths of its branches.[2][4]

Weiss and Ahlers could also distinguish the rate of heat transport from the time the water spent in each pattern. In one of their comparisons a single roll carried about 1.6 percent more heat than two. At the low end of the range they studied the water nonetheless spent more time in the two-roll state. A pattern may carry heat quickly once it has formed and still take a long time to reach. Any claim about the fastest route has to account for both clocks.[1]

## When a boundary does work

The tilted cylinder offers a precise meaning for *downward causation*. Tilt the whole vessel and a particular parcel of water is more likely to find itself in the rising arm of one long roll. Each parcel moving that way helps carry heat to the top. The roll exists because the water moves, and the return path through the roll changes where a parcel can move next. The thermometers recorded the difference that made to the current.[1]

There is a way to change a boundary even for a field with no ordinary particles present. In a superconducting circuit, Christopher Wilson and his colleagues changed the electrical conditions at one end of a transmission line so fast that it acted like a moving mirror. Pairs of microwave photons emerged. The pump that varied the boundary supplied the energy. A field in its ground state under fixed conditions cannot power a machine that returns everything else to its starting state after every turn.[5][6]

## How a region could inherit a constant

Historical materialism asks how a relation people encounter as a fact came to hold. A buyer sees the price of a yard of cloth. It takes a history of production and exchange to explain what the weaver can charge. A physical constant raises a harder version of that question. What event could establish a value, and how could places now billions of light-years apart inherit it?[7]

In 2019 Gian Francesco Giudice, Alex Kehagias and Antonio Riotto calculated what would happen on either side of a bubble forming in the early universe. Inside its wall the energy of empty space dropped. The same event shifted a quantity that helps determine the masses of the particles that carry the weak force. Wherever the wall passed, it left the same pair of values behind.[8]

In their calculation a field stores energy in steps. A whole number counts the units of flux in a region. Forming a bubble wall costs energy. Once a large enough bubble forms, its interior holds one unit less, and the energy released as the interior grows drives the wall outward. Another bubble can form inside the first. If the last transition triggers a period of rapid expansion called inflation, one patch can grow large enough to contain the observable universe. Stars billions of light-years apart could then inherit some of the same physical constants from a single event.[8]

The flux is a property of a region. When it changes, a particle anywhere inside the new region encounters a different Higgs background once that background settles. Here the proposed downward influence has a measurable consequence, the mass a particle acquires there. In a separate calculation, Lev Kofman and his colleagues followed energy in the other direction. A moving field produced particles. As the field moved farther, increasing their mass cost energy and slowed it down. On returning it could make more particles, which helped hold it near the same value.[8][9]

Put the two operations together and there is a possible history for a constant. A collective structure alters which states are accessible to a field, energy leaves in excitations, and the material produced helps stabilize the structure on which later particles depend. A changing early universe would supply the drive that the electronic pump supplied in Wilson’s laboratory. To carry the argument through, a single model would have to supply that drive, select the values and recover them from the physics. Chapter 13 follows one such prediction to the light from a distant supernova. A different question comes first. Even when every region obeys the same rules, what makes their histories diverge?[5][8][9]
