# Chapter 13 — Fine tuning paradox

In the fall of 1997 Adam Riess used a set of exploding stars to estimate how much matter the universe contained. On the scale in his notebook, one meant enough matter to make space flat. His answer was minus 0.36. He had let the amount of matter vary in his calculation and fixed the cosmological constant at zero.[1]

The stars were type Ia supernovae. From the way each one brightened and faded, Riess could estimate how much light it had emitted. From how faint it looked, he could work out how far the light had come. The distant explosions lay farther away than he expected in a universe whose expansion had been slowing under the pull of matter. His team and a second group found evidence that the expansion had begun to accelerate. A small positive cosmological constant could account for it. The minus sign in the notebook had called into question the zero Riess put there himself.[1]

Where did the positive value come from? As a contribution to the energy density of empty space, it affects the expansion even after matter has thinned out. A much larger positive value could have driven galaxies’ raw material apart before gravity gathered it. That sensitivity is one reason people speak of *fine-tuning*. In the model from Chapter 2, a change in a field gave a whole expanding region the same physical constants. Riess’s result asks more of that account. If the same event helped set the mass of the W particle, could a measurement in a particle accelerator have warned him against putting zero in his calculation?[2][3][4]

The constants could have been assembled through a *hierarchical entropy rejection funnel*. What gets assembled is the physical state that gives a relation its measured value. At a vent, water carries heat away while a mineral wall keeps a catalyst. The next parcel of water encounters a different chemistry. In the early universe, a field could lose energy to particles while a new arrangement of fields persisted across a region of space. Everything that formed there would start with the relations that arrangement had established. Two quantities measured today in very different ways give this idea some precision.[3][4]

## Two constants from one change

At CERN, physicists produce brief excitations of the Higgs field by colliding protons. The excitation decays. The background value of the field endures. The W and Z particles that carry the weak force acquire their masses through their interactions with that background. Its value sets what physicists call the *weak scale*. An astronomer infers the vacuum energy from the expansion of space. A particle physicist measures the weak scale in the laboratory. How could those numbers have a common history?[1][4]

In a model developed by Gian Francesco Giudice, Alex Kehagias and Antonio Riotto, an early region of space contains energy in another field. The amount of *flux*, a quantity that labels the state of that field, comes in whole units. For the region to shed one unit, a bubble has to form. Its surface is a charged membrane. Inside the bubble the flux is one unit lower. The energy difference across its surface drives the bubble’s growth. The cost of making the surface helps determine how long a region waits for a bubble to form.[5]

One count now does two jobs. Before the Higgs field takes on a nonzero background, the energy density stored in the flux grows as the square of the count. Three units store nine shares of energy per unit volume. Two store four. Each unit also shifts the parameter that governs the Higgs background by a fixed amount. A drop from three to two therefore changes both quantities together. A table made by pairing every imaginable weak scale with every imaginable vacuum energy would include combinations this field could never produce. The relation between the allowed values is a constraint in what the book calls *Platonic space*. A membrane forming somewhere is how a region moves from one allowed pair to the next.[5]

After enough steps, a single jump in flux changes where the Higgs field can settle. A nonzero background now has the lowest energy. The W and Z acquire mass there. A collision must supply that much energy to make one of them. The Higgs background also changes the vacuum energy. Here a constant is an outcome at one level of assembly and an input at the next. For the values to look universal to us, one region with that pair must grow large enough to contain the universe we can see, or regions with different histories must converge on the same pair.[4][5]

## Where the released energy goes

A region can reach a new vacuum and still be nearly empty. Earlier expansion may have diluted the particles it once contained. Hyun Min Lee worked out how the last membrane transition could also populate the region. In one version of his model an additional field has settled at the value where its energy is lowest. When the flux changes, that minimum moves. The field starts out at its old value, now with energy to spare. It oscillates about the new minimum. Through its coupling to the Higgs field, it can turn the energy of that motion into pairs of Higgs particles. Their decays and subsequent collisions distribute energy through a hot population of particles.[6]

The region keeps the new flux while energy leaves the collective motion and spreads among many particles. As the particles come to a common temperature, they carry the entropy produced in reheating. This is the *rejection* in the funnel. The receiving particles are part of the universe. A hot enough plasma can temporarily restore electroweak symmetry. When it cools, the retained flux still helps determine which Higgs background forms. The same transition can leave a weak scale, a contribution to the vacuum energy and matter to which those values apply.[5][6]

The step that sets the weak scale in Lee’s calculation changes the vacuum energy by far more than the small amount inferred from the sky. To leave that little energy over, another contribution must nearly cancel the flux energy. Lee assumes that cancellation. The size of a flux unit and the strength of the coupling between fields also enter the calculation as given. If these quantities were themselves assembled, an earlier level of the funnel would have to set them.[5][6]

## What could hold a value in place?

Lev Kofman and his colleagues calculated a way for newly formed particles to act back on a field. In their model, particles are light when a moving field passes a particular value. The passage produces some of them. As the field moves on, those very particles grow heavier. The energy for their extra mass has to come from the field’s motion. The field slows, turns and passes the same value again. This pass makes more particles, which exert a stronger pull on the next pass. A value the field initially crossed at speed becomes one around which it can be trapped.[7]

By producing particles, Kofman’s field changes its own chances of staying near one value. The trapping can weaken as particles disappear or expansion dilutes them. A state can also last behind a barrier. In a calculation by Robson Christie and his colleagues, interactions with the surroundings suppressed quantum tunneling and could keep a field in a *metastable* state, one with a lower-energy alternative. This effect of decoherence could help a region retain an earlier choice. For the cosmic funnel to be *autopoietic*, it must also have a way to rebuild what steadies the field. A disturbance could restart particle production and replenish the population that draws the field back. Finding that return path for a field that sets the constants remains an open problem.[6][7]

The funnel hypothesis predicts that, among configurations reachable with the same supply of free energy, the quickest route to the highest sustained rate of entropy production will prevail. Selection could work this way if the route that disperses energy fastest also replenishes the particles that steady the field before expansion carries them apart. A region could then recover its value after a disturbance that extinguishes other arrangements. To tell whether this works, follow the field and everything it heats over the same interval for each reachable state. If a lower-rate state consistently recovers while a rival could sustain a higher rate under those same conditions, maximum entropy production has picked the wrong value.[3][6][7]

Could the clock for that comparison also arise inside the universe? In Turin, Ekaterina Moreva and her colleagues used the polarization of one photon as a clock for its entangled partner. A joint property stayed fixed, yet sorting the readings by the first photon revealed change in the second. That supplies a sense in which an unchanging whole can contain a succession of events. To give the succession a thermodynamic direction, the field histories still begin with energy concentrated in a form that can disperse.[8]

Billions of years later, a grain of dust absorbs starlight and emits the energy as a larger number of infrared photons. Each carries less energy. Raphael Bousso and his colleagues calculated how much entropy processes like this could produce in the region accessible to an observer. They varied the vacuum energy. The range their calculation favored included the measured value, in large part because enough matter stayed in reach while stars shone. Their result concerns entropy added up over a history. A dust grain can tell us something about the consequences of the cosmological constant. To explain its origin, the relevant feedback must have operated while different field states were still accessible.[2][9]

The test would start with a measurement made at CERN and end with a prediction for Riess’s supernovae. Express the weak scale and the vacuum energy in gravitational units, so their numerical ratios are independent of our yardsticks. Fix the early field rules from other evidence. If the funnel selects a narrow set of pairs, measuring the weak scale should narrow the range of vacuum energies before anyone consults the sky. Does the value left by that history give the distances Riess actually measured?[1][4][5][6]
