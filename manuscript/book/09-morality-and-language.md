# Chapter 9 — Morality and Language

*Part III · Technology*

In April 1789 members of Parliament received a drawing of a ship. Someone had taken off the deck so the reader could look straight down. Along the narrowing sides lay rows of people, their bodies turned to fit the hull. A section showed the clearance above their heads. Many people who saw the sheet could never have named the vessel. In the London printing its name, *Brooks*, occurs partway through a paragraph.[1]

A merchant could look up the same ship in a register. There her name mattered. She had first gone in as *Brook*, then as *Brooks*. The register could tell a subscriber which vessel he was dealing with. Lloyd’s Register had developed a compact language for the purpose. In its surviving 1764 register the compilers used vowels to grade the hull and the letters G, M and B to grade equipment as good, middling and bad. The symbols subsequently changed. The problem they solved remained. A man in one port had to decide whether to trust a vessel he might never see in another.[1][2]

Removing information made the register useful. Checking the soundness of a hull was expensive. Once someone else had done it a merchant could consult a line of print. He could compare dozens of ships on the same terms. He would learn much less about the experience of anyone shut below deck. A sound hull might give a merchant confidence in the very voyage whose conduct should have made other people want to stop it. One of the most consequential choices in making a medium is deciding which question a stranger will be able to ask of it.

## A ship drawn to scale

In late 1788 William Elford and his colleagues in Plymouth had obtained a different kind of information. A naval officer, Captain Parrey, had measured nine vessels engaged in the slave trade. The Plymouth group used the dimensions of the Liverpool ship to draw a plan. In London the abolition committee added views. James Phillips printed the result and the committee sent it to MPs and others. People copied it again, rearranging pieces and sometimes cutting much of the accompanying text. Parliamentary readers who disagreed about almost everything could inspect the same few feet of deck on the same sheet of paper.[1]

A naval measurement had changed employers. A measurement could tell someone how much a ship would hold. In the hands of Elford and the abolition committee the width all but instructed the eye to do a second calculation. How much room did one person have to lie down? The rows answered in units a reader carried around without having to consult a table. A body had shoulders. A person who could lie on one side might wish to turn over. The men who made the print could use the reader’s knowledge of that movement without having to tell the reader what to feel.

A drawing that could cross the country for the cost of a sheet of paper also exacted a price. No one in it has a name. Its orderly rows can be mistaken for a picture of a particular voyage, down to the number and posture of the people on board. It was constructed from dimensions for an argument. In his autobiography, published in 1789, Olaudah Equiano described a crossing on another vessel. He recalled the heat and foul air and the difficulty of turning over once the ship filled. People sickened. People died. He could say what it was like to have to remain in that space after the reader had been free to put down the print. His account also gave a name and a subsequent life to someone the drawing would have represented by an outline.[3]

One recipient of the drawing could refuse to back a voyage. Another could let her neighbors see what she had seen, then ask them to sign their names to a petition. Both began with one sheet and the same law. The second response could put the evidence into more hands than the printer had originally reached. It could also fail at the first door. The material change depended on the next recipient doing something that the last recipient alone could not do, and on enough of those actions reaching someone with power over a sailing. That is what it means here to call moral judgment a communication technology.[1][4]

There is a dated choice in the record. In 1792 the Commons considered abolition and settled on a gradual end to the trade. The resolution brought no immediate prohibition. A British shipowner could still undertake a voyage, seek another cargo or leave a ship in port. *Brooks* continued to carry enslaved people until 1804. The 1807 Act later changed which ventures could operate legally under British authority. What the campaign assembled in the intervening years was a capacity to change the terms under which other people could organize a voyage. One such change could govern ships whose owners had never seen the print.[1][4]

The two sides of the vote make different predictions. While the trade remained lawful, the register could reduce the cost of finding a ship acceptable to investors. Under an enforced prohibition that same entry could establish a hull’s condition but could no longer make the voyage lawful. Continued trade, delayed abolition and immediate prohibition each left different uses open to the same ships, docks and provisions. The physical comparison turns on what those resources could carry over the same years. If a port could refit its ships for paying cargo, that route could sustain a flow of goods that idle hulls could never carry. Refitting took time and cargo required buyers. Enslaved people’s labor, the use of ships elsewhere and the fuel used in enforcement also belong in any wider account. The dates establish a real change in what people could do. They leave the ranking of those physical histories open. People had grounds to end the trade whatever that ranking might be.[1][3][4]

## What a second opinion is worth

Confidence also travels. The register economized on inspection only so long as users could learn how much reliance to place on its marks. In a much smaller setting Bahador Bahrami and his colleagues arranged for two people to see patterns on screens. On each trial one of two displays contained a patch of higher contrast. Each observer picked the interval in which it had appeared. On trials where the partners disagreed, they had to reach a joint decision. One might have seen a difference at once. The other might have been a long time choosing.[5]

With similar visual sensitivity and a means to communicate, pairs could do better than either member alone. With a large difference in sensitivity, a pair could do worse than its better observer. Having a second opinion helped to the extent that each person could convey the strength of the evidence behind a choice, and the strength conveyed meant something comparable to the partner. Both people could be sincere. Confidence is information which changes the weight someone else gives an observation. Someone who speaks with assurance on every trial eventually ceases to transmit that information.[5]

## What a listener can pass on

Simon Kirby, Hannah Cornish and Kenny Smith sat people in front of a screen and taught them names for objects moving across it. The shapes, colors and movements varied. A learner saw names for only some combinations, then had to name the whole set. The next learner saw a sample of the names the previous one had produced. A word that stopped with its inventor was gone from the language the group was making.[6]

One way to make the task easy was to use a single label for several objects. Across generations distinctions disappeared. A successor could remember the words and still have no way to tell a listener which object was meant. The researchers changed what got passed down. In a second experiment they kept ambiguous labels out of the examples the next person learned. Now a word had to be learnable and distinguish its object. Participants began passing on pieces of words that marked recurring features. Someone who had never seen a particular combination could put a name together for it.[6]

The experiment gives a reason why a form of speech can outlive its speakers. A child can hear only a fraction of the things other people will one day ask her to say. If a distinction recurs in words she already knows, she can use it on the occasion she missed. The same restriction also explains a failure. When nothing required the learners to preserve differences, an easy-to-repeat vocabulary could lose the capacity to tell one moving object from another.[6]

A petition has to survive a different passage. A person who heard Equiano could remember an individual voyage. A demand to end the trade could apply to the next voyage and to a ship whose name neither person knew. It also named an act that a member of Parliament could vote on. In 1792 even the word *gradual* mattered. Agreement on the evil could leave another sailing within the law. Words gave people a way to carry a moral judgment from one encounter into another. What people undertook to do together depended on which words they carried.[1][3][4]

A register built to communicate confidence in ships and a print built to induce moral judgment imposed different constraints on the same material. What each omitted helped it travel. Each offered different strangers a way to act together. Each could mislead in ways characteristic of its form. For a reader looking up an insured ship, leaving that name out would spoil the entry. Copies of the abolitionist drawing could lose it and still make their point. A reader could recognize what was being done to people on a ship whose name she never learned.
