# Chapter 7 — The Observer Who Makes Mistakes

*Part II · Humans*

Charles Darwin wrote down unwelcome facts. He explained the practice in his autobiography. An observation that favored his theory could be trusted to stick in his head. One that ran against it had a way of vanishing. The notebook gave the objection a second chance. It also left us evidence that Darwin knew something about the instrument with which he was attempting to understand nature, namely his own attention.[1]

Sterile worker ants gave him a particularly stubborn objection. A worker might have jaws different from those of the fertile parents that produced her. She could leave no daughters to inherit an improvement in those jaws. Frederick Smith supplied driver ants from one West African nest. Darwin dissected workers of different sizes. John Lubbock used a camera lucida to draw what he saw, looking at the specimen and the drawing surface together. Between the smallest and largest workers they found intermediate forms.[1]

Darwin argued that parents could leave more descendants when the workers they produced helped their colony. A trait could be favored through the success of a family even when the individual bearing it never had offspring. Whether every aspect of that argument was right is a separate matter. What interests us here is what Darwin did before he examined a jaw. He had adopted a rule for protecting the inquiry from a predictable weakness in the inquirer. An ant could have a property that surprised him. His own memory could also have a property that surprised him, and learning it changed what he did about the ant.[1]

## Knowing where one is looking

My definition of intelligence is **a hierarchical and recursive self-modeling system**. A lower level estimates something in the world. Another estimates something about how the first estimate was made, where attention went, what evidence was missed, where an error is likely. When that knowledge changes the way the next estimate is made, the system can put its own operation among the things it learns about. Darwin did not need a complete theory of memory to put a pencil between himself and a mistake.

This definition asks more than selecting a successful response. Walter’s tortoise could approach a charger on a low battery and avoid it on a full one. The use of an internal signal can be demonstrated by disconnecting the battery circuit. To find a self-model, look for a representation of the system’s own activity that can be used to revise activity again. There are degrees of such ability. A model of where one is looking can be useful long before anyone can write an autobiography.[2]

Andrew Wilterson and Michael Graziano gave an artificial player a paddle, a falling ball and a noisy field of view. A small area of the field could be made clear. The player could move this window to see the ball, then move its paddle to catch it. It also received a separate display of the location of its own attention. After training, the researchers took away the second display. The window still worked. The player could still move the paddle. Its catches suffered.[2]

That removal makes the inference better than pointing to a machine that won a game. The player had used a signal about what *it* was doing to see. There are further tests we should want before calling a machine intelligent in the stronger sense used here. Can it tell that its usual way of looking fails in a changed environment? Can it change that policy and carry the change into another task? A single score can rise because someone supplied a better sensor. Recording the adjustment the player makes to its own search would tell us more.

A brain supplies many opportunities for this kind of return. Nikola Markov’s group traced connections running in both directions between levels of the macaque visual cortex. Information from a lower area can alter a higher estimate, and signals can travel back. The anatomy identifies roads on which a correction could travel. Knowing that two towns share a road does not tell us what message a particular traveler carried. The separate records of the ant observation and Darwin’s rule give a functional example of the sort of traffic we are looking for.[2]

## When the instrument is a person

The return can pass through other people. In Nabenchauk, Mexico, researchers filmed a young weaver learning at a loom. An experienced weaver could put her hands to the threads while the learner continued holding her place. Later learners called for different amounts of help. One question for an instructor is whether a thread has gone wrong. Another is whether the learner is ready to see and correct that error herself. Help given a moment too soon can finish the cloth while depriving the learner of the chance to find out what she missed.[3]

The Jacquard loom offers the other side of the comparison. A pattern embodied in a chain of punched cards can select threads accurately for thousands of passes. In Lyon, J.-L. Moulin prepared a squared design for Carquillat’s woven portrait of Jacquard. He used brown to stand for black silk because he needed to see the marks on the drawing. The loom required the distinctions in the pattern. The draftsman needed to know which distinctions he could still see after he had covered the square with ink. A change of color for his own benefit could preserve the desired color in the silk.[3]

This suggests how a self could emerge from the recursive activity of a mind. Memories of success and failure include information about one persisting source of those events. A person can consult that history, redirect attention and add what happens next to the same history. No little observer has to sit behind the person doing the observing. Recognition of one’s habits can accumulate while the machinery and much of the material that does the recognizing change. The proposal concerns a capacity to guide action. What an experience feels like to its subject is a further question.[2]

Keeping an account has costs. A learner cannot inspect every movement while making it. Darwin’s device was economical because it identified a particular class of observations worth a second look. Reflection improves cognition when it changes which evidence is saved or which action is taken. More rounds of reconsideration on the same mistaken premises can preserve the error. At an industrial scale someone still pays for the extra computation, as the meter in the epilogue will show.

A child learning when to speak of a successful hunt encounters an especially severe test. It is possible to judge the animal correctly, to know that there will be plenty to eat, and still to misunderstand what everybody is saying about it. Richard Lee made that mistake with an ox he had bought himself.
