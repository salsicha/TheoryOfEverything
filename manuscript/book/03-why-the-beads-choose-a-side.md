# Chapter 3 — Why the Beads Choose a Side

*Part I · Physics*

Ko van der Weele and his colleagues stopped the shaker so they could count the beads. A few on the emptier side of the partition could still jump it. On the crowded side, the beads knocked one another about and settled. At some settings it looked as though every last one had gone to one side. The act of getting a still picture had helped put them there.[1]

The apparatus at the University of Twente contained six hundred glass beads and a wall twenty-three millimeters high. A motor shook both compartments. When it drove them hard enough, the counts stayed close to three hundred apiece. The team reduced the frequency while keeping the length of the stroke fixed. One compartment could then end up with most of the beads. Either compartment would do. Machinery that treated left and right alike had produced a state in which left and right had different futures.[1]

Where did the difference come from? Start with one extra bead in a compartment. At high drive it adds to the number available to jump out. Departures can cancel the surplus. At a lower drive the extra bead changes the experience of a bead landing there. It will collide with more neighbors before it reaches the partition. Each collision costs some of the motion it needs to clear the wall. Under those conditions the more crowded compartment can actually supply *fewer* escaping beads. A small excess can retain the next arrival. The same collision rule gives different results on opposite sides of a threshold.[1]

There is a way to call the result before either pile forms. At the chosen motor setting, measure departures per second from compartments with counts just above and below three hundred. Now start the two sides at 301 and 299. If the side with 301 sends beads out more slowly, the side with 299 supplies the difference and the imbalance initially grows. If the fuller side sends beads out faster, it loses its lead. Repeat with the extra bead on the other side and the prediction reverses with it. The rate curve predicts whether a fluctuation will grow and its initial average speed. In a balanced box it leaves the eventual winning side to chance.[1]

The motor matters. Equal numbers in a photograph might suggest equilibrium. But the table was delivering energy on every stroke, and the collisions were dissipating it. The uniform distribution was one way of carrying on under that drive. Its stability changed when the researchers turned a control. With the drive cut off for good, the apparatus could leave a pile on one side. It could never run a continuous bead elevator on the strength of that pile alone.

## What it takes to upset a balance

This is the difficulty in the proposition that equilibrium always leads to disequilibrium. At genuine thermal equilibrium a closed system has no steady gradient from which to draw useful work. Small fluctuations occur. A machine that could rectify each favorable fluctuation at no cost and reset itself for the next would give an inexhaustible source of work at one temperature. Even the pawl that is supposed to stop a ratchet going backward is subject to thermal motion.[2]

The motor can raise the same bead many times. Each collision consumes some of the motion it received. At one fixed drive, a left-hand pile and its right-hand mirror have equal claims to dissipate the most power. The apparatus gives neither side a permanent advantage. Beads per second crossing the wall tell us how an imbalance develops. To rank heat production we would also need the motor’s work and the temperatures at which it ended up. A striking pattern can leave that question entirely open.[1][2]

## Two currents in the same cylinder

Stephan Weiss and Guenter Ahlers had a way to see the difference. They heated water from below in a cylinder and cooled it at the top. Sometimes one large roll took warm water up one side and brought cooler water back down the other. Sometimes two rolls turned one above the other. The water switched between the patterns within a run. The experimenters could sort their readings by what the water was doing while the vessel, the fluid and the imposed driving conditions stayed the same.[2]

A single roll carries fluid the length of the cylinder. With two rolls, heat has another exchange to make near the middle. In one set of runs the single roll transported about 1.6 percent more heat than the double roll, and at a stronger drive its advantage was about 0.9 percent. Across fixed hot and cold temperatures, each watt carried from one to the other produces the same amount of entropy per second. Under that boundary condition the heat-transport ranking is also the entropy-production ranking. Of the two organized routes, the one spanning the vessel could sustain the higher rate.[2]

How often did the water actually take it? Near the upper end of the measured driving range the single roll occupied about four-fifths of the record. Even there, the water sometimes divided into two rolls or passed through less orderly states. Near the low end the double roll occupied more time than the single one. To predict the next afternoon at a given setting, one could use the morning’s record to estimate how often each roll forms, how long it survives and how much time the water spends between them. The heat-current readings alone could never tell the experimenter how to divide up the afternoon. A theory of maximum production has to account for those periods in the slower state too.[2]

Weiss and Ahlers changed one thing in a later experiment. They tilted the cylinder a few degrees. Buoyancy then helped drive warm water along the lower plate toward its high side and cold water the other way along the upper plate. Both motions favored one circulation around the whole vessel. At tilts of about three and a half degrees and above, the water spent nearly all its time in the single roll in their runs. Heat transport rose slightly. One can follow the cause from the angle of the plates, through the forces on the water, to the extra time spent carrying heat by the faster route.[2]

On December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler drew a box around a set of counts in his notebook. Chien-Shiung Wu had come to the National Bureau of Standards to test a different symmetry. A cold sample of cobalt with its nuclei oriented provided a way to count the directions in which electrons came out in beta decay. The excess on one side depended on the orientation. Warming the sample disturbed the alignment and provided a check. The team had found that the weak interaction could distinguish an arrangement from its mirror image.[3]

The cobalt and the beads answer different questions. In the bead apparatus the rule governing each collision treated the two compartments alike. In the cobalt experiment it was the assumption about the rule that failed. Still another question concerns the abundance of matter and antimatter. To get from a possible early balance to the observed excess of matter requires processes that change the balance and a history that preserves a surplus. Small differences measured in particular decays narrow the choices for a theory. A jar full of beads on the left gives no estimate of the amount of matter a universe should contain.[3]

The beads show one way an old arrangement can end. Once it loses stability, an ordinary fluctuation can start a change that grows. Our mine owners faced a different problem. A shallow working could keep paying for itself while the means of working a deeper seam lay behind the cost of the first pump. That arrangement could change when someone found a way to bear the cost. In the first case we can look for the motor setting at which a disturbance starts to grow. In the second we want to know how often anyone can attempt the expensive move and how often the attempt succeeds. Both questions make sense before we know which history occurred.[1]

In the language of dialectics, quantity has changed into quality. More collisions alter the fate of the next bead, while the impacts of individual beads make the crowd. That description suggests looking for a point at which the group changes what its members can do. The departure counts tell us where that point lies and which way the change will go. Later, in a harvest field short of hands, the corresponding quantities will be the days to bring in the crop and the wage that can bring another reaper to it.[1][4]

The stopped apparatus also offers a check on the way we read history. A still photograph can preserve the difference and lose the process that made it. In astronomy we often have little else. One image shows a black line across a bright cloud. There are good reasons to think a star is forming behind it. Most of the energy that explains the image comes from something that happened before the star could shine by its own fusion.
