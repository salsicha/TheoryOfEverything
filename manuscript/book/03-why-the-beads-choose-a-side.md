# Chapter 3 — Why the Beads Choose a Side

*Part I · Physics*

Ko van der Weele and his colleagues stopped the shaker so they could count the beads. A few on the emptier side of the partition could still jump it. On the crowded side, the beads knocked one another about and settled. At some settings it looked as though every last one had gone to one side. The act of getting a still picture had helped put them there.[1]

The apparatus at the University of Twente contained six hundred glass beads and a wall twenty-three millimeters high. A motor shook both compartments. When it drove them hard enough, the counts stayed close to three hundred apiece. The team reduced the frequency while keeping the length of the stroke fixed. One compartment could then end up with most of the beads. Either compartment would do. Machinery that treated left and right alike had produced a state in which left and right had different futures.[1]

Where did the difference come from? Start with one extra bead in a compartment. At high drive it adds to the number available to jump out. Departures can cancel the surplus. At a lower drive the extra bead changes the experience of a bead landing there. It will collide with more neighbors before it reaches the partition. Each collision costs some of the motion it needs to clear the wall. Under those conditions the more crowded compartment can actually supply *fewer* escaping beads. A small excess can retain the next arrival. The same collision rule gives different results on opposite sides of a threshold.[1]

The motor matters. Equal numbers in a photograph might suggest equilibrium. But the table was delivering energy on every stroke, and the collisions were dissipating it. The uniform distribution was one way of carrying on under that drive. Its stability changed when the researchers turned a control. With the drive cut off for good, the apparatus could leave a pile on one side. It could never run a continuous bead elevator on the strength of that pile alone.

## What it takes to upset a balance

This is the difficulty in the proposition that equilibrium always leads to disequilibrium. At genuine thermal equilibrium a closed system has no steady gradient from which to draw useful work. Small fluctuations occur. A machine that could rectify each favorable fluctuation at no cost and reset itself for the next would give an inexhaustible source of work at one temperature. Even the pawl that is supposed to stop a ratchet going backward is subject to thermal motion.[2]

Many balances we meet have a different history. Their surroundings are working to keep them where they are. Others can persist for a long time behind a barrier, then change when cooling, loading or a fresh connection opens a route. A pan of water at a uniform temperature lacks the steady vertical flow that appears when it is heated sufficiently from below and cooled from above. The hot surface, the cold surface and the supply of heat belong in the account. Leave them out and the circulation appears to have started in water that had no reason to move.[2]

How fast did the imbalance grow after the drive changed? Successive counts on each side could answer that question in beads per second. How fast did the apparatus dissipate energy? That takes a power measurement. Calculating the rate of entropy production also requires knowing how and at what temperatures that energy was dissipated. One pile of beads can be the outcome of several drives of different duration and power. Symmetry breaking has a rate of its own. A theory that connects it to entropy production has to account for both clocks.[1][2]

The conjecture is that the universe takes the fastest accessible course toward dissipating a gradient. The word *accessible* does real work. For heat crossing between two fixed temperatures, entropy production is the heat current times the difference of their reciprocal temperatures. A thick layer of insulation can sustain a steep drop while carrying little heat. Two conducting routes in parallel can both carry heat. Their conductances let us estimate the current in each before we put a thermometer at the outlet. A preference for the fastest feasible overall flow is a stronger claim than the familiar direction of heat transfer. The motor and wall let us investigate one instance. At the beginning of the universe there was no experimenter to switch on a shaker.[1][2]

On December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler drew a box around a set of counts in his notebook. Chien-Shiung Wu had come to the National Bureau of Standards to test a different symmetry. A cold sample of cobalt with its nuclei oriented provided a way to count the directions in which electrons came out in beta decay. The excess on one side depended on the orientation. Warming the sample disturbed the alignment and provided a check. The team had found that the weak interaction could distinguish an arrangement from its mirror image.[3]

The cobalt and the beads answer different questions. In the bead apparatus the rule governing each collision treated the two compartments alike. In the cobalt experiment it was the assumption about the rule that failed. Still another question concerns the abundance of matter and antimatter. To get from a possible early balance to the observed excess of matter requires processes that change the balance and a history that preserves a surplus. Small differences measured in particular decays narrow the choices for a theory. A jar full of beads on the left gives no estimate of the amount of matter a universe should contain.[3]

Why bring these cases together? Many accounts of creation start with a general permission for opposites to appear and proceed straight to the existence of a world. Between those things lies the business end of the apparatus. An extra bead had to reduce the rate of escape. The cobalt nuclei had to orient before the counters could reveal the preference. An initially small excess in the universe had to last through processes that might erase it. As soon as someone states the relevant step, another person can ask what would happen if its conditions changed.

There is a use for the word *dialectical* here. A little more of the same can alter what the whole can do. The next bead goes into a compartment much like its neighbor. Enough additional collisions can turn a restoring tendency into one that amplifies an imbalance. What happens at the scale of the group changes what happens to an individual, and individual impacts make the group. Later we will see the same two questions arise in a wage agreement. The resemblance tells us where to look. Nobody should expect a pay dispute to obey the equation for escaping beads.[1][4]

The stopped apparatus also offers a check on the way we read history. A still photograph can preserve the difference and lose the process that made it. In astronomy we often have little else. One image shows a black line across a bright cloud. There are good reasons to think a star is forming behind it. Most of the energy that explains the image comes from something that happened before the star could shine by its own fusion.
