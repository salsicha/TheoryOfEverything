# Chapter 9 — Feudalism and Capitalism

In 1335 Matthew Gilbert took possession of his share of four acres at Walsham le Willows in Suffolk. He had been eleven when his father died. Matthew and his three brothers inherited the house and land, but the lord of the manor kept Matthew’s share for four years. His father could leave him ground to grow food on. The manor court decided when he could have it.[1]

The family holding and the lord’s estate drew on the same working day. A tenant with customary labor dues might have two harvests to bring in, the lord’s and his own. The claim on his time came with the land his household needed. Out of what families like his grew came the food for people who governed, fought and prayed. Lords also owed service to other lords. When the bishop of Chartres explained those obligations to the duke of Aquitaine in 1020, he had an immediate reason to care whether the duke honored them. His city and church had burned, and he was counting on the duke’s help to rebuild. The bishop could appeal to an oath of mutual aid. The peasant whose work fed them both had to answer to the court that controlled his holding.[1][2]

Marx and Engels began their account of history with the work it takes to live. A family that sowed its holding and worked the lord’s fields fed itself and supplied the lord with the means to remain a lord. With each harvest people reproduced a social order along with their food. *Historical materialism* asks what becomes of that order when the conditions of the work change. In Matthew’s lifetime landlords who had plenty of land began to run short of people.[3]

Grain supplied free energy in a literal sense. A laborer could digest it and work another day. A lord could keep a household and retain people to administer his estate. The same harvest supported both activities, but who controlled the fields decided whose work the surplus could sustain. When people changed the terms on which they obtained food, they changed the conditions for reproducing the economy itself.[1][3]

## When a landlord needed hands

At its sitting on June 15, 1349, the court of Walsham manor recorded 103 deaths. Across England the plague had left fewer people to reap the grain. The king’s complaint in the Ordinance of Labourers that year was that servants knew how badly masters needed them and demanded higher pay. He ordered people under sixty who were fit to work and lacked land, a trade or other means to live on to accept suitable employment when offered. Employers had to pay the old rates. A reaper who left another man’s service without leave or good cause before his term was up risked prison. So did the employer who took him on.[1][4]

Why punish a man for hiring a reaper? A landlord who underpaid could lose his crew to a neighbor and his crop to the rain. A change in the number of reapers had become a change in who could set the terms of a harvest. Matthew lived through the plague, served as reeve, the local officer who helped run the manor, and took part in eighteen land transactions over his lifetime. When he died in 1370, he held two houses, twenty-four acres of land, four more of meadow and a patch of woodland. His daughter inherited. On taking possession she owed the lord a cow valued at twelve shillings.[1][4]

Two generations later the Walsham estate had a different problem. In the early 1430s it let its own farmland to tenants on short leases, some for just a year. A run of bad harvests and unsettled markets made rents hard to collect. The estate accounts had shown no arrears from 1428 to 1434. By the end of 1436 the unpaid sum exceeded half a year’s gross revenue. In 1437 the estate put most of the land under one local tenant. The arrears came under control. In October 1453 Alice Chaucer, lady of the manor, divided the 387 acres of arable land into twenty-two holdings. Each tenant could pass the holding to an heir and owed a fixed annual rent.[5]

Alice could collect an income without having to organize a single harvest on those acres. Her tenants had to find seed and labor and make the rent even in a poor year. In a good one they could keep what remained after costs and dues. A century earlier, the loss of so many lives had given workers a chance to demand better terms. Later, harvest failures and unpaid rents had given the estate reason to change its terms too. The lordship survived. On land the estate had once farmed for its own account, twenty-two tenants now decided how to bring in a crop.[3][5]

The right to use common ground could determine how much of a week’s labor a family had to sell. In 1549 Robert Kett and his fellow petitioners in Norfolk asked the king to stop further enclosure and reserve the use of the commons to tenants and freeholders. They wanted rivers open for fishing and passage. When a holding changed hands, they wanted the fee set as low as a capon, a fattened rooster. A household that could graze a cow on the common had milk between paydays. Losing the pasture meant finding the price of feed or going without the milk. A family in that position had less room to refuse the wages on offer.[6]

A tenant who grew enough to eat might still have to sell grain or hire out at harvest to pay a rent due in money. A tax due in money gave the taxpayer much the same problem. In 1690 Massachusetts Bay offered its wartime creditors paper bills. The treasury would accept those bills for taxes. That gave a supplier a way to spend one and a taxpayer a reason to take it.[7]

## The price of a day’s work

Gregory King estimated 364,000 families of laborers and out-servants in England and Wales in 1688, more than twice the number he classed as farmers. A century before the Leeds wool workers petitioned against machinery, employers could draw on a large population who already worked for wages. The growth of the cloth trade changed who could put those hands to work.[7][8][9]

In Leeds, clothiers used to carry their woollens to a market on the bridge. Lord Irwin supplied a site for the first White Cloth Hall, and merchants and tradesmen paid to put it up. It opened in 1711. When it grew too small, clothiers subscribed for a larger one. In 1775 merchants took the lead in opening a third hall. A rival market at Gomersal could save some weaving households seven or eight miles on the journey to sell their cloth. The Leeds merchants wanted those sellers coming to them. The cloth trade now put enough money in their hands to build a market where they wanted it.[8]

A clothier who owned wool and a loom could bring a length of cloth to market and take home the proceeds. A hired hand who prepared the wool had already sold his part in it. His wages bought food while the cloth went on to a buyer he might never meet. Marx singled out the use an owner could make of that difference. Someone with money to advance could buy wool, pay for the work, sell the cloth and use the proceeds to hire again. When a profit went into new equipment or a larger lot of wool, the next round of production started on a larger scale. For the people who lived on wages, it started when someone offered them work.[3][8][9]

In 1786 Joseph Hepworth, Thomas Lobley and other Leeds wool workers put their own figures on a new machine. Before spinning, someone had to tease the tangled wool apart. The workers reckoned that in twelve hours four people tending a scribbling machine could prepare as much wool as ten working by hand. Run it for two shifts and it employed eight people to do what had been twenty people’s work. They counted 170 machines in the district southwest of Leeds. Their petition had a question for anyone who advised the displaced workers to learn a trade. What were they to apprentice their children to when a machine might take over that trade too?[9]

Five years later the Leeds cloth merchants defended a further round of machinery, this time for finishing cloth. Much of their cloth went abroad. Overseas buyers could turn to manufacturers whose workers, the merchants said, earned half the English wage. The merchants credited the scribbling mill, the spinning frame and the flying shuttle with helping the trade grow. They also made a promise. If the new machinery left too little finishing work to go around, they would give preference to local workers who had offered no opposition to it.[9]

If cheaper cloth brought in more orders, owners had a reason to keep the machinery turning through the night. Each yard could cost fewer hours of labor while a growing trade used more wool and power in a year. In Leeds the merchants reckoned that growth would bring higher wages too. In February 1817 cloth dressers from the district sent Parliament their own account. In Yorkshire, they said, machine-driven shears had increased from at most a hundred pairs in 1806 to 1,462. On February 3 the dressers counted 1,170 of their trade with no work at all and 763 in full employment. The rest had part-time work. They asked for relief and for the freedom to seek work abroad. They told Parliament that the law barred skilled workers like them from going.[9]

Cheaper goods and a wage that will buy them give a household reason to welcome the growth of an industry. The two gains can come apart. What a buyer saw as a few pence off a yard of cloth could represent the loss of a family’s wages. Marx called it *commodity fetishism* when a relation among the people who made a good appeared to be a property of the thing itself. In Leeds even the promise of preference in hiring came on the merchants’ terms. Workers who wanted a guarantee they could offer one another had to find the money for it themselves.[3][9][10]

Robert Applegarth, a carpenters’ union secretary, described what he did around the sixth of each month. Reports arrived from the branches. He looked for a town where carpenters were out of work and another where employers needed them. Then he wrote to the secretary in the first town to say where the jobs were. The union paid the railway or steamboat fare. Parliament heard his account in 1871.[10]

Matthew’s daughter had a customary claim to the land that fed her. A carpenter who owned every tool he needed could still be a train fare away from earning his dinner. Over the intervening centuries more families had come to depend on selling their labor, and firms had grown by investing the proceeds of that labor in the next round of production. The dependence that let an employer set terms also gave workers a reason to organize. The bishop of Chartres had counted on help from his duke. The carpenters could draw on a fund they had paid for themselves.[1][2][3][10]
