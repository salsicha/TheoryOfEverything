# Chapter 11 — Morality and language

In April 1789 members of Parliament received a drawing of a ship with its deck cut away. Rows of people lay along the narrowing sides, their bodies turned to fit the hull. A section showed the clearance above their heads. Anyone looking for the ship’s name in the London printing had to find it partway through a paragraph. It was the *Brooks*.[1]

A merchant could find that same hull in a register, first under *Brook*, then *Brooks*. Lloyd’s Register had developed marks a man in one port could use to judge a ship in another. In the surviving 1764 register, vowels graded the hull and G, M and B marked its equipment as good, middling or bad. The marks changed over time. A few characters still spared the subscriber the cost of inspecting every ship himself.[1][2]

A merchant comparing a dozen entries could pick a sound hull. He would learn little about anyone shut below its deck.

## A ship drawn to scale

In late 1788 William Elford and his colleagues in Plymouth obtained Captain Parrey’s measurements of nine vessels in the slave trade. They drew a plan from the dimensions of the Liverpool ship. The London abolition committee added views, James Phillips printed the sheet, and the committee sent it to MPs and others. Copies circulated with the views rearranged and much of the text cut away.[1]

The breadth of the deck could tell a shipowner how many people he could carry. Put those bodies side by side on paper and a reader could use the same measurement to ask whether one person had room to turn over.[1]

No one in the drawing has a name. Its rows were a reconstruction from measurements. In his 1789 autobiography Olaudah Equiano described crossing on another vessel. He recalled the heat, the foul air and how hard it was to turn over once the ship filled. People sickened and died. Equiano could tell a reader what it was to remain in that space after she had put the sheet down.[3]

A reader could show the sheet to a neighbor and ask for a signature. The judgment that the trade ought to end could then travel in a petition beyond the people who had seen the drawing. Enough signatures reaching someone with power over a sailing could give that judgment force. Morality works as a communication technology when a judgment can make that passage.[1][4]

In 1792 the Commons resolved on a *gradual* end to the trade. The resolution barred no sailing. The *Brooks* carried enslaved people until 1804. Only in 1807 did Parliament prohibit the British trade. The prohibition could reach shipowners who had never seen the drawing.[1][4]

The register could still tell an investor that a hull was sound. What it could earn now depended on the cargoes the law allowed, the cost of refitting and whether buyers could be found. A wider physical account would have to include the work extracted from enslaved people, the ships used elsewhere and the fuel burned enforcing the ban. Those flows bear on a claim about entropy production. People had grounds to end the trade whatever the energy accounts might show.[1][3][4]

## What a second opinion is worth

Bahador Bahrami and his colleagues asked two people to watch a screen. On each trial one of two displays contained a patch of higher contrast. Each observer picked the interval in which it appeared. When their answers differed, they had to reach a joint decision. The value of speaking depended on what one observer could convey about the reliability of a choice.[5]

Partners of similar visual sensitivity could outperform either observer alone when they could communicate. With a large disparity in sensitivity, the pair could do worse than its better observer. A person who speaks with equal assurance after a glimpse and after a good look gives a partner little means of telling which was which.[5]

## What a listener can pass on

Simon Kirby, Hannah Cornish and Kenny Smith showed people pictures of colored shapes with arrows indicating how each shape moved. There were twenty-seven combinations. A learner saw names for only some of them, then had to name the whole set. The next learner saw a sample of the names the previous one had supplied.[6]

By the eighth generation in one chain, *tuge* covered everything moving horizontally and *poi* everything spiraling. A learner could remember the whole rule and still have no word to distinguish a horizontal triangle from a horizontal circle. The researchers changed what passed to the next person. When the same name served for several objects, at most one of those pairings could appear among the examples. Learners began passing on pieces of words that indicated color, shape or movement. Someone who had never seen a particular combination could put a name together for it.[6]

What had to survive in a petition was a demand that another person could put into words and Parliament could vote on. A reader could forget the name *Brooks* and still ask that the trade should end. In 1792 the Commons agreed that the trade should end. The word *gradual* left the shipowner free to fit out the next voyage.[1][4]
