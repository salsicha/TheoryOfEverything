# Chapter 1 — From Zero Energy to Asymmetry

*Part I · Physics*

Olaf Nairz and his colleagues heated a powder in an oven in Vienna until molecules of seventy carbon atoms began flying out. Two slits let a narrow beam of them through. When the team closed the second slit from 1.4 micrometers to about a twentieth of that width, the molecules that got through spread *farther* across the detector. The researchers knew more precisely where each molecule had passed. They had less certainty about where to find it downstream.[1]

The oven ran at about 900 kelvin. Heat can set a molecule vibrating. Yet Nairz could change the spread at the detector by adjusting the slit while leaving the oven alone. What he varied was how closely the slit confined the molecule’s sideways position. Quantum mechanics sets a lower bound on the product of the spreads in position and momentum. The narrower opening gave the emerging beam a wider range of sideways momenta. What would remain of such a spread if the heat could be taken away?[1]

At Caltech, Amir Safavi-Naeini and his colleagues used light to cool one vibration of a tiny silicon beam. This mode vibrated nearly four billion times a second. They sent in a second beam of light to measure it. A photon could leave with a little more energy by taking one unit of vibration from the silicon. It could also leave with a little less energy by adding a unit. That gave the group two signals to follow as the silicon cooled. The nearer the mode came to its lowest energy, the fewer units there were for a photon to take. A photon could still put one in. As cooling continued, the photons that added a unit increasingly outnumbered those that took one away. From that ratio the team could measure the fluctuations that remained.[2]

Absolute zero matters here because it sets a limit on what cooling can remove. At that temperature an oscillator in equilibrium occupies its lowest energy state. For the beam to sit at one exact position with exactly zero momentum would violate the same uncertainty relation the Vienna team had tested. Confining it closer to the center costs energy in the spread of momentum. A wider spread of positions stores more elastic energy in the silicon. Even the state of least energy retains some spread in both. Physicists call the energy of that remaining vibration *zero-point energy*. A mode in its ground state has no more energy to give up.[2]

Cooling answers one question. If thermal motion accounted for the whole spread, taking away the last thermal excitation would remove it. The silicon shows why this expectation fails. The ground state still has a spread in position and momentum. We can call its energy zero on a scale and the spread will still be there. To make an engine run, on the other hand, we must supply an accessible difference in energy.[1][2]

Do things have definite properties before anything interacts with them? In Delft, experimenters measured two electron spins far enough apart that news of one measurement could not reach the other in time. Their correlations exceeded a bound that applies if the answers were fixed locally in advance under the assumptions of the test. One way to read that result is to take the indeterminacy as a property of reality itself. This is the idea of *ontological uncertainty*. Several interpretations of quantum mechanics agree on the observations. An uncertain outcome still leaves a question about physical history. How can a difference, once it arises, last?[3]

## A difference that grows

In Berkeley, Lorraine Sadler and her colleagues lowered a magnetic field around a cloud of cold rubidium atoms. The change took five milliseconds. Before it, the cloud had no detectable magnetization. After a short wait, patches appeared in which the atoms acquired a common magnetic orientation. One patch could point a different way from the next. The researchers had set the strength and axis of the field. They had left equivalent directions in the plane across that axis. Their images showed the gas choosing among those directions.[4]

Here a change in the conditions made a nearly uniform arrangement unstable. It was possible to predict the growth of magnetic regions without predicting the direction of each one. The field change and the interactions among the atoms supplied a history for the pattern. The images could not identify the fluctuation that seeded each patch. For a case in which the fluctuations can be counted one by one, the apparatus needs only a wall and some beads.[4]

Ko van der Weele and his colleagues at the University of Twente put six hundred glass beads in a tube divided by a low wall. A motor shook the tube. At a brisk setting about three hundred beads stayed on either side. At a slower setting most could end up on the left. In a fresh run they could end up on the right. The motor supplied the energy in both cases. Why did an extra bead that used to hop back over the wall begin to keep others on its side?[5]

Where did the difference come from? Start with one extra bead in a compartment. At high drive it adds to the number available to jump out. Departures can cancel the surplus. At a lower drive the extra bead changes the experience of a bead landing there. It will collide with more neighbors before it reaches the partition. Each collision costs some of the motion it needs to clear the wall. Under those conditions the more crowded compartment can actually supply *fewer* escaping beads. A small excess can retain the next arrival. The same collision rule gives different results on opposite sides of a threshold.[5]

Van der Weele’s team then put the same number of glass beads in a tube with three connected compartments. Vigorous shaking left about two hundred in each. As the team reduced the drive, an even distribution gave way to one crowded compartment. They plotted the final size of the pile against the drive. In two compartments the imbalance grew continuously from zero. In three the plot had a jump. Returning the shaker to the setting where the beads had first clustered left the cluster in place. It took stronger shaking to disperse it.[5]

Forty-two seconds after Devaraj van der Meer and his colleagues started shaking a row of five boxes, their photograph still showed a cluster of steel beads in the middle box. In the picture taken at forty-three seconds the cluster was gone. The motor had kept the same setting. For most of the run the crowded box had leaked beads to its neighbors. As the pile thinned, fewer collisions robbed the remaining beads of the speed they needed to get out. Each loss made further losses easier. After the collapse the beads went on spreading through the boxes.[5]

Here is a physical instance of what dialectics calls the passage from quantity into quality. The number of beads crossing the partition can change by one at a time. Beyond a threshold the same crossing starts to amplify an imbalance the earlier arrangement would have erased. The input that keeps the beads in motion and the collisions that dissipate it can be identified separately. In thermal equilibrium, fluctuations alone provide no source of continuous work. One has to find the drive in each new case.[5]

## Which difference carries more heat?

Stephan Weiss and Guenter Ahlers heated water from below in a cylinder and cooled it at the top. Sometimes one large roll took warm water up one side and brought cooler water back down the other. Sometimes two rolls turned one above the other. The water switched between the patterns within a run. The experimenters could sort their readings by what the water was doing while the vessel, the fluid and the imposed driving conditions stayed the same.[6]

A single roll carries fluid the length of the cylinder. With two rolls, heat has another exchange to make near the middle. In one set of runs the single roll transported about 1.6 percent more heat than the double roll, and at a stronger drive its advantage was about 0.9 percent. Across fixed hot and cold temperatures, each watt carried from one to the other produces the same amount of entropy per second. Under that boundary condition the heat-transport ranking is also the entropy-production ranking. Of the two organized routes, the one spanning the vessel could sustain the higher rate.[6]

How often did the water actually take it? Near the upper end of the measured driving range the single roll occupied about four-fifths of the record. Even there, the water sometimes divided into two rolls or passed through less orderly states. Near the low end the double roll occupied more time than the single one. To predict the next afternoon at a given setting, one could use the morning’s record to estimate how often each roll forms, how long it survives and how much time the water spends between them. The heat-current readings alone could never tell the experimenter how to divide up the afternoon. A theory of maximum production has to account for those periods in the slower state too.[6]

Weiss and Ahlers changed one thing in a later experiment. They tilted the cylinder a few degrees. Buoyancy then helped drive warm water along the lower plate toward its high side and cold water the other way along the upper plate. Both motions favored one circulation around the whole vessel. At tilts of about three and a half degrees and above, the water spent nearly all its time in the single roll in their runs. Heat transport rose slightly. One can follow the cause from the angle of the plates, through the forces on the water, to the extra time spent carrying heat by the faster route.[6]

On December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler drew a box around a set of counts in his notebook. Chien-Shiung Wu and her colleagues had oriented cobalt nuclei at low temperature and counted the electrons emitted in beta decay. More electrons emerged in one direction relative to the nuclei’s orientation than in the other. Warming the sample disturbed the alignment. In the tube of beads, either side could win under the same symmetric rule. In Wu’s experiment the weak interaction itself distinguished left from right.[7]

That distinction matters to the history of the universe. Any account of why matter outlasted antimatter must identify a process that could produce the excess and conditions that could preserve it. The shaken beads supply a working example of amplification. They give no value for the cosmic excess. And neither the beads nor the cobalt tell us where the rules governing them came from. We will return to that question when we ask whether time and physical laws could themselves have been assembled. For now we can follow a piece of the matter that survived. What had to happen before an electron passing a proton could stay there?[7]
