# Chapter 17 — Why Did Sam Eyde Want to Replace His Own Furnace?

In June 1910 the builders of the fertilizer works at Rjukan had to choose a furnace. Sam Eyde had helped develop one of the two designs. The German company Badische had backed the other. The latest comparison gave nearly equal results. The builders installed eight rows of the German furnaces and two rows of the kind that bore Eyde’s name.[1]

Ten rows of furnaces shared one source of power. Was the industry assembled around Eyde’s particular invention, or could another machine take its place by doing more with the same falling water? Within two decades Eyde was urging Norsk Hydro, the company he had helped found, to replace the arc process used by both designs.[1][2]

## What the waterfall could do

The trial works had opened in 1905 at Notodden. Tinfos, a local company, had expanded its small power station enough to supply the trial. Water ran from the intake to the river below through turbines. The drop in elevation and the amount of water passing each second set a ceiling on the power the station could deliver. A generator and a line made some of that power available in a building beside Lake Heddalsvatnet. The nitrogen the farmers wanted was in the air passing through the building.[3]

Kristian Birkeland, Eyde’s partner, had found a way to spread an electric arc into a broad disc with a magnet. Air passed through the disc. The heat let some of its nitrogen combine with oxygen. The works recovered the product and sold the nitrogen in the form of calcium nitrate. Only a small fraction of the air yielded fixed nitrogen on each pass. A reliable supply of cheap electricity made a furnace with that appetite worth trying.[3][4]

On July 20, 1905, the Norwegian paper *Aftenposten* reported favorable results on farms that had tried the product. Its hopes for agriculture depended on whether the fertilizer could be “produced cheaply enough.” Growers could already buy nitrate from Chile. The company had to cover the cost of running its furnace and delivering a sack for a price a farmer would pay.[4][5]

By the autumn of 1907, a new power station at Svelgfoss could supply the works with 30,000 horsepower over a line four and a half kilometers long. More power was one way to make more fertilizer. There was another. The kilowatt-hours available in an hour, divided by the kilowatt-hours a process took per tonne of nitrogen, set a ceiling on hourly output. Once the station reached full load, another row of the same furnaces would only divide the supply. A furnace that used fewer kilowatt-hours per tonne could increase output on the same wires.[3][4]

That ceiling is a constraint in *Platonic space*. It holds for a German furnace just as it does for a Norwegian one. A design that needed more power than the station could deliver for a promised output could be struck from a drawing before anyone built it. The two designs compared for Rjukan both remained in contention. The near tie left the partners room to negotiate how many rows of each to install.[1][3][4]

## Why Eyde changed sides

The rival Eyde came to favor used a different reaction. In Germany, Fritz Haber had combined nitrogen with hydrogen to make ammonia. Carl Bosch and his colleagues built equipment that could sustain the reaction under pressure in a working plant. At a Norwegian waterfall, Hydro could use electricity to split water and supply the hydrogen. Once engineers could keep the high-pressure plant running, Hydro could buy a way to fix more nitrogen on its existing power supply.[2][3][6]

The old furnaces could keep making nitrate, and the company had trained the people who ran them. Harald Bjerke, who succeeded Eyde as managing director, resisted the change. The argument for conversion was strongest wherever Hydro could keep the expensive parts that served either method. Water would still drive the turbines. Workers and trains still had access to the valley. What Hydro had to add was a hydrogen supply, suitable electrical equipment and a synthesis plant. What it could leave in place lowered the cost of trying the new route.[2][3][7]

The decisions at two works show how far that reasoning reached. In 1926 the board approved a plant at Notodden using technology from an American engineering company. Hydro’s engineers improved it. At Rjukan, Eyde supported an agreement with IG Farben. Bjerke resigned. His successor, Axel Aubert, made the agreement in 1927. The German group took a quarter of Hydro’s shares and responsibility for marketing its products outside Norway. The Notodden plant started on February 9, 1928, with its chiefly American design. Rjukan used the German process. Both gave Hydro a route through ammonia. The ability to sell fertilizer abroad gave it a separate reason to sign with the Germans.[2][7]

## What reached the coast

In 1929 a load of liquid ammonia leaving Rjukan for the new coastal works at Herøya went by rail to Lake Tinn. A railway ferry carried the wagons across. For the last stretch the cargo went by barge. The railway and the ferry had served the earlier nitrate industry. Now Hydro could put the final stage of production near its customers and supply it from the inland plants. A missing crossing would have broken that route however well the reaction worked.[8]

The ammonia wagons crossed the lake on a ferry line that the arc industry had already paid to put into service. That crossing helps explain Eyde’s choice. The cost of replacing his furnaces was a different proposition from the cost of building an ammonia industry in a valley without turbines, rail or a route to the coast. The first assembly brought its successor within reach.[1][2][3][8]

Hydro credits the conversion with quadrupling capacity. On October 19, 1929, it served beer and sausages to the workforce in a disused factory at Rjukan. Within days the New York stock market crashed. As the ammonia market weakened, the company negotiated a guarantee from IG Farben for sales of at least 83,000 tonnes of nitrogen, close to its capacity at the time. Even with the agreement, Hydro laid off workers and posted a loss in 1933–34.[2][7]

The new works required less electricity per tonne. They still needed enough orders to keep the plants running. The sales guarantee recorded a promise to buy. To learn whether the industry drew more power from the falls after the conversion, we would need its operating hours and electricity records. At the data center in the Finnish town of Mäntsälä, we have the readings from both the building that draws the electricity and the system that sells its heat.[2][7]
