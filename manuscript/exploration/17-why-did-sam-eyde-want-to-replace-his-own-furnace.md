# Chapter 17 — Why Did Sam Eyde Want to Replace His Own Furnace?

In 1920 the people of Rjukan celebrated for two days when a statue of Sam Eyde was unveiled in their town. The furnaces that bore his name used electricity from Norwegian waterfalls to make fertilizer from the air. Within a few years Eyde was urging Norsk Hydro, the company he had helped found, to replace them. Harald Bjerke, the man who had succeeded him as managing director, resisted. The old works could still make a product farmers wanted. That was precisely what made the decision difficult.[1]

Eyde’s change of sides is worth following because it puts the book’s argument to a harder test than the original invention. Cheap electricity explains why a power-hungry method could get a start in Norway. The waterfalls were still there when Hydro chose a method that required less electricity to fix a tonne of nitrogen. What advantage had changed hands?[1][2]

## What the farmers were buying

At Notodden in 1905, the physicist Kristian Birkeland and Eyde’s industrial collaborators put air through an electric arc. A magnetic field spread the arc into a broad, thin region through which the air could pass. At its high temperature, some of the nitrogen and oxygen combined. The works recovered the combined nitrogen in a form they could sell as calcium nitrate. Most of the air passed through without yielding the desired product. The machinery could do the job because it had a large supply of electric power at its disposal.[2][3]

Downstream from the turbines the water had lost elevation. In a farmer’s field, some of the nitrogen that had passed through the furnace could become part of a crop. Between the two stood a power station, an agreement to use its output, and a chemical works capable of turning enough of the incoming air into saleable goods. The farmer was buying the result of a waterfall doing work in a place where no crop was growing.[2][3]

In July 1905 a Norwegian newspaper reported that farmers had tried the product on fields and meadows. The report tied its favorable verdict to a condition that mattered greatly to Eyde. Could the fertilizer be produced cheaply enough? The following year the company took its product to an agricultural exhibition in Odense, Denmark. The growers there already knew that nitrogen-bearing fertilizers were useful. Chilean nitrate was an established competitor. A Norwegian works had to deliver a form of nitrogen the growers could afford to put on their fields, after paying to get it there.[3][4]

The Japanese farmer who had sent vegetables into Osaka could judge manure in the same terms. A sample that helped a crop grow was worth little if the cost of hauling it used up the gain. For the Norwegian company, industrial fixation opened a source that did not depend on collecting another city’s wastes or mining an overseas deposit. It added the expense of running a furnace and getting its output from a Norwegian valley to a customer. The first sale established that this whole journey could pay at least sometimes. Every proposed enlargement of the works put the question back to the buyers.[2][4]

## What was worth taking out

Hydro’s first response to competition was to improve its arcs. It had trained people, working plants and debts it had managed to pay down. In Germany, Fritz Haber had shown how nitrogen and hydrogen could be combined into ammonia. Carl Bosch and his colleagues had worked out how to sustain that reaction on an industrial scale. The useful difference for Hydro was that ammonia could be the starting material for nitrogen fertilizer by a route that consumed less energy per unit of fixed nitrogen. A company that already had hydroelectricity could use it to make the hydrogen from water.[1][2][5]

The arc works and the ammonia works could deliver nitrogen to the same market, but the old furnaces could not perform the new reaction. The company had to obtain equipment for producing hydrogen and plants capable of sustained ammonia synthesis. Parts of the power supply had to be adapted to deliver direct current. On the other hand, the company had already developed the falls, brought in skilled workers and built a transport route to the coast. A rival could compete with Eyde’s method while giving the installations around it a new lease of life.[1][6][7]

There was more than one way to change course. In 1926 Hydro’s board authorized an ammonia plant at Notodden based on an agreement with an American engineering company. Engineers at Hydro improved the design. At the same time the company was negotiating with the German group IG Farben. The Germans could supply technology and sell fertilizer abroad. With producers forming cartels, the agreement that opened a foreign market could be as valuable as the license to build a new plant.[1][6]

Eyde pressed the case for adopting the ammonia method and for an agreement with the Germans. Bjerke left, and in 1927 the new managing director, Axel Aubert, committed Hydro to the collaboration. IG Farben took a quarter of the company’s shares and responsibility for marketing its products outside Norway. On February 9, 1928, the new plant at Notodden started work using chiefly American technology with Hydro’s improvements. At Rjukan the company installed the German process. The two sites are a useful check on any account of an invention sweeping the field. Hydro had real choices even after the case for making ammonia had prevailed.[1][6]

Eyde had relinquished the managing directorship a decade earlier. He could advocate a change. Aubert and the board could commit the company. The German partner could provide a sales organization. The lower cost of fixing nitrogen gave all three a reason to act. How much more nitrogen they could put within reach of farmers depended on the agreement they made and on the equipment already in the valley.[1][6]

## A train that had to take a ferry

The changes divided the work among several sites. When the fertilizer works at Herøya on the coast were ready in 1929, they depended on liquid ammonia from the inland synthesis plants. A shipment from Rjukan went by rail, crossed Lake Tinn on a railway ferry and finished the journey to Herøya by barge. A fertilizer plant could now stand near the sea while drawing on nitrogen fixed inland. It depended on a boat capable of carrying a train.[7]

This defines what the celebrated efficiency gain actually bought. Cheaper fixation made it worth concentrating more work at the synthesis plant. That created a reason to have tank wagons, a ferry schedule, and coastal works equipped to receive their loads. Much of the transport route was already in service before the conversion. It had carried materials and goods for the older industry. Its existence changed what a tonne of ammonia made inland was worth, just as an agreement to take a farmer’s boat at an Osaka wharf changed what a load of manure was worth.[6][7]

Hydro credits the conversion with quadrupling capacity. On October 19, 1929, it served sausages and beer to its workers in a disused factory at Rjukan to celebrate the completion of the projects. Within days the New York stock market crashed. As the market for ammonia deteriorated, the company needed orders that would keep the new plants busy enough to pay for their construction. It later secured an agreement with IG Farben that guaranteed sales of at least 83,000 tonnes of nitrogen, close to capacity at the time according to Hydro’s account.[1][6]

The guarantee put a number on how much fertilizer Hydro could count on selling. It still had to lay off workers and reported a loss for 1933–34. The sales agreement and the engineering estimate recorded what a partner promised to buy and what the plants could make. To learn how much of the new capacity Hydro actually used, we would need production records. To learn what that cost the river, we would need to know how much water went through the turbines.[6]

The book proposes that arrangements which can put more available energy to work gain an advantage when part of the return pays for building more of them. The falls helped the electric arc overcome the initial cost of fixing nitrogen. The more economical route then made a greater quantity of useful product possible from that flow. Whether this brought about a greater *total rate* of energy use depends on what farmers bought, how much electricity Hydro subsequently drew, and what other users of that electricity would otherwise have done. The farmer’s gain from the last tonne and the power company’s gain from the last kilowatt-hour need their own accounts.[1][2][6]

Eyde’s proposal kept the waterfalls at work by retiring the furnaces that had first made them valuable to a fertilizer company. It left one question open. How much does the power drawn by an industry change when the same work can be done for less? At a data center in Finland there are meters on both sides of such a transaction. One records electricity used at the site. Another records heat delivered to the town.
