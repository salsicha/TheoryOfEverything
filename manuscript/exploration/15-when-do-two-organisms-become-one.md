# Chapter 15 — When Do Two Organisms Become One?

Kyoko Hagino’s team marked the round body in their photograph with an S. They had collected the alga that held it in a bucket of seawater at Tomari Port in western Japan on June 18, 2011. A fine net over a kitchen sieve caught the plankton. A section through the alga showed its nucleus, its chloroplasts and the small body beside them. Under an electron microscope the team could make out layers inside it. Across the three cells they sectioned, they found either one such body or two.[1]

Was this cell assembled from two organisms that once lived apart? The small body resembled a bacterium, but a photograph could establish only that the two shared a space. An alga can swallow a bacterium. What mattered was what these two could do together and what passed to their offspring.

In 1998 Jonathan Zehr’s group had recovered a fragment of a gene for fixing nitrogen from a Pacific seawater sample. It pointed to an unfamiliar cyanobacterium the researchers called UCYN-A. Hagino’s group found a close relative in their algae. They now had a reason to look at the gas dissolved in the water around each cell. The bacterium might be making a nutrient for its host out of nitrogen gas.[1][2]

## What the light could pay for

Sunlight striking a chloroplast sets electrons moving and helps concentrate hydrogen ions on one side of a membrane. As the ions flow back through an enzyme, the cell makes ATP. That gradient supplies some of the energy the alga uses to build carbon compounds from carbon dioxide. It can have light all day and still run short of an ingredient it needs to grow. It takes nitrogen to make proteins. Seawater contains dissolved nitrogen gas, but the alga lacks the enzyme that can make use of it.[3][4]

The body inside the alga has that enzyme, nitrogenase. It spends energy to convert the gas into ammonia. In 2012 Anne Thompson and her colleagues traced the exchange in related partners collected in the open ocean. Nitrogen fixed by the bacterium appeared in the alga. Carbon the alga had fixed appeared in the bacterium. In waters short of usable nitrogen, this trade could let the pair keep turning sunlight into new cells.[4]

The exchange puts a limit on the proportions such a partnership can take. A small nitrogen fixer paired with a much larger alga supplies too little nitrogen for them to grow fast together. An alga carrying too large a partner runs up against the opposite limit. It cannot fix carbon fast enough to feed both. Many such pairs could fit in a drop of water. Far fewer could double their mass together at a competitive rate.[5]

Francisco Cornejo-Castillo and his colleagues measured 167 pairs from three lineages. They also calculated how fast different pairs could grow if the host supplied the fixed carbon, its partner supplied the fixed nitrogen, and both divided at the same rate. They described each cell by the radius of a sphere of the same volume. In the model, growth peaked when the radius of the whole algal cell was 2.47 times that of its partner. In the water samples, the average ratio was 2.33.[5]

The ratio gives *Platonic space* a meaning one can check with a microscope. The two rates of supply pick out a narrow range of proportions for fast growth. Different lineages fell near it. An association could benefit both partners for as long as they stayed together. A daughter that inherited the fixer could begin obtaining nitrogen without another encounter in the open sea. The calculation assumes the two divide together. Each of Hagino’s photographs had caught a single moment.[4][5]

## What went to the daughters

Hagino made more than three hundred collecting trips over more than a decade before she could grow the algae in culture. At Berkeley, Valentina Loconte and her colleagues reconstructed the insides of cultured cells from X-rays taken at many angles. They examined cells at successive stages of division. The counts of one and two in Hagino’s sections now made sense. As the host prepared to divide, the nitrogen fixer divided too. Each daughter received one. A single undivided partner could have gone to at most one daughter.[2][6]

Tyler Coale then compared proteins in the isolated body with those in the whole alga. Some of the proteins inside it came from instructions in the alga’s nucleus. The smaller body lacked genes for some of the work that these proteins could do. It still had a genome of its own, but to account for the machinery inside it Coale had to read two genomes. The team called it a *nitroplast*, a nitrogen-fixing organelle.[6]

Two lineages had contributed the parts. Their descendants now build the nitrogen-fixing machinery between them and pass it to the next cell. That is the sense in which the cell was assembled. Which came first, reliable inheritance or the supply of proteins from the host? Both capacities are present in the living cells. Their order remains open.[5][6]

Shigekatsu Suzuki and his colleagues followed one of Hagino’s strains, collected in 2015. At first they could see the body inside it. Later it disappeared, and the algae kept growing in the laboratory. A cell can lose its means of fixing nitrogen and still grow if it can obtain usable nitrogen from the water. The proposed advantage of inheriting a nitroplast should be greatest where light is ample and usable nitrogen is scarce. Counts of the offspring left by algae with and without it under those conditions would test that claim.[7]

Back in Hagino’s photograph, the chloroplast sits beside the nitroplast. One releases oxygen in daylight. The other fixes nitrogen in daylight with an enzyme that oxygen can disable. In a 2026 preprint, Ethan Li and colleagues reported that layers of membrane and small structures at the nitroplast boundary change between day and night. How the cell keeps those neighbors working side by side remains an open question about the few micrometers around that S.[4][8]

Nitrogen can limit what a farmer gets from sunlight too. When farmers near Osaka sold a crop in town, some of the nitrogen in their soil left in the boat. Whether they could get it back depended on who had the right to load the boat for the return journey.

The application to Platonic constraints is the book’s interpretation. [Coale and colleagues, *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adk1075) · [Berkeley imaging account](https://als.lbl.gov/symbiotic-nitrogen-fixing-microbe-evolves-into-organelle/)

7. Suzuki and colleagues observed strain KC1-P2 lose its partner in culture. The reproductive comparison in nitrogen-poor water is a prediction of the book. [Suzuki and colleagues, *Frontiers in Plant Science*](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2021.749895)

8. Li and colleagues’ April 2026 preprint describes differences at the nitroplast boundary during the day and night. The authors discuss possible roles for the membranes and vesicles. [Li and colleagues, preprint and figures](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.04.11.717942)
