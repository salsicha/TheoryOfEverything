# Chapter 16 — Who Owned the Contents of Osaka’s Privies?

In the summer of 1724, two groups of farming villages disputed the right to empty privies in Osaka. The farmers from the Yamazaki and Takatsuki areas wanted the contents badly enough to fight over who could collect from which parts of the city. People in Osaka had occasion to complain about the trade. Manure boats used the same wharves as ships carrying tea, and the smell was considerable. The magistrates heard the complaints and let the boats stay.[1]

Farmers had been sending produce into Osaka by water and taking human excrement, or night soil, out to the fields. At first vegetables and other produce could buy the return cargo. As the price of fish fertilizer and other dressings rose, and as farmers opened more paddies, what came out of the city became more valuable. By the early eighteenth century a boatload of vegetables no longer settled the matter. Growers had to pay silver.[1]

It is easy enough to explain why a farmer valued the cargo. It is harder to explain why the right to collect it belonged to a particular village, why a landlord had a claim on what a tenant produced in the privy, and why, two centuries later, Osaka began charging for a service that farmers had once paid to perform. None of those changes required a change in the chemical composition of manure.

## What went back in the boat

A market gardener who sells a crop sends some of the soil’s nutrients away with it. In Osaka, many people ate their meals a boat ride from the fields where the food had grown. They used the energy in the food. Much of the nitrogen, phosphorus and other material they took in eventually passed out of their bodies. Collectors could return a share to cultivated ground. Next season’s crop would get its energy from another season of sunlight. The cargo could help the crop put more of that sunlight into growing tissue.[1][2]

Growers had alternatives. Miyazaki Yasusada’s agricultural manual, published late in the seventeenth century, discussed how to store farm and household wastes. Among the materials he considered were oilcake and dried sardines. A farmer making up a dressing had to reckon what each ingredient cost and how much trouble it took to bring it home. The price of fish therefore mattered at a city privy. When a competing fertilizer became expensive, farmers could afford to bid more for the contents waiting at the wharf. A crop could gain just as much from a given load as it did a year earlier while the person selling the load received more for it.[1][2]

One feature of the trade makes the difference between chemistry and price especially clear. In Osaka a building owner held the right to the occupants’ feces while the tenants had the right to their urine. The solid material commanded the higher price. Urine contains valuable nutrients, but much of a load is water. Historian Susan Hanley suggested that the difficulty of carrying and storing it helped account for the lower price. The terms of a tenancy could even reflect the expected income from the privy. Hanley found that rent could go up when the number of occupants fell. Fewer people meant less for the landlord to sell.[1]

That last detail lets us see a property right at work. With one fewer tenant, a household might appear to make a smaller claim on a building. On the landlord’s accounts, the household also supplied less. The reason both things were true was a rule about what the landlord owned. This is what the book means by a system being jointly material and Platonic. Food and bodies supplied the material. A distinction between two claims on the contents of a privy could be applied to house after house. It changed who got paid, and even what an occupant owed for a room. The distinction had its force because collectors, householders and civic authorities acted on it.[1]

## The people entitled to collect

A farmer had reason to want the arrangement to last beyond one return trip. Buying the right to collect in a quarter of the city secured a future supply. Other farmers had the same reason to want the same right. As Osaka grew, villages organized to obtain exclusive buying rights. There were associations on the city side too. That helps explain why the contestants in 1724 were groups of villages. Each faced another group capable of taking an entire supply away from it. Knowing how well a field would respond to fertilizer gives us a reason for the contest and very little basis for picking its winner.[1]

Some urine went uncollected by the holders of the household rights. Dealers made a business of taking it away. In 1772 they paid the city for recognition of a guild with authority to regulate their trade and set prices. Even that grant had a boundary. The village of Watanabe held the rights to urine from the containers set out on Osaka street corners for passersby. Watanabe was a community of low status in the order of the time. Others challenged the grant, offered to buy it, and interfered with the containers. The village kept its rights through the Tokugawa period.[1]

The offers to buy Watanabe’s rights tell us something the fertilizer price alone cannot. Other collectors believed they could earn enough from the same containers to pay the village for access. What they lacked was permission. The damage to the containers threatened Watanabe’s means of collection. The bids threatened its claim to the income even if collection went on as before. By defending both the vessels and its grant, the village kept the proceeds in the hands of its own people.[1]

The same success had costs for people outside it. Hanley found that poorer farmers had trouble securing sufficient fertilizer as prices rose. A higher price could keep a boat in service and put its cargo beyond the reach of a grower who needed it. More food for a town, more revenue for the collectors and more nitrogen for a particular field are three possible consequences. They need not accrue to the same people. It matters which return pays for the next trip.[1]

## When the city had to pay attention

In its 1930 account of public health, Osaka recorded when it had entered the collection business. In 1921, to speed removal, the municipality had begun collecting night soil for a charge alongside the private collectors and farmers. For the households that used it, the direction of payment had reversed. A resident who once supplied a valuable commodity could now be a customer buying its removal.[3]

Takanori Hoshino reconstructed the difficulties that preceded that change. After 1910 urban growth and competing fertilizers eroded the value of night soil to nearby growers. Waste accumulated in the city center. In 1930 a revision of the national cleaning law made disposal a municipal obligation. Osaka considered a sewerage scheme, but residents were reluctant to pay for the installation of flush toilets. The city eventually worked with the prefecture and agricultural associations to build storage tanks and supply farming villages. That decision preserved work for existing dealers and limited the city’s disposal costs.[4]

A storage tank is an answer to a problem of timing. City households need waste taken away at intervals determined by the people using the privies. Fields need fertilizer when a farmer can store or use it. A tank lets collection go ahead before a field is ready for the load. Earlier, the price a grower could offer had given households and dealers terms on which to bridge that interval. By taking on storage and disposal, the city acquired a way to bridge it out of a different budget. The route from city to field could outlive the market that had first made the route worth working.[2][3][4]

There is a historical prediction here. When competing fertilizer gets dearer or more nearby acres come under cultivation, collection rights should become more valuable, provided the cost of carrying the material allows it. With expanding urban distances and cheaper substitutes, the return to collecting should fall. The change from payment in vegetables to payment in silver, and much later to a municipal fee, fits that reasoning. It leaves room for political power at every stage, including the power of Watanabe to hold on to a right others wanted. A price tells us about an arrangement among people. The plant can tell us what the nitrogen did after it arrived.[1][3][4]

The city recovered nitrogen that some earlier process had already made fit for living things. There was a different way for a farmer to acquire it. In 1905, while Osaka’s old collection trade was still operating, a works in Norway began making fertilizer from nitrogen in the air. Nobody owned the air passing through the plant. Access to falling water, the electricity it could generate and a buyer for the product would decide what that air was worth.[5]
