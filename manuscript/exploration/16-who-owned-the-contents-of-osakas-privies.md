# Chapter 16 — Who Owned the Contents of Osaka’s Privies?

In the summer of 1724, farmers from two groups of villages fought over the right to empty privies in Osaka. The Yamazaki villages and their rivals from Takatsuki wanted access to particular districts. The trade had grown large enough to trouble the city at the waterfront. Manure boats used wharves where ships unloaded tea. People complained of the smell. The magistrates let the boats stay.[1]

Was the trade those farmers fought over assembled from a route that already carried food into town? The records begin after growers had started carrying waste back. The quarrels tell us what a grower needed, beyond a boat and a field, to bring the next load home.

## What a return trip was worth

Boats reached Osaka carrying vegetables and other produce. In a rice leaf, as in Hagino’s alga, sunlight drives hydrogen ions across a membrane. Their flow back helps the plant make sugars. Some of that energy reached Osaka as food. Each harvest also carried nitrogen and phosphorus out of cultivated soil. Much of that material passed through the city’s privies. Next season the fields would receive sunlight again. Where the soil lacked nitrogen, returning a load of manure could let a crop store more of that sunlight in grain and leaves.[1][2]

The farmers already had the boats in town. In the early years they exchanged some of their produce for human waste, known as night soil, and carried it home. The return journey gave this heavy cargo an advantage. A farmer still had to load it, take it from the landing to the field, and store or spread it. But the boat had a voyage home to make in any case. The food trade had brought a second trade within reach.[1][2]

Which fields could that arrangement serve? A grower had to set the value of the extra crop against two expenses, the price at the wharf and the work of getting the load to the field. Beyond some distance even a free load could cost too much to carry. A grower near the landing could pay silver for the same material and still come out ahead.[1][2]

This limit on the possible routes is a constraint in *Platonic space*. Whoever owned the boat, a privately financed voyage had to cover its costs. Farmers around Osaka also used fish fertilizer. When its price rose and growers opened new paddies, the city’s waste fetched more. By the early eighteenth century farmers had to offer silver where vegetables had once sufficed. A dearer substitute could bring a longer voyage within reach.[1][2]

## A load someone else owned

A tenancy in Osaka could assign the landlord the right to sell the occupants’ feces while the occupants retained the right to their urine. The solid material fetched more. Historian Susan Hanley found cases where rent rose after someone moved out. The landlord now had less to sell from the privy. A different division of the rights would have left the same quantity in the building and given the landlord a different reason to set the rent.[1]

On the buying side, villages organized to secure exclusive access to particular districts. Even a grower next to the landing could lose access to its cargo if another village held the right. The cost of carriage narrowed the number of fields a wharf could serve. A grant narrowed the choice again by naming who could load the boat. This was the prize in 1724.[1]

In 1772 urine dealers paid Osaka for recognition of a guild that could regulate their trade and set prices. Their authority stopped at the containers on street corners. Watanabe, a village of low status, held a separate grant for those. Others offered to buy the rights. Some challenged them or damaged the containers. Watanabe kept its monopoly through the Tokugawa period.[1]

The bidders placed a value on permission to collect from vessels already in place. Breaking a vessel spoiled the collection even while the right stood. Growers faced a third difficulty. Hanley found that as prices rose, poorer farmers had trouble securing enough fertilizer. The trade could earn a collector a living and still leave nutrients beyond the reach of a field that needed them.[1]

## When the direction of payment changed

After 1910 Osaka grew and farmers gained access to competing fertilizers. The return from selling night soil fell. Households went on filling their privies, but waste accumulated in the city center. In its own account of public health, Osaka dated its response to 1921. The municipality began charging to remove night soil alongside the remaining private collectors. For households that used the service, money now went out with the load.[3][4]

The crop still needed nutrients and the household needed its privy emptied. What failed was the overlap between the price a grower could offer and the cost of getting the load to a field. The earlier trade had allowed a farmer’s payment for one service to finance both. The city could now spend money for a reason of its own, to get the waste out of the neighborhood. In 1930 a change in national law made disposal a municipal responsibility.[3][4]

Osaka considered sewerage. Residents resisted the expense of installing flush toilets, and the city worked with the prefecture and agricultural associations to build storage tanks and supply farming villages. The tanks changed which deliveries were possible. A household could empty its privy before the grower needed fertilizer. The same nutrients could reach some of the same fields under a different timetable and from a different budget.[4]

Osaka’s farmers had assembled the trade from a voyage they already made. The crop’s need for nutrients gave them a reason to carry a return load. Collection rights secured the next one. When the farmers’ payments fell short, the route that survived depended on a municipal budget and a tank where a load could wait. Watanabe’s rejected bidders are evidence of how much choice that history contained. Other people could have carried the same nutrients to the same fields.[1][3][4]

The farmers’ boats returned nitrogen that living things had already made usable. In 1905 a works at Notodden in Norway began drawing nitrogen from a source no collector had to bring to the gate. It used the power of a waterfall to fix nitrogen from the air.[5]
