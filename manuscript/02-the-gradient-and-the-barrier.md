# Chapter 2 — The Gradient and the Barrier

In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot considered which loss England might bear more easily, its steam engines or its navy. He thought the country might do better to keep the engines. Lose the fleet and England would still have its mines and ironworks. Lose the engines and water would reclaim workings on which its iron industry depended for coal. A country could own the same coal on Tuesday that it owned on Monday and be a great deal poorer. This was how Carnot chose to introduce a book about heat.[1]

Carnot had a question the owners of a flooded mine would have understood. Was there a limit to the work a firing could do, even if the builders had their choice of machinery and every defect in it could be put right? He was prepared to dispense with steam altogether. An inventor might prefer to try alcohol or mercury. Carnot wanted to know what would set the limit even for an inventor ingenious enough to build a machine nobody had yet imagined.[1]

James Prescott Joule, working near Manchester, made the connection measurable from the other end. In the experiment he reported to the Royal Society in 1849, descending weights drove paddles in water. He measured the fall, raised the weights and repeated the operation until a thermometer could register the small rise in temperature reliably. The attention Joule paid to friction in his pulleys makes the logic of the experiment plain. Part of the work could warm the bearings on its way to the paddle. The thermometer in the water could account for only what reached it.[2]

Carnot had compared the opportunity to do work with a fall of water. The same water reaches the bottom of the wheel, but it has given up its height. He thought heat itself was a substance, caloric, passing from the boiler to the cold end of an engine. Joule’s measurements helped overturn that account. Mechanical work and heat are ways of transferring energy. What Carnot got right was that the temperature difference sets a limit on how much of the transfer can be taken as work. The miners at Whitehaven had a use for that difference.[1][2]

In a mine engine of the kind Thomas Newcomen built, a jet of cold water condensed steam beneath a piston. With the pressure beneath it reduced, the weight of the atmosphere above it could push the piston down. A beam tipped and lifted a pump rod at the other end. Follow a single stroke and the air all around the engine has a hand in lifting the water, as does the person who provided somewhere for the heat to go. The coal in the boiler fire keeps the whole performance repeatable.[3]

The boiler and the water used to cool the cylinder are at different temperatures. The engine can draw on that *disequilibrium* as long as somebody keeps the fire lit and the cold water coming. Across a thickness of iron, the change in temperature from place to place is called a *gradient*. Heat flows down it. A difference in pressure can likewise move water along a pipe. What it can do for the miners depends on the fit of the machinery. Let air leak freely past the piston, and the people firing the boiler may be just as busy while the people underground stay just as wet. The fitter has something to contend with that would be hard to find on either part by itself. Each might be a fine piece of metal. The clearance between them determines how much of the pressure difference survives long enough to do a stroke. This is what I mean by calling an assembly both material and *Platonic*. One can weigh the parts separately. To measure the fit one needs them both.

Coal and oxygen coexist in a yard because their reaction has a barrier. Lighting a fire supplies enough energy to get across it. A colliery faced a second expense even after the fire was lit. Its owners had to keep a first, inefficient engine going while people learned how to maintain it. The price of coal delivered to an engine varied sharply even within England. As Robert Allen points out, a design worth trying at the Newcastle coalfield could be a very poor buy at London prices. The machinery had the same physical possibilities in both places. They could afford different amounts of time to find them.[3]

## What the engine put within reach

Some years before Carlisle Spedding sank Saltom Pit, John Spedding sent Sir James Lowther an assessment of the first engine they had installed at Whitehaven. In all the time it had been working, John reported, the only people able to manage it or put a fault right were P. Walker and his own brother Carlisle. This was in 1719 or 1720. The machinery Lowther had undertaken to hire in 1715 was now working well enough for its agent to identify a problem that had scarcely existed when he signed. The district had learned how to pump by steam faster than it had learned how to replace an engine man.[3]

After the first installation, an engine man possessed a skill for which there had been no use at the pit a few years before. A man who could recognize a fault could restore hours of pumping without altering the rating of the engine by a single stroke. John thought this distinction valuable enough to report it by name. And one man on his list went on to use that knowledge for something larger than the repair of Lowther’s first engine.[3]

Carlisle’s later shaft at Saltom had horses winding coal beside two pumping houses built eight years apart. Other workings eventually benefited from the drainage. The first capacity people had wanted was the power to lift water in one pit. Once the district had machines and people able to work them, it could take on the drainage of connected workings. I call that a *higher assembly*. A new relation among existing parts brings work within reach that those parts could not do in their earlier arrangement.[3]

How often will such an arrangement be made again? In biology *autopoiesis* names an organization that produces its own components through its activity. I extend that idea to production when the coal won by pumping supplies fuel, wages and training for further pumping. The relevant organization is the mine together with its workforce and suppliers. An iron beam on its own has never hired a mechanic.[4]

There are two claims to keep track of here. The first concerns the opening of the seam. The second is a prediction about which arrangements recur. I expect those that tap a larger flow of usable energy to spread when some of the gain reaches the people and processes that can build more of them. John’s list identifies one place where that prediction can fail. The mine could raise its output while the knowledge needed to reproduce the achievement remained in the hands of just two men.[3][4]

## Where the heat goes

Does this history have a direction that can be stated as a law of physics? My conjecture is that a lasting difference can favor arrangements that draw it down faster. The strongest version proposes a *maximum rate of entropy production* among the routes the materials and constraints permit. Joule’s falling weights make clear why evidence for it depends on when the account is closed.

Entropy production measures irreversibility. In Joule’s apparatus, by the time the weights reached the floor some of their mechanical energy had become heat in the water. The water could not stir itself back into lifting the weights to their former height. A colliery put the interval before dissipation to another use. It stored part of the work as the height gained by the water it raised. Comparing direct conduction between two temperatures with an engine working between them gives a precise difficulty for my conjecture. At the same heat input, the engine that exports useful work can produce *less* entropy during the stroke. What eventually happens to that work, and what further fuel becomes accessible, affect the longer account.[2]

Saltom’s significance lies in the coal its drainage put within reach over years, including at other workings. Counting only what burned underneath its boilers leaves out the principal reason for their existence. An engine could thus accelerate a district’s use of coal while its operator tried to burn less fuel per unit of water raised. The two accounts cover different places and times. Physicists have proposed conditions under which stationary flows favor high entropy production. Establishing a maximum for Whitehaven would require a comparison with its other feasible means of drainage and an account of the coal they would have made accessible.[5]

A revealing difficulty turns up even in a world that consists entirely of chemistry on a computer screen. Jordan Horowitz and Jeremy England modeled mixtures in which only unusual combinations of abundances could draw strongly on an external source of work. A person choosing mixtures at random would seldom hit on one of those combinations. In the model a mixture got its ingredients from the mixture of a moment before. Let that history unfold, and unusual outcomes became more common than the random draw suggested. Some mixtures settled where the outside source could drive them unusually hard. Others settled where it could barely get a purchase. History made exceptional arrangements accessible in both directions.[6]

Those mixtures make a useful difficulty for my argument. History can open exceptional routes, including ones that take up very little of the energy on offer. The preference I propose concerns what these openings make possible over time. A route that releases more energy can help bring another such route into existence. To establish that preference we have to trace the return as well as the first release. For now we have taken the rules of chemistry as given. When we come to the early universe, we will ask whether even the rules could have a history of their own.

There is an old word, *dialectic*, for the relation between these reversals. The coal was both the object of extraction and a means for continuing it. The new organization preserved the miners’ ability to cut coal but made that work depend on an engine crew. An incremental gain in pumping capacity could have a discontinuous consequence at the depth of a seam. These are the three relations I mean by the interpenetration of opposites, the negation of the negation and the passage from quantity into quality. Naming them commits us to finding the dependency, the capability carried over and the threshold in the particular history.[4]

At Saltom, removing water made existing fuel accessible. At a vent on the floor of the Atlantic the water itself helps supply a chemical difference from which work can be drawn. In December 2000 a research party brought up a piece of chimney with its inhabitants still inside.
