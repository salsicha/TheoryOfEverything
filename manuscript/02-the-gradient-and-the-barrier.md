# Chapter 2 — The Gradient and the Barrier

In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot asked which loss would do England more harm, its navy or its steam engines. His answer was the engines.[1]

England could replace a ship while its ironworks had coal. Stop the pumps and water would rise in the mines that supplied the ironworks. Carnot wanted to know how much work those engines could do with the fuel they burned. Heat left every engine in the cooling water. How much of that loss could a builder prevent? He even considered a boiler filled with alcohol or mercury. The limit he wanted would apply to engines no one had built.[1]

The price of coal could explain where someone bought an engine. Carnot’s question went deeper. Even an owner who got coal for nothing needed a difference the engine could turn into motion. To understand how a new arrangement can open a source of work, we need to account both for the energy and for what makes some of it available.

In his cellar at Oak Field, outside Manchester, James Prescott Joule raised two lead weights about five feet above the stone floor. As they fell, their cords turned a brass paddle wheel in a copper vessel of water. He wound the weights up and let them drop twenty times in all. Between himself and the vessel he put a wooden screen to keep his body heat from affecting the reading.[2]

In the first trial the thermometer rose from 55.118 to 55.774 degrees Fahrenheit. Joule left the paddle still for the same length of time. The thermometer rose again, to 55.882. The cellar air was warmer than the water. He paired his stirring trials with readings at rest and recorded the air temperature. After correction, twenty drops of the weights warmed the water by just over half a degree Fahrenheit on average.[2]

Joule knew what the lead weighed and how far it fell. He allowed for the work spent turning the pulleys. In 1849 he reported his result to the Royal Society. Warming a pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit took about as much work as a 772-pound weight could supply by falling one foot.[2]

Carnot had treated heat as a substance passing from a boiler to a cooler. In Joule’s cellar the water warmed each time the lead lost height. Yet once the water reached room temperature, nothing in the vessel could lift the lead back up. A heat engine needs somewhere hotter to take in heat and somewhere cooler to give it up.[1][2]

At a mine, Thomas Newcomen found a use for cold water inside the engine itself. The piston fitted a cylinder open to the air at the top. Steam admitted beneath the piston filled the cylinder as the weight of the pump rods at the other end of the beam pulled the piston up. Then a jet of cold water went into the cylinder. The steam condensed into a small amount of liquid, leaving less pressure below the piston than the air exerted above. Now the air could drive the piston down and pull the rods up. Getting the steam out of the way at the right moment was how the engine lifted water.[3]

A fireman could burn coal all day without lifting anything if air leaked past the piston as fast as the steam condensed underneath. The boiler and the cooling water supplied a temperature difference, or *gradient*, that made work possible. The fit between cylinder and piston allowed a second difference, in pressure, to last for the duration of a stroke. This is what I mean by the jointly material and *Platonic* character of an assembly. An engine maker could measure the diameter of either piece on a bench. To know whether the engine would pump, he needed the difference between those two measurements.[3]

Coal and oxygen can remain together for years. Lighting the coal supplies the energy needed to start combustion. Building and running the first engines presented a different barrier. They used a great deal of fuel. A colliery with cheap coal at the pithead could afford to run one long enough for its crew to learn how to use it. Robert Allen’s comparison of British fuel prices shows why the same design could be worth trying on the Newcastle coalfield and too expensive in London. More fuel could make a new machine practical before anyone learned to make it efficient.[3]

## What the engine put within reach

Around 1720 John Spedding wrote to Sir James Lowther about the pumping engine Lowther had rented at Whitehaven since 1715. John knew of only two men who could manage the engine and put it right when it failed. One was P. Walker. The other was John’s brother Carlisle.[3]

A drawing could preserve the position of the cylinder after a change of crew. When the pump stopped lifting water, someone still had to find the fault. The scale on which a district could use steam therefore depended on whether one of the two men could pass on his skill. By the time Carlisle sank Saltom Pit, he had learned that part of the trade.[3]

At Saltom, Carlisle’s pumps drained the shaft on one side while horses wound coal up the other. In time the pumps served neighboring workings. This connected mine is what I mean by a *higher assembly*. We can identify its new capacity on the day a crew first enters a seam the old pumps could never keep dry. We can then ask a separate question about its energetic advantage. Over a season, how much more coal could the district deliver after paying the cost of drainage? The seam could open and the expense of keeping it open could still use up the gain. In that case a capacity would have been created and my prediction of a greater usable return would have failed there.[3]

The first morning miners can enter a newly drained seam, the owners have yet to sell a pound of its coal. By the time the owners order another pump they may be using the proceeds of working that very seam. In biology, *autopoiesis* names the way an organization produces the components it needs to go on producing. I use the term for industry where we can actually follow part of a mine’s return into fuel, repairs, training or another installation. The distinction in time matters. The second machine may be paid for by a capacity that did nothing to meet the cost of building the first.[4]

Why should assemblies that draw on a larger flow become more common? Two mines might both win coal enough to meet their running costs. The one with a surplus can afford to free an experienced hand to teach an apprentice, or carry a second installation through weeks when it produces nothing. There is a reason to expect more working pumps at the end of that process. If the surplus is all spent elsewhere, the reason disappears. John Spedding’s letter identifies a dependency on which this distinction could turn. An owner who could buy all the coal he needed might still have nobody to send when a cylinder leaked.[3][4]

## Where the heat goes

Can the direction of these changes be stated as a law of physics? My stronger conjecture is that, under specified conditions, assemblies develop that dissipate an available energy difference at the fastest sustainable rate. Physicists call the proposed quantity a *maximum rate of entropy production*. To ask whether a mine approached it, we have to say what to count and for how long.

Joule had to wind his weights back up after every descent. The water never cooled of its own accord in just such a way as to turn the paddle backward and lift the lead. In the downward run, an amount of energy that could have been used to lift a load had been dispersed through the water and the vessel. *Entropy production* measures the irreversibility of such a change. A mine engine allowed some energy to lift a load before it ended up as dispersed heat. Over one stroke that could produce less entropy than letting the heat pass directly from hot to cold. Over years the same engine could make a great deal more coal available to burn. The answer depends on when the observer stops counting.[2]

The engine crew had reason to burn less coal for each gallon of water raised. The mine owners had reason to pump more water and sell more coal. Both could succeed. Establishing the maximum possible rate for the whole district would require figures for how much coal every feasible drainage method could release, how much fuel it consumed and where the extracted coal went. Physical arguments for high entropy production under steady conditions specify their own boundaries and alternatives.[5]

Jordan Horowitz and Jeremy England let a computer follow successive reactions in a mixture driven by an outside source of energy. Mixtures capable of absorbing large amounts of energy were rare when sampled at random. Under the drive, some mixtures began to absorb unusually large amounts. Others came to absorb unusually little. Both outcomes appeared more often than random sampling would predict.[6]

The model puts a limit on what follows from having an energy supply. Repeated reactions favored particular mixtures, including some that absorbed very little. To get from that result to a preference for higher extraction takes another step. An arrangement with a larger flow must be able to use its advantage to renew a part, extend its reach or produce a successor. We can look for the missing step in what one generation leaves for the next.[4][6]

The coal mine suggests how to recognize further creation when it occurs. Coal is both the object of the work and a means of doing it. Dialecticians call that relation the *interpenetration of opposites*. It changes what it pays to preserve. The pump overcame the limit that had threatened to put the shaft and its crews out of use, and thereby gave the old winding gear and the cutters a new use at depth. That recovery of an old capacity on new terms is the *negation of the negation*. The decisive measurement is the water level. Inches of drainage become the difference between a seam people can enter and one they cannot. That is the change from *quantity to quality*. Together these claims tell us where to look for novelty, including in a mine where the horses are still walking round the same pole.[3][4]
