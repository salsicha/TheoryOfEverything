# Chapter 2 — The Gradient and the Barrier

Take two blocks of iron, one hot and one cold. Insulate them from the room and press them together. Wait until they reach the same temperature. Now pull them apart.

Separating the iron takes very little effort. Now try putting the temperature difference back.

Between them the blocks still have the energy they started with. What they have lost is a chance to do work. While one was hot and the other cold, a small heat engine between them could have lifted a weight. Leave them touching until morning and that opportunity is gone. You can make one block hot and the other cold again. Somebody will have to supply the work.[1]

A mine engine lives on a difference of this kind. Fuel keeps the boiler hot. There must also be somewhere cooler for heat to go. The engine takes a share of the energy passing between hot and cold and puts it into the motion of a rod. Far down the shaft, water rises. That last event is worth attending to. The two blocks managed to come to the same temperature quite nicely by themselves. It took some ingenuity to arrange for a load of water to change level along the way.

The difference that can drive a flow is a *gradient*. A temperature gradient can drive heat through iron. A pressure gradient can drive water through a pipe. A difference in chemical potential can drive molecules across a permeable membrane. Being out of equilibrium gives a system something to spend. How fast it can spend it, and what happens on the way, depend on what connects the two sides. A piston that fits its cylinder closely enough can turn a pressure difference into a stroke. Let the steam leak around it and much of the same fuel can burn while the rod barely moves. A tolerance narrow enough to give a machinist trouble is part of what gives the mine its power.

That is the sense in which the book calls every assembly both material and Platonic. The piston has a weight. Its fit is a relation between two pieces of metal, and neither piece has that fit in isolation. The water coming up the shaft owes as much to the relation as it does to the iron.

There are two ways the crew in the last chapter might get a failing pump past its first bad week. A fitter could find the leak and make each firing count for more. Or the mine could supply enough cheap fuel and paid labor to keep a wasteful engine going until someone learned to improve it. One changes the route by which the energy does its work. The other gives the crew more to spend on a route it can already use. Looking back from the day the efficient machine finally runs, it is easy to credit the fitter and forget whoever kept the bad engine fed. At a mine that had to pay dearly for its coal, the same fitter might never have had a second week.[2]

## The spare that earns nothing

Give two imagined collieries equally good pumps. At both, the water falls below the seam. Each crew raises coal that nobody could reach a week ago. Each sells its first load for the same price. If the story of invention ended at the first sale, these mines would have identical histories.

At one mine, the person who collects the proceeds pays a fitter to make a spare rod. It lies against a wall. At the other, the owner takes the money out of the business. For as long as the rods in service hold, both mines raise the same coal. Anyone inspecting the yard can point to exactly where the first mine’s money went. It is a length of iron doing nothing.

Then a rod breaks. At the first mine the fitter draws the spare out of stores and the crew has a chance to put the pump back to work before the water reaches the passage. Let the same fault occur at the second. An order has to go out. Someone has to agree to pay. While those perfectly ordinary things happen above ground, the water below ground is rising at its ordinary rate. The cost of waiting changes when it covers the entrance to the seam. At that point the mine has to pay for the repair *and* for the work of winning back the ground it had already won.

Here is the distinction on which the argument turns. **Reaching the coal and keeping the means of reaching it are two achievements.** A machine can manage the first in a single afternoon. To explain the second you have to leave the engine house and follow a load of coal to its buyer, part of the payment to a fitter, and a rod back to the shaft. The mine that takes in more usable energy can afford more of that work. Whether it gets the work is a question about the journey home.

This is where the book places *autopoiesis*, the capacity of an organization to renew the means of its own operation. The term comes from biology. At a mine the iron has done very little to reproduce itself. Draw the circle wider and there are people being paid, skills being passed on, orders being filled, and fuel for another firing. Calling that wider organization self-renewing commits us to finding those connections. Ten rods in a yard could equally well be the last delivery to a mine about to close. A count tells us what has accumulated. The arrival of the eleventh rod tells us something about what the place can still do.[3]

The spare also makes trouble for a simple picture of energetic progress. For six good months it can tie up part of the return while adding nothing to output. Over a winter of breakdowns it can save a working seam. The meaning of *more* depends on what we count and for how long. A hungry boiler can use more fuel in an hour than a good one. A well-run mine can use a better boiler to reach more coal in a year. And an owner can take every penny of an excellent year elsewhere. The proposed tendency is quite specific. Assemblies that open greater access have a better chance of recurring **when part of the gain sustains the work that produces them again**. An arrangement that exhausts its fuel bed or its workforce can be outlasted by one that takes less.

## Where the heat goes

The mine leaves a harder physical question. Does a world out of equilibrium tend to build the routes along which it can run down its gradients fastest? This is the conjecture about maximum entropy production. Entropy production keeps account of irreversibility. It registers something the two blocks taught us how to feel in our hands. After heat has spread, restoring the original difference has a price.

Think of steady heat transfer between two reservoirs held at fixed temperatures. Let more heat pass directly from hot to cold each second and the rate of entropy production rises in proportion. Now put a working engine in the gap. Suppose a hundred joules leave the hot side each second. Ten lift a weight and ninety arrive as heat on the cold side. With the same temperatures and heat input, less entropy is produced along that route than if all hundred had passed straight through as heat. The ten joules are still there in the height of the weight. What becomes of them when it comes down belongs to the rest of the story.[1]

This is why a claim about a maximum needs a boundary. The engineer who wants more useful work from a firing and the physicist who follows all the energy into the surroundings can both be keeping honest accounts. Their figures answer different questions. Dewar and Maritan have argued that, under particular constraints, a steady state with high entropy production can be compatible with more histories of its environment and hence be more probable. The specified conditions matter. Applying a rate principle to an operating engine is one problem. Explaining how the engine came to be there between hot and cold is another.[4]

A clue comes from a world small enough to build in a computer. Jordan Horowitz and Jeremy England modeled networks of chemical reactions in which only uncommon mixtures could couple strongly to an outside source of work. A list of every possible mixture would give little reason to expect one of these. Let the reactions actually take place, however, and the list becomes a poor guide. Each reaction changes what the next one has to work with. Some histories settled where the external drive acted unusually strongly. Others settled where it acted unusually weakly. The chemistry found its way into both kinds of exceptional state by following the transitions available from one moment to the next.[5]

The model takes the rules of chemistry as given. Whether even some physical rules could have formed in a reality still acquiring definite structure is the question of ontological uncertainty we will meet in chapter 6. Here, one step downstream, we can ask a question about the mixtures themselves. Why might one of those arrangements leave a longer history than another? At the mine we could follow the return far enough to see it resting against the wall. The surplus paid for a part that kept the route to further surplus open. This is what the book means by a higher assembly. Yesterday the crew and pump could reach the seam. Today the shaft, the repair shop, and the trade between them can keep that capacity alive through a failure. Some of what yesterday’s organization produced has become part of the equipment with which tomorrow starts. Chapters on discovery and intelligence will have to find equally tangible connections if the same argument is to hold there.

At the shaft, we can say which things changed places in that history. Coal began as the prize at the far end of the operation and came back as the means of doing it again. Flooding had taken away the crew’s route to the lower seam. Powered drainage gave it back on new terms. Now getting at the coal depended on the pump, and keeping the pump running could depend on the coal. For the crew, the decisive change came when more water went out each shift than seeped back in. Once the water stayed below the entrance, they could work. Those changes give the book’s dialectical language its substance. Each has a moment when something happens that a person standing at the shaft could recognize.[3]

There is still a pair of hands outside the pump. The fitter can set down a tool, walk out of the engine house, and go home. A cell faces a stranger task. As it draws on a chemical difference, it has to keep rebuilding the very boundary across which that difference can do work. To see how much of that job the surrounding world might once have done, we can go to a place where water and rock are already at work on the problem.
