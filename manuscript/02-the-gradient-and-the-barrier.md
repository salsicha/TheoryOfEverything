# Chapter 2 — The Gradient and the Barrier

In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot asked which loss would do England more harm, its navy or its steam engines. His answer was the engines. England could build ships as long as it had ironworks and coal. Its ironworks depended on mines deep enough to flood when the pumps stopped.[1]

Carnot wanted to know how much work an engine could get from a given amount of heat. An improved engine might waste less fuel. But was there a limit even for a perfectly built machine? He asked whether anything would change if the builders used alcohol or mercury in place of water.[1]

Near Manchester, James Prescott Joule measured how much mechanical work it took to warm water. In an experiment he reported to the Royal Society in 1849, he let weights fall and turn paddles in a vessel. The temperature rise was so small that he had to lift the weights and drop them repeatedly to measure it reliably. He also had to account for friction in the pulleys. Some of the work warmed the bearings before it ever reached the water.[2]

Carnot believed heat was a substance that passed from the hot end of the engine to the cold end. Joule’s measurements helped correct that account. A falling weight could do work and warm the water. Carnot’s most important conclusion still held. In a heat engine, the difference between the hot and cold temperatures sets a limit on how much heat the engine can convert into work.[1][2]

In Thomas Newcomen’s mine engine, steam filled a cylinder beneath a piston. A jet of cold water condensed the steam and lowered the pressure. Atmospheric pressure pushed the piston down. At the other end of a beam, a pump rod rose and lifted water out of the mine. Coal heated the boiler for the next stroke.[3]

The hot boiler and the cold water were out of equilibrium. That temperature difference supplied the engine with a way to do work. The change in temperature over a distance is called a *gradient*. The piston also had to fit its cylinder closely enough to hold a pressure difference. A gap let air leak past, however much coal the fireman burned. I call this the material and *Platonic* character of an assembly. The piston and cylinder are material things. Their fit is a relation between them, and it has a measurable effect on how much water the engine can lift.

Coal and oxygen can remain together for years. Lighting the coal supplies the energy needed to start combustion. Building and running the first engines presented a different barrier. They used a great deal of fuel. A colliery with cheap coal at the pithead could afford to run one long enough for its crew to learn how to use it. Robert Allen’s comparison of British fuel prices shows why the same design could be worth trying on the Newcastle coalfield and too expensive in London. More fuel could make a new machine practical before anyone learned to make it efficient.[3]

## What the engine put within reach

In 1719 or 1720 John Spedding wrote to Sir James Lowther about the engine Lowther had leased at Whitehaven in 1715. In all the time it had run, John could name just two people who knew how to manage it or put it right when it broke. One was P. Walker. The other was John’s brother Carlisle.[3]

Lowther’s lease bought him the use of the engine. Several years into the lease, a breakdown could still leave the whole operation dependent on two men. One of them went on to sink Saltom Pit.[3]

At Saltom, Carlisle’s pumps first drained a shaft where horses wound coal to the surface. In time they served other workings as well. Machines, workers and a set of connected workings could now drain a larger area. This is what I mean by a *higher assembly*. Existing parts arranged in a new way can do work they could not do before.[3]

The first opening of a seam is one event. For other pits to do the same, someone has to supply fuel, train engine men and pay for equipment. Biologists use *autopoiesis* for the way a living organization produces its own components. I extend it to industry where the proceeds from working a mine help supply the people and equipment needed to do the work again.[4]

I expect an arrangement that gives access to more usable energy to become more common when some of the gain goes toward building further installations. John Spedding’s letter shows a limit on that process. A district can have enough coal to run many pumps and only two people able to repair one.[3][4]

## Where the heat goes

Can the direction of these changes be stated as a law of physics? My stronger conjecture is that, under specified conditions, assemblies develop that dissipate an available energy difference at the fastest sustainable rate. Physicists call the proposed quantity a *maximum rate of entropy production*. To ask whether a mine approached it, we have to say what to count and for how long.

In Joule’s apparatus, falling weights stirred water and warmed it. Once the water was still, its warmth alone could never lift the weights back to their starting height in the same cycle. This loss of the ability to do work is one consequence of entropy production. A mine engine used some of the energy to lift water before the energy dispersed as heat. During a stroke it could therefore produce less entropy than a direct transfer of the same heat. Over years, draining a seam changed how much coal people could burn.[2]

The engine crew had reason to burn less coal for each gallon of water raised. The mine owners had reason to pump more water and sell more coal. Both could succeed. Establishing the maximum possible rate for the whole district would require figures for how much coal every feasible drainage method could release, how much fuel it consumed and where the extracted coal went. Physical arguments for high entropy production under steady conditions specify their own boundaries and alternatives.[5]

Jordan Horowitz and Jeremy England found a complication in a computer model of chemical reactions. Only a few mixtures in the model could absorb much energy from an external source. Those mixtures were rare in a random sample. When the researchers let successive reactions change the mixtures over time, some came to absorb an unusually large amount of energy. Others absorbed unusually little. Both kinds arose more often than random sampling would suggest.[6]

That result supports one part of my argument. Earlier reactions can direct later ones toward combinations that would be hard to find by chance. The result also leaves open which combinations will persist. To explain a preference for greater energy use, we need to show how the additional energy helps produce more of the arrangements that use it.

The mine illustrates three ideas from *dialectics*. Coal was both the fuel the miners sought and the fuel that helped them get it. That mutual dependence is the *interpenetration of opposites*. The engine overcame a limit of the old mine and gave the cutters and horse teams new work. This is what I mean by *negation of the negation*. Finally, the water could fall an inch at a time until the seam was dry enough to work. That threshold is the change from *quantity to quality*. In each case the words describe a particular change in the mine.[4]

At Saltom, water stood between the miners and their fuel. At a vent on the floor of the Atlantic, the water carries chemicals from which living things draw energy. In December 2000 a research team brought up a piece of the vent with animals still inside.
