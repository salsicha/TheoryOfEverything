# Afterword — What Would Make This a Theory?

In October 1834 the witnesses at Fowey Consols measured how much water William West’s engine could raise with a bushel of coal. Fourteen years later the directors of the South Devon Railway gave up a service that had carried passengers by atmospheric pressure. A pressure difference did useful work in both places. The mine could sell what it brought up. The railway had to pay to keep a valve tight along the line. Could we have identified the difference that mattered before we knew which enterprise continued? [Cornish trial and railway records](05-how-disequilibrium-builds-a-new-pathway.md#chapter-notes)

The book asks how a system acquires a capacity it lacked. To explain an invention we have to identify the source of usable energy, the constraints on how it could be used and what became possible when the parts came together. Then we can ask how often the new arrangement appeared. If some of the gain went into producing it again, we have a reason for its recurrence. We still need to explain the first one.

The proposal extends from machines to living things and, more tentatively, to the universe. For a learning system we can trace the equipment, records and decisions that people supplied. For a physical constant we have only the universe in which to look for a history. Calling both assembled commits us to finding a cause in each case.

The same is true of dialectics. If two activities depend on each other, changing either should affect the other. If a new arrangement overcomes a limit while preserving an earlier capacity, we should be able to name what survived and see it at work. If a change in quantity brings a change in kind, we should be able to say which quantity and predict roughly where the change occurs. These claims can fail separately. Even if all three succeed, a further argument is needed to connect them to ontological uncertainty. Deterministic models can produce such changes too. [The three principles](../research/sources.md#s130)

## What changes the next starting point?

Saltom’s pumping and winding installations stood on opposite sides of the shaft. Faster winding could do little while the seam was under water. Once the pumps had drained it, the horses could lift coal to the surface. Getting coal away from the beach gave Ravenhill’s neighboring winding shaft a use in turn. We can distinguish the first day miners could enter the seam, the years they kept it in use and what happened at the next shaft. Each needs a different explanation. [Saltom and Ravenhill](01-the-square-with-one-answer.md)

A comparison could begin by varying the available energy and the suspected constraint, separately and together. Which combination allows a new arrangement to form? Once it exists, measure how much usable energy it actually obtains. To test what makes it recur, interrupt the part of the return said to pay for another instance, with other supplies held steady. Does recurrence decline? Does it recover when that return is restored?

Much depends on what gets counted. A loom can produce the same picture a hundred times because someone supplies the silk, cards and labor. A cell supplies many of the parts required to produce a daughter. An account of recurrence should say in advance which organization it follows and what counts as another instance. A repair, a repeated operation and an offspring answer different questions. Count losses and chances of detection as well as appearances. If an assembly often recurs without the return predicted to sustain it, that would count against the claim. [Assembly and frequency](../research/sources.md#s02) · [Biological self-production](../research/sources.md#s33)

How soon did life appear on Earth? The first persistent environment suitable for life, the oldest surviving sign of life and the date estimated for a common ancestor are three different things. None gives us the day life began. Early traces may have been destroyed. We are also observers on a planet where life began early enough to produce observers. Any estimate of the waiting time has to allow for both facts. [Early liquid water](../research/sources.md#s131) · [Evidence for early life](../research/sources.md#s132) · [The timing question](../research/sources.md#s135)

A claim that life can form promptly when conditions are ready needs an independent account of *ready*. Which materials had to be present, which reactions could supply energy, and how long should formation take? A history with the predicted conditions and a long delay would tell against the proposal. Knowing that few configurations are possible gives us no rate on its own. Even a constraint invisible to the eye must make a measurable difference to what can happen.

For the vent proposal, a model could compare the same minerals and chemical gradient with and without a network capable of renewing its components. How much energy could each arrangement use over the same interval, after paying the costs of building and renewing the network? One arrangement might give a higher yield per reaction and another process more material per hour. Choosing which result the theory predicts is part of the work. This would test an extension of Smith and Morowitz’s account of metabolism. [Smith and Morowitz](../research/sources.md#s140) · [Vent chemistry](../research/sources.md#s144)

At Saltom, some coal went under the boiler and much more could go into fires elsewhere. At Fowey Consols, the mine imported coal to reach metal ores. A study confined to the engine houses could measure pumping duty at both sites and miss what made either engine worth building. Where we measure and for how long are part of the prediction. [The fuel accounts](05-how-disequilibrium-builds-a-new-pathway.md)

We also have to identify the physical difference that supplied useful work, perhaps a difference in temperature or chemical potential. The fact that researchers ate and their buildings were heated tells us they had an energy supply. To explain why they worked on one problem at a particular time, we need to follow how some of that energy was used.

Tang Tingshu worked this out in money and cartloads at Kaiping. His proposed colliery would produce more coal than the district’s carts could carry at an acceptable cost. His railway estimate changed how much coal it made sense to mine. A theory that predicts where effort will go should be able to use estimates like his before learning whether the railway was built. [Tang’s transport comparison](05-how-disequilibrium-builds-a-new-pathway.md#three-hundred-carts)

Dates can help establish which way an influence ran. Income from a first undertaking might finance a later investigation. Its results can affect a still later investment. Dated accounts also let us look for a third change, such as a fall in the price of a necessary material, that could explain both investments.

Consider the forty francs Mademoiselle Pommet was owed. We can trace them to a hearing in Lyon where her employer acknowledged the debt. The newspaper has no date of payment. To explain how wages kept a workshop going, we would want accounts from the household as well as the employer. What did Pommet live on while she waited? [Pommet’s hearing](09-the-system-that-produces-its-tomorrow.md#chapter-notes)

In 1453 Alice Chaucer divided the Walsham demesne among twenty-two tenants at fixed rents. People went on growing crops on the same land under different terms. To connect that change to a failure of feudal guarantees, we would need evidence of which service had failed, what else the estate had tried and what the rents allowed the tenants and landowner to do. [Walsham and the terms of protection](09-the-system-that-produces-its-tomorrow.md#the-price-of-protection)

## A model that can fail

One way to test the theory is to model a small number of assemblies whose construction requirements are known. Specify which earlier structure each one needs, how long it takes to build and which energy flows it changes. The model should allow equipment to wear out and a promising assembly to remain impossible to build.

Mathematics can help us decide what to measure. In a puzzle we can trace which choices depend on earlier ones. For a physical system near an unstable state we can measure whether a small disturbance grows or dies away. In a production network we can ask whether necessary parts can be replaced as fast as they wear out. For a controller we can ask which signals let it distinguish situations that need different responses. In a record of past behavior we can measure how much it lets us predict about what comes next.

With resources held steady, changing one dependency between the parts might make a previously impossible result attainable. Interrupting the supply of a replacement part should give a different prediction, an interval before reserves run out. Giving a controller information about where it is looking should affect a third outcome, how well it performs a task with the same equipment.

The difficult question is whether these mechanisms, taken together, explain a tendency to draw more usable energy. Compare a model that links the return to reproduction with one that offers the same opportunities to form and has the same replacement needs, but in which the return makes no difference to reproduction. Which one better predicts what forms and persists?

Give each assembly only the materials and energy available at the time. Calculate what it draws, what is lost and what its operations can repair or reproduce. Does access to more energy actually help it produce successors? An assembly that could use enormous amounts of energy may never appear if none of its precursors can get past the initial expense. A simulation that simply chooses the best future outcome has already assumed the result.

A barrier can be lowered or people can spend more to get over it. An arrangement that pays immediately and one that needs support for years can look equally productive once each is established. That difference mattered to the English mine. It mattered in a different way to the bacteria that acquired the capacity to use citrate. A common account should make a prediction in addition to what we already know about each case.

Profit might predict which engine owners buy. Communication and shared mathematics might predict which equation two physicists will arrive at. The wider theory has to tell us something those accounts leave open. A claim that a process actually maximizes the rate of entropy production goes further still. It requires a comparison with accessible alternatives.

For an artificial player, we can take away information about its own attention and see what it loses with the same equipment, task and time. Better performance with the information would show a use for a self-model. Subjective experience is a further question. To test a connection between control and autopoiesis, we would have to trace both directions. Which components does the controller help replace, and which operations replace the controller?

For a language model, keep the trained weights and questions fixed. Change the prompt, the computing time or access to an outside check, one at a time. Then compare the results with further training. Record the answers it gets wrong on a second attempt as well as the ones it corrects, and the cost of each. A gain in accuracy says something about the calculation. To use it as evidence for an energy claim, follow what someone actually does with the answer.

In the case of relativity, two papers can agree on a transformation yet differ on what clocks measure. Predicting agreement on the equation would be an achievement. It would still leave Einstein’s account of the clocks to explain.

To test the claim that AI emerges naturally, say in advance which capability is expected, what resources and prior knowledge it requires, and over what period. Would a change in access to one of those resources affect when the capability appears? Failures and successful substitutes matter as much as the familiar machine that was built.

Data centers make a separate prediction. Compare the work done in large shared facilities with similar work done on dispersed equipment. If capable artificial systems repeatedly develop in places without the predicted concentration of equipment, that finding would weaken the argument for data centers while leaving room for the argument about artificial cognition.

At Saltom, investors paid for a harbour that storms destroyed. At Kaiping, water in the mine was a real difficulty even while cart transport was too expensive for the proposed output. In chip design, Cheng’s group found that an older search method often produced shorter wires. Each case had a real demand and more than one way things could turn out. [The mine](01-the-square-with-one-answer.md) · [The chips](12-the-machines-that-build-the-next-machines.md)

How long should we measure? A reserve can reduce what a system does today and allow it to keep working tomorrow. A peak rate, a steady rate and an average across the whole history are different predictions. There should be a physical reason for choosing the interval before seeing the result.

At Kaiping, the cart estimate gave a reason to expect that a change above ground would alter what happened in the mine. In 1905, the rules for changing reference frames narrowed the equations a physicist could use. On a chip, a requirement for signals to arrive on time could favor a layout with longer wires. In each case the constraint predicts where another hour of work might pay. [Tang](05-how-disequilibrium-builds-a-new-pathway.md#three-hundred-carts) · [Poincaré](04-the-discovery-that-became-possible.md#a-result-that-could-travel-by-post) · [Chip placement](12-the-machines-that-build-the-next-machines.md#laying-out-a-chip)

## Where the laws come from

Chapter 6 begins with a philosophical premise. Some distinctions are indeterminate in reality itself. I argue that absolute nothingness would be a completely determinate absence and that indeterminacy rules it out. Moving from that argument to a particular piece of physical structure requires further steps. If time is one of the things that forms, what orders those steps? A quantum uncertainty relation already presupposes physical quantities. A vacuum already has properties. Neither yet explains how those quantities and properties came to be.

Suppose two accounts of cosmic history reproduce the same constant. Does one also predict a relation between two other quantities, or a measurable pattern that its rival cannot explain? That would give us a reason to prefer one account beyond its agreement with the number it was designed to explain.

There is a narrower question about whether interaction with its surroundings can help keep a field in a long-lived vacuum state. In a model, vary that interaction while keeping the field’s potential and starting state fixed. Vary the expansion history separately. Loss of quantum coherence, the state the field occupies and the time it takes to escape from it are separate outcomes. If the barrier by itself accounts for the lifetime, environmental interaction adds little to this explanation. Applying such a result to our universe would require an observable difference. [Decoherence](../research/sources.md#s136) · [Vacuum decay](../research/sources.md#s137)

For an assembled law or constant we need to show how a present regularity could arise from earlier conditions. The possible outcomes and their frequencies should follow from the model. We also need to know which rules the model takes for granted. An explanation for the universe’s initial disequilibrium has more to do if it starts by assuming the very energy gradient it sets out to explain.

My judgment that the constants were probably assembled is a conjecture. The formation of objects under known laws does not establish the origin of the laws themselves. We should be able to identify a measurement that would favor an assembled law and one that would count against it.

Start with a dimensionless ratio so the answer cannot be changed by choosing different units. Can a model produce its value? Does work drawn from a gradient restore the arrangement that fixes the ratio when disturbed? If it recovers just as well after that supply is interrupted, ordinary stability may explain it. Then compare the arrangements that can form with the same energy over the same interval. If a lower-power arrangement regularly outlasts an accessible rival that can sustain a higher rate, the maximum-extraction claim fails in that case. The ratio could still have been assembled.

A model could make one constant look natural by putting an equally precise number into the starting conditions. Follow the constraints back far enough to see whether that has happened. We can question how anyone estimated the odds of our universe without yet having a better explanation for its particular values. A convincing alternative would produce a value or a relation we can check.

Symmetry breaking also raises several distinct questions. A surplus of matter over antimatter calls for a quantitative account. The difference between left and right in weak interactions, the number of spatial dimensions and the direction of time each need an explanation of their own. To establish a maximum in entropy production, we must also identify the physical boundary, the interval and the rates along other accessible paths. An asymmetry alone supplies none of those measurements.

The argument can fail closer to home. Suppose an assembly draws more energy, yet after costs it is no more likely to reproduce. Suppose its apparent gain depends on a subsidy we left out, or that its supposed successor appears just as often where the earlier assembly is absent. Each outcome tells us which connection we got wrong.

At Fowey the beam kept moving for nearly three hours while the Lilly lift raised no water. The witnesses deducted that time from the work they credited to the lift. I would put their question to each case in this book. For the energy spent, what could someone or something actually do that had been impossible before?
