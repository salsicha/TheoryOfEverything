# Chapter 6 — From Uncertainty to an Assembled Universe

In 1850 Léon Foucault sent light through a tube of water and timed its journey with a turning mirror. By the time the light returned, the mirror had turned a little farther. A late ray struck at a different angle and sent an image sideways. Foucault could measure that displacement. He compared it with the displacement of an image formed by light traveling through air.[1]

Both waves and particles had been invoked to explain why a ray bends as it enters water. They made opposite predictions about its speed. The familiar particle account required the light to speed up in water. The wave account required it to slow down. The light that had gone through Foucault’s tube came back later.[1]

For a physicist tracing a ray, the speed of light in water is a number to put into the calculation. For someone studying water, it is a property of the material to explain. I suspect that some of the numbers every star obeys have a history too. This is a harder claim than saying the stars formed. A history of star formation can take gravity for granted. An account of how a law acquired its present form owes us an explanation of what made that form possible and what keeps it in force.

## What an experiment can settle

In Delft in 2015, Bas Hensen and his colleagues arranged for two diamonds 1.3 kilometres apart to share an entangled pair of electron spins, one in each diamond. Once a pair was ready, the experimenters could choose which measurement to make on each spin. Two stations were necessary. They were far enough apart and made their choices late enough that a message traveling at the speed of light could not tell either station which measurement the other had chosen before its result was recorded. Later the teams could bring the records together. Over 245 trials the results agreed in a pattern too strong for the broad class of theories they were testing, in which each site carries local answers set in advance and the choices are independent of them.[1]

A quantum vacuum already has fields and laws that describe how those fields can be excited. By *absolute nothingness* I mean the absence even of a possibility of change. My starting premise is that indeterminacy belongs to reality. If that premise holds, absolute nothingness cannot be its starting state. It would require every possibility to be settled in advance, every one of them excluded. The premise is philosophical. Someone who takes fixed laws as basic can consistently decline it. What might count in its favor is an account of how a distinction once open came to be settled so reliably that two laboratories can now measure the same value.[2][3]

There is an ordinary sort of uncertainty in Foucault’s story. Before he started his mirror, the physicists disagreed about a property the water could already exhibit. They could give instructions for finding out which of them was right. I am proposing a deeper uncertainty, *ontological uncertainty*. A distinction we now measure may itself have become definite in the course of physical events. The Delft records make it hard to maintain that local answers to every possible question were there all along. They leave room, among other possibilities, for a deterministic account with nonlocal relations. Choosing between those accounts, and explaining how a world comes to have definite properties at all, are different undertakings.[1][3]

If time also emerged, a theory would have to explain how earlier conditions led to later ones without assuming a clock already existed. For the same reason, a claim about a *rate* of energy extraction applies only where elapsed time has a physical meaning.

## Counting after the shaking stops

At the University of Twente, Ko van der Weele and his colleagues put six hundred glass beads in a tube divided in half by a wall twenty-three millimetres high. A shaker sent beads bouncing over the wall. At high frequency the counts stayed near three hundred on each side. The researchers slowed the shaking, keeping the length of each stroke the same. One compartment could now end up almost full.[4]

A bead crossing the wall lands among others. At every collision it loses some of its motion. On the more crowded side, it is likely to hit several neighbors before it has a chance to get back over the wall. When the shaker is vigorous there are usually enough fast beads in the larger crowd to send the surplus back. Lower the drive sufficiently and adding beads can actually reduce the number that escape. At that point the first accidental excess helps decide where the next beads will stay. The crowded side fills up by the same rule that applies to the empty one.[4]

To count the beads they switched off the shaker. A few stragglers on the emptier side could still clear the wall. A bead in the crowd rarely had the speed to come back. Those last few bounces made one compartment appear completely full at a setting where the theory still predicted a few beads on the other side.[4]

Either side could fill. The researchers could predict the imbalance without knowing which side it would favor. Physicists call this loss of symmetry in the state of a system *symmetry breaking*. For an initially chance difference to survive, it had to change what the next bead was likely to do. At high drive the extra beads tended to escape. At lower drive their presence helped hold the next arrival. This supplies a step missing from an appeal to uncertainty alone. A possibility becomes a physical history when resolving it one way changes the conditions for what follows. The laboratory shaker supplied those conditions. An account of the universe has to explain how its own usable differences arose.[4]

A magnet gives a second example. In a simple model, each magnetic moment points up or down, and neighboring moments tend to align. At high temperature, thermal motion disrupts the alignment. Below a critical temperature, a large sample can retain one orientation. The orientation of the whole depends on the individual moments, and each moment is influenced by its neighbors. I call that mutual dependence the *interpenetration of opposites*. The up–down symmetry of the rules survives as two possible orientations of a magnet that has settled into one. That change is my example of the *negation of the negation*. The onset of lasting order as temperature falls through a threshold illustrates *quantity into quality*.[5]

What makes a dialectical account possible, on my view, is that a distinction can acquire a new use in the course of formation. Above the critical temperature, a local moment may point up or down. Below it, a whole sample can retain an orientation that matters to a neighboring piece of iron. Ontological uncertainty extends that reasoning to the distinctions the laws themselves admit. The bead experiment gives a reason to take the intermediate step seriously, though even fully deterministic collisions could make a crowd gather on one side. A magnet can remain ordered at equilibrium. The beads require a shaker. Alan Turing showed mathematically how chemicals that react together and diffuse at different rates can form a spatial pattern. Each system can develop order, for reasons particular to its parts and their interactions.[4][5][6]

## Which side of the mirror?

On December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler drew a box around a set of counts in his notebook in Washington. Chien-Shiung Wu had brought her cobalt experiment to the National Bureau of Standards because its laboratory could cool a crystal to a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. At higher temperatures the nuclear spins pointed every which way. Even if each nucleus emitted most of its electrons in one direction, the detector could count as many coming up as going down. In the cold crystal a magnetic field could align enough spins to make the comparison.[7]

A small crystal about two centimetres above the cobalt gave a flash when an electron struck it. The flashes traveled up a clear rod to be counted outside the cold apparatus. At first the count at that end was high. As the cobalt warmed and the spins lost their alignment, the count fell. Then the researchers reversed the field and repeated the observation with the same detector in the same place. The count began low and rose as the sample warmed. A detector that happened to count too many electrons would have done so both times. The change followed the orientation of the nuclei.[7]

The electrons favored the direction opposite to the spin. Reverse all three directions in space and an electron traveling upward is now traveling downward. The spin axis, which describes rotation, keeps its orientation under that transformation. The reflected decay would therefore favor the direction that the actual sample disfavored. Here was an experiment a physicist could distinguish from its mirror image by looking at the counts. Ralph Hudson later recorded the conclusion in capitals at the top of Ambler’s page.[7]

At Twente either compartment could fill. In Wu’s experiment, reversing the field reversed the direction the electrons favored. The bead apparatus had a symmetry that a particular crowd of beads could lose. The weak interaction responsible for cobalt decay distinguishes the mirror alternatives in the law itself.[4][7]

In 2025 the LHCb collaboration reported a difference between the probabilities of corresponding decays of a baryon and its antimatter counterpart with space reflected. This violation of the symmetry called CP matters to the cosmic question. It also shows what remains unexplained. Creating or annihilating baryons and antibaryons in pairs leaves their numerical excess unchanged. Starting from equal numbers, the usual account of a net excess requires processes that can change baryon number, distinguish matter from antimatter in the requisite ways, and act outside thermal equilibrium. A difference in decay probabilities alone supplies only part of the explanation.[8]

Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow considered whether there might be large separate regions of matter and antimatter. Along their boundaries, particles and antiparticles would meet and annihilate, producing gamma rays. The researchers calculated the expected radiation and compared it with what astronomers observe. Under their assumptions, the region of matter containing our galaxy would have to occupy virtually the entire observable universe.[9]

Why does space have three large dimensions? Why do macroscopic processes have a direction in time? A gas released from confinement will overwhelmingly tend to spread because there are so many more ways for its molecules to occupy the whole vessel. That argument starts with the gas confined. A cosmic history must explain how the early universe came to have such low entropy in the first place. I suspect that both the arrow of time and the number of large dimensions have histories.[10]

My further claim is that disequilibrium is fundamental to creation. Once a difference can sustain a flow, an arrangement that draws on it may persist or multiply if some of the gain goes back into producing that arrangement again. I suspect such competition can favor the greatest sustained rate of entropy production that the circumstances allow. An unequal stock on two sides of a partition gives us no measure of this rate. In the shaker we could trace the electric supply, the energy lost in collisions and the flow across the wall. A cosmic explanation has to identify its own source of usable differences and the histories across which a comparison would make sense.[4][10][11]

## How long a vacuum state can last

The state of a field can affect the properties of other particles. The Higgs field contributes to the masses of the W and Z particles that mediate the weak interaction. The photon remains massless. In 2012 physicists discovered the Higgs boson, a short-lived excitation of that field. The field remains present after a boson decays. Its state helps determine properties that might otherwise seem to belong to each particle alone.[12]

A field can occupy a state stable against small disturbances even when a lower-energy state exists. A barrier separates the two. Physicists call the first state *metastable* and can calculate under specified conditions how a quantum field can tunnel out of it. The lifetime of that state and its energy are distinct quantities. Both enter an account of which physical history other structures will inherit.[13]

Interactions with other fields can carry information about a field’s state into its surroundings. Interference between alternatives then becomes harder to observe by measuring the original field alone. Physicists call this *decoherence*. The interactions can occur entirely within the universe.[14]

Robson Christie and colleagues modeled a field with two possible states during cosmic expansion. The rate at which a barrier developed affected the probability of occupying the higher-energy state. In their model decoherence had little effect on the initial proportions. Later it could substantially reduce the chance of escape from that state. Under certain conditions, repeated interactions that distinguish the alternatives suppress transitions between them. This is the quantum Zeno effect. Thomas Bachlechner also found conditions under which an environment could delay vacuum decay.[13][15]

I propose that decoherence may have helped hold our universe in a metastable state. Measurements still leave uncertainty about the stability of our electroweak vacuum. And in these models the possible states and the barrier between them are specified at the outset. To explain how the physical laws arose would require an account of those features too.[13][15][16]

## Fine-tuning and the cosmological constant

Foucault’s measurement bears on an older result. At a flat boundary between air and water, a light ray takes a path for which the travel time is *stationary*. A small change in where the ray crosses the boundary leaves the time unchanged to first order. This is Fermat’s principle. It relates the bending of light to its speed in each material. The more general stationary-action principle similarly restricts the motion possible between endpoints. Both require established physical relations.[1][17]

The refractive index of water measures how much light of a particular frequency slows as it enters. The response of water’s constituents helps explain the measured number. Here a quantity we can use as a fixed input at one scale can be explained at another. The question is whether we can do something comparable for a fundamental constant.[18]

In a stellar model, changing one constant while holding the others fixed can prevent a star from forming. To turn that sensitivity into odds against our universe, we would have to know which combinations of values could physically arise and how often. Varying each dial independently assumes an answer. If two constants acquired their values in the same physical process, some combinations entered in the calculation might never have been possible.

On the usual picture, a law and its constants are settled and events play out within what they allow. Ontological uncertainty opens up another possibility. The relation between two measured quantities could itself have become definite, much as a state of a field can come to set the mass of a particle. In that case the fine-tuning question changes. We would want to know which values can be reached *together* and what keeps that particular relation in place. An accurate count of combinations that no physical history could produce would tell us nothing about the odds of a star.[12][18]

My positive hypothesis is that measured constants are the enduring features of *autopoietic* physical assemblies. For that to be true, an arrangement of fields must open a route for drawing work from a gradient, the strength of an interaction must depend on the arrangement, and part of the work must restore the arrangement after disturbance. Its own activity would thereby help maintain the value that a physicist measures. At a vent the corresponding return is visible in a cell making its membrane. A field can remain metastable for a long time through the height of a barrier alone. There is a genuine question which of those processes, if either, accounts for a particular constant.[13][19]

I propose that, among arrangements physically accessible to the early universe, those that extract energy at the greatest sustainable rate are most likely to endure and recur. Some of the energy would have to go into reproducing the relations that set the constants. Establishing a maximum rate of entropy production would also require tracing how the work dissipated and comparing the alternatives over the same interval. On this view the formation of a universe is a natural process. We do not yet know its possible histories well enough to calculate the probability of ours.[11][19]

In the autumn of 1997 Adam Riess used distant supernovae to calculate how much matter the universe contained. The fading of each explosion gave his team a way to estimate its intrinsic brightness. From how faint it looked they could estimate its distance. They also had its redshift. With the cosmological constant set to zero, gravity should have slowed the expansion while the light was on its way here. How much matter would account for the distances he measured?[20]

Riess circled the result in his notebook. The density of matter came to minus 0.36 times the critical density, with an uncertainty of 0.18. Ordinary matter could hardly be present in a negative amount. Allowing a positive cosmological constant let the expansion accelerate and put the stars as far away as their faint light implied. Brian Schmidt checked the calculation. He got the same answer. A second team also found evidence of acceleration.[20]

A small positive cosmological constant can fit the acceleration. Particle physics has yet to explain its size. Steven Weinberg asked which values would allow galaxies to form at all. With enough positive vacuum energy, matter gets too widely separated to collect into galaxies like ours. That condition narrows the possible values an observer could measure. It still leaves many.[20]

Peter Graham, David Kaplan and Surjeet Rajendran proposed a mechanism for a different quantity. In their model a slowly moving field changes a parameter of the Higgs field in the early universe. Once the Higgs field acquires a value, barriers grow and can stop the other field. The proposal, called the *relaxion*, requires new physics and particular early conditions. It does specify the interaction that would stop the change. Removing that interaction gives a precise way to test the explanation within the model.[21]

Riess had two kinds of evidence, a brightness and a redshift. A constant chosen to fit that relation tells us how the universe is expanding. Explaining where the constant came from would require deriving its value without putting that same measurement in at the start. The assembly hypothesis incurs a further obligation. Which flow of energy restores the value after a disturbance, and which other measurable relation follows from the same mechanism? If a barrier alone accounts for its persistence, we have an explanation for stability and no evidence here for self-production. I have no calculation that yields the observed cosmological constant. The case for pursuing one is that an outcome of physical history can serve as a fixed condition for everything that forms afterward.[13][19][20]
