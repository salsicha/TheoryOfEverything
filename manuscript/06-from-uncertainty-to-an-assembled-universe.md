# Chapter 6 — From Uncertainty to an Assembled Universe

In 1850 Léon Foucault used a revolving mirror to settle a question about water. Light returning through a tube of water came back later than light traveling the corresponding distance through air. The mirror had time to turn a little farther. Foucault could read the difference from the displacement of an image. It was a measurement of two journeys too brief for anyone in the room to time by hand.[1]

The relative speed mattered because the rival accounts of refraction predicted different winners. The familiar bend in a ray at the water’s surface had been available to both sides for centuries. Measuring which journey took longer gave the bend a new use. A property of water that now appears in tables had once been an open question to which two accounts of light gave opposite answers.[1]

In Cornwall the witnesses to an engine trial could take gravity for granted. Foucault could similarly take the properties of his water as given once he had measured them. Cosmology raises a harder question. Could the physical relations an investigator must put into an account themselves be outcomes of a history? I think the universe is assembled down to some of the quantities we now call fundamental. We first need to establish what it means for something to have become definite.

## What an experiment can settle

In Delft in 2015 Bas Hensen and colleagues compared measurements on electron spins in two diamonds 1.3 kilometres apart. Each station chose which measurement to make late enough that news of the choice could not reach the other station in time at the speed of light. In 245 trials the correlations exceeded the bound for theories in which local pre-existing variables account for the answers under the experiment’s assumptions. Distance and timing gave a philosophical dispute about pre-existing answers an experimental consequence.[1]

That result sharpens the question of what can count as a starting condition. A quantum vacuum already has fields and a set of possible excitations. Calling it absolute nothingness would smuggle those relations into the premise. My philosophical starting point is that absolute nothingness is impossible. It would require even the possibility of a definite structure arising to have been settled, once and for all, in the negative. I take uncertainty to run deeper than that. On this account creation is natural to reality.[2][3]

*Ontological uncertainty* is the claim that a distinction itself can still be taking shape. A laboratory notebook can be missing a reading that was definite all along. Hensen’s result puts pressure on a particular way of extending that ordinary explanation to entangled systems. Other interpretations, including deterministic theories with nonlocal relations, remain possible. My additional claim is that how a system comes together can determine which properties it makes sense to ask for. The arrangement is material and the relation it establishes is Platonic in the sense used throughout this book.[3]

This premise puts an obligation on the idea of assembly. If time itself has a history, an account of what the clock depended on cannot simply use a clock to order every step. The same difficulty will arise for my proposal about a preferred *rate* of energy extraction. A rate requires a physical measure of elapsed time.

## Counting after the shaking stops

At the University of Twente, Ko van der Weele and his colleagues had a way to make six hundred glass beads choose a side. The apparatus consisted of a clear tube divided into two compartments by a low wall. A shaker underneath could keep the beads bouncing high enough to cross the wall. Shake vigorously and you get roughly three hundred beads to a side. Turn the frequency down far enough, keeping the amplitude fixed, and nearly the whole population can collect in one compartment. If you mean to predict which side will fill, it is frustrating to learn that the apparatus was built properly.[4]

Consider the next bead to leave a crowded compartment. Before it reaches the lip it is liable to hit several neighbors. The collisions dissipate some of its energy. The more company the bead has, the less speed it is likely to retain for the jump. On the emptier side a bead has a better chance of getting a clear run. With vigorous shaking the side that has an extra bead generally sends a little more traffic back, and an accidental excess dies away. With weaker shaking an extra bead in the crowd can reduce the outward traffic. Now a small excess helps create the conditions for a larger one. Which side happens to get ahead matters only after the machine has supplied a way for getting ahead to count.[4]

The authors point out a small inconvenience in their measurements. They counted the beads after they turned the shaker off. In the last few bounces some lively beads from the sparse side could still make it over. Beads huddled in the crowded compartment had less prospect of making the return trip. The act of getting a count could make the inequality look a little larger than it had been while the machine ran. It sounds like a tiresome detail until one remembers the people at Fowey Consols trying to count how much water came up on a stroke after the bucket had broken. In either case attention to what the instrument registered can tell us what the final number means.[4]

The laws of the shaker accord left and right equal treatment. Its recent inhabitants may have had a very different experience on the two sides. This is the sort of thing physicists mean by *symmetry breaking*. An observer can have good grounds for predicting an inequality without possessing the slightest reason to give the fuller side a name in advance. Nor does every symmetry have to break. Turn the drive back up and the beads will, given time, disperse. Throughout the demonstration somebody is supplying energy to the shaker. Its power cord is one of the things we should remember to remove before we try using it to explain the universe.[4]

A magnet makes a related point in a different way. In a simple model each microscopic magnetic moment can point up or down and neighboring moments favor agreement. At high temperature, a group that happens to line up one way comes apart with the next bout of thermal agitation. Cool a sufficiently large system and an orientation can persist. A single moment now finds it favorable to align itself with an ordered neighborhood whose order comes from the other moments doing much the same thing. Here is a practical sense for the *interpenetration of opposites*. Part and whole take part in making one another what they are. Up and down once described two available orientations for an individual. In the ordered magnet they also describe the relation between two possible states of a whole sample. A definite direction has displaced the initial indifference while preserving the old symmetry in a new form. That is how I read the *negation of the negation* here. And the slow lowering of a temperature through a threshold supplies a case of *quantity into quality*. In the ideal large system there is an order on one side of the transition that the other side cannot sustain.[5]

I think dialectics works because a categorical opposition can belong to a stage in the history of a thing. Calling it provisional then commits us to finding out what changes it. The magnet orders as it cools and can remain ordered at equilibrium. The glass beads need continuing agitation to sustain their traffic. In a chemical model devised by Alan Turing, even diffusion, a process one might hire to smooth out a difference, can help create a pattern when substances react and spread at different rates. The equations give different reasons for anticipating order in these three cases. If we hope to explain a particular feature of the universe we shall have to be at least as particular about how it came about.[4][5][6]

## Which side of the mirror?

In Ernest Ambler’s laboratory notebook for December 27, 1956, there is a box around an observation made in Washington that day. The rate at which a detector was counting electrons had gone down. This was encouraging. A second run in which the count went *up* was better still. Chien-Shiung Wu and her colleagues were testing whether nature could tell left from right. The nucleus under examination was cobalt-60, which emits an electron when it undergoes beta decay. Cool the sample and apply a magnetic field and one can get many of its nuclei to orient their spins in the same direction. Set a detector looking along that axis. Would electrons emerge more readily one way than the other?[7]

The sample gradually warmed and the spins lost their alignment. In one setting an initially high count at the detector fell toward its ordinary value. Reverse the polarizing field and an initially low count rose toward that value as the sample warmed. A detector that merely liked its own end of the apparatus would have had trouble arranging both performances. The nuclei preferentially emitted electrons opposite to the direction of their spin. Under a spatial inversion, the electron’s direction of travel reverses while the spin, an axis of rotation, keeps its orientation. The mirror version would exhibit the opposite preference. The experiment, published in 1957, showed that the weak interaction itself distinguishes the two. Ralph Hudson later wrote the verdict in capitals at the top of the notebook page. The counts in the middle had come first.[7]

The Twente apparatus produced either a left-heavy or a right-heavy population. Wu and her colleagues found a preference that survived reversing the nuclear orientation and checking their detector. In the bead experiment the observed state lost a symmetry of the apparatus. In cobalt a fundamental interaction itself distinguished the mirror alternatives. The excess of matter over antimatter in the observable universe raises a third question.

In 2025 the LHCb collaboration reported a difference between the probabilities of corresponding decays of a baryon and its antimatter counterpart with space reflected. This violation of the symmetry called CP matters to the cosmic question. It also shows what remains unexplained. Creating or annihilating baryons and antibaryons in pairs leaves their numerical excess unchanged. Starting from equal numbers, the usual account of a net excess requires processes that can change baryon number, distinguish matter from antimatter in the requisite ways, and act outside thermal equilibrium. A difference in decay probabilities alone supplies only part of the explanation.[8]

Andrew Cohen and two colleagues pursued a rival possibility. A universe divided into regions of matter and antimatter would leave evidence at the joins. Matter and antimatter brought into contact at the boundaries between large regions would annihilate and produce gamma rays. Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow calculated how luminous those joins would be under specified conditions. To keep the predicted glow down to the observed level, the matter region containing our galaxy would have to occupy virtually the entire observable universe. Dividing space into compartments was a promising first move with six hundred glass beads. The sky itself can object when we propose it for this purpose.[9]

There remain two asymmetries for which these experiments supply no origin story. Space has three large dimensions. Macroscopic processes have a direction in time. Statistical physics explains why a gas released from confinement is overwhelmingly likely to spread, given the initially prepared state. Its account draws on a difference between a special past and the much larger set of configurations accessible afterward. The low entropy of the early universe is precisely the preparation that a cosmic history must explain. I regard the number of large dimensions and the arrow of time as possible historical outcomes.[10]

My further claim is that disequilibrium is fundamental to creation. Once a difference can sustain a flow, an arrangement that draws on it may persist or multiply if some of the gain goes back into producing that arrangement again. I suspect such competition can favor the greatest sustained rate of entropy production that the circumstances allow. An unequal stock on two sides of a partition gives us no measure of this rate. In the shaker we could trace the electric supply, the energy lost in collisions and the flow across the wall. A cosmic explanation has to identify its own source of usable differences and the histories across which a comparison would make sense.[4][10][11]

## What the vacuum remembers

Modern physics already lets us tell a modest version of the story in which a background helps decide what subsequent things can be. The low-energy state of the Higgs field matters to the masses of the W and Z particles that mediate the weak interaction. The photon remains massless. Physicists managed to excite the field sufficiently to discover a Higgs boson in 2012. When that brief excitation has decayed, the state of the field still matters to the next particle that comes along. The mass one might have put down as a property of an individual particle turns out to depend on its relation to something present throughout space.[12]

A field can occupy a state stable against small disturbances even when a lower-energy state exists. A barrier separates the two. Physicists call the first state *metastable* and can calculate under specified conditions how a quantum field can tunnel out of it. The lifetime of that state and its energy are distinct quantities. Both enter an account of which physical history other structures will inherit.[13]

When a field interacts with other fields, information about which alternative is occupied can spread into the surroundings. The possibility of observing interference between the alternatives by examining the original field alone can dwindle drastically. That is *decoherence*. The surroundings may belong entirely to the universe under discussion. No spectator with a notebook, still less a spectator outside the universe, is required.[14]

In a model studied by Robson Christie and colleagues, a barrier becomes important during cosmic expansion. How quickly this happens helps determine how much of the field’s probability ends up in the higher of the two resting states. The authors compared what happened with and without the influence of other fields. Decoherence made little difference to those initial proportions. Later it could make an enormous difference to the chances of tunneling out. In a regime known as the quantum Zeno effect, interactions that repeatedly distinguish the alternatives can inhibit a transition between them. In another model Thomas Bachlechner had also found conditions under which the environment delays vacuum decay. It is possible to give a physical account of how an early choice could endure for people who arrived too late to see it made.[13][15]

This gives substance to my conjecture that decoherence helped hold our universe in a metastable state. Whether our vacuum has that history remains open. Even the stability of the electroweak vacuum depends on measured quantities whose uncertainties matter to the result. There is a further difficulty that no improvement in a measurement will by itself clear up. The theorists put the two hollows and the ridge into the model. The calculation can tell us how the field acquired and retained a setting. Could the shape of the two hollows also have a physical history?[13][15][16]

## Fine-tuning and the cosmological constant

Foucault’s water tube brings an old mathematical idea back into the argument. At a flat interface the ordinary path of a light ray makes its travel time stationary. Moving the crossing point a little leaves the time unchanged to first order. This is Fermat’s principle. It relates the bending of the ray to the different speeds Foucault measured. The more general stationary-action principle imposes an equally exact constraint on motion between specified endpoints. Such principles narrow the space of paths with extraordinary economy. They presuppose definite physical relations.[1][17]

For light of a given frequency, the refractive index of water can be supplied as a measured number. At a finer scale the response of the water’s constituents helps account for that number. Explaining a bulk property from its constituents gives a concrete precedent for treating an apparent input as an outcome. It provides no derivation of a fundamental constant, but it changes what would count as an argument that a number has to be accepted as given.[18]

An observed sensitivity and an estimated probability answer different questions. Changing one number while holding the others fixed can destroy a stellar structure in a calculation. If several of the numbers owe their values to the same physical history, only some of the variations entered into that calculation may ever have been accessible. Estimating a probability would require their joint distribution. A catalogue of settings at which stars fail supplies no such distribution.

That is my objection to the inference from fine-tuning to cosmic improbability. Each stage of assembly can alter the constraints on what follows. The formal space in which an investigator can vary the constants separately can be much larger than the space reachable by a material history. Ontological uncertainty makes even the relations among the quantities candidates for such an account.

My positive hypothesis is that measured constants are the enduring features of *autopoietic* physical assemblies. For that to be true, an arrangement of fields must open a route for drawing work from a gradient, the strength of an interaction must depend on the arrangement, and part of the work must restore the arrangement after disturbance. Its own activity would thereby help maintain the value that a physicist measures. At a vent the corresponding return is visible in a cell making its membrane. A field can remain metastable for a long time through the height of a barrier alone. There is a genuine question which of those processes, if either, accounts for a particular constant.[13][19]

Why these particular values? I propose that combinations which maximize sustainable energy extraction, among those that the earlier history made physically accessible, are the ones most likely to endure and recur. A burst that consumes the arrangements that made it possible has won the contest for one instant. An assembly capable of paying the cost of its own repair can continue drawing on a gradient across many such instants. Whether this also maximizes entropy production requires an account of the work, its eventual dissipation, the time interval, and the rival histories open to the system. The emergent universe seems to me a natural outcome of successive constraints and renewals. A probability for this universe would require knowledge of its possible histories that nobody possesses.[11][19]

In the autumn of 1997 Adam Riess wrote a disturbing result in his laboratory notebook. He had used distant exploding stars to ask how much the gravity of matter had slowed the expansion of the universe. On the page of his notebook where the density of matter should have appeared, the answer carried a minus sign. He had assumed that matter alone governed the expansion. To keep that assumption he would have to make ordinary matter push galaxies apart. Reintroducing a cosmological constant into the equations let him keep ordinary matter and take seriously what the observations appeared to say. Expansion had been speeding up. Two groups obtained evidence for that acceleration. A positive cosmological constant is one physical account of it, equivalent in the gravitational equations to a uniform energy density of the vacuum. It leaves the spectacular difficulty of accounting for the small net value we infer compared with characteristic particle-physics scales.[20]

Steven Weinberg asked what would happen to the formation of galaxies if the positive vacuum energy were too large. A person making observations from a galaxy could already rule out some of the numbers on a theorist’s dial. My proposal undertakes a different debt. For the cosmological constant I would have to identify the gradient, exhibit the return that keeps an interaction in operation, and derive the observed value across a range of starting conditions. A mechanism that needs its inputs tuned to just the observed precision would have moved the original problem to an earlier page. Ideally the same history would leave another measurable trace and tell us where to look for it. If the alleged return could be switched off in the calculation and the number still came out the same, I would have learned something awkward about my explanation. I can describe what a successful account would have to calculate. The value of the cosmological constant is still beyond it.[20]

There is a useful comparison for a different physical scale. Peter Graham, David Kaplan and Surjeet Rajendran proposed a slowly moving field that changes a parameter of the Higgs field in the early universe. Once the Higgs field takes on a value, barriers grow that can arrest the other field’s motion. A quantity that would ordinarily be supplied at the beginning now has a hand in stopping its own adjustment. Their proposed *relaxion* needs new physics and particular early conditions. We can at least say which terms in the theory to remove if we want to see the stopping mechanism fail. To turn the idea of assembled constants into physics, one would like to give a critic of this book an equally specific thing to try.[21]

Riess’s minus sign was worth preserving because a later reader could recover the assumption it challenged. An assembled-law account owes physics a comparable piece of trouble, an observable difference between a world that inherited a relation and one in which the relation always had to hold. In the history of our own species, relations sometimes change within the life of an observer. The anthropologist Richard Lee learned how little the purchase of a Christmas ox in the Kalahari entitled him to decide about the feast.
