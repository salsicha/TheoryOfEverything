# Chapter 6 — From Uncertainty to an Assembled Universe

Put a coin at the bottom of an empty bowl. Lower your head until the rim just hides it. Keep still and have someone pour in water. The coin comes back into view. It lies exactly where you put it.[1]

Suppose you had entered the room a minute later. You could measure the depth of the water and the angle from the rim to your eye. You could give an excellent account of how the light reached you, down to the bend it took at the surface. From any such calculation you could leave out the person holding the jug. Yet a minute ago the light from the coin could never have reached you by that route. An event you missed has become a condition that every ray leaving the water has to obey.

The engineers in Cornwall could leave a great deal out of their calculations on similar grounds. They needed to know how much water an engine raised and what it cost them in coal. They could take gravity for granted. Now suppose you are asking where gravity came from. Where do you stand to make *that* calculation? Which things may you still take for granted? I suspect that the universe itself was assembled, down to some of the relations we call its laws and constants. The question I want to follow is how an outcome could become a rule.

## What was there to begin with?

Empty the bowl. You still have the bowl. Take that away and you can picture the empty patch on the table where it stood. It is extraordinarily hard to think of absolute nothingness. The imagination keeps smuggling back a place where something could turn up, or a stretch of time before it did. Physics has a remarkably fruitful account of empty space. A quantum field in its vacuum state still has properties. To write down that state a physicist needs a theory telling her which states the field can occupy.[2]

Here I will put my own commitment on the table. I think absolute nothingness is impossible. It would require an absolutely settled fact about existence, the absence even of the possibility that anything could become definite. I take indeterminacy to belong to reality itself. On that premise there could never have been a completed state of nothingness waiting for a first event. Creation belongs to what reality does. This is a philosophical starting point. The difficulty of picturing nothing could, after all, tell us quite a bit about a human imagination.[2]

There are several familiar ways to be uncertain about a coin. Cover one with your hand and I may have to guess which face is up. I can also know the odds in a well-specified game without knowing its next result. In both cases the sides, the object that bears them and the rules of the game are settled. *Ontological uncertainty* goes deeper. The very distinctions in which an outcome will be described can still be taking shape. Matter acquires a definite arrangement and with it a definite set of relations. These are the material and Platonic aspects of the same event. The uncertainty relations of quantum mechanics give precise limits on the spreads of particular measurable quantities. Physicists who agree on those limits can disagree about the underlying reality. The further claim here is mine.[3]

One consequence is that apparently exhaustive opposites can acquire a history. A wave and a particle once seemed to divide the possibilities quite well. Chapter 4 followed what happened when light made demands on both descriptions. I take dialectics seriously because the categories with which we begin a history can themselves undergo change in it. That claim takes on physical content when we can find the quantities that change and see a new relation take hold. We can begin with a machine considerably smaller than a Cornish engine.

## Three hundred on either side

In a laboratory at the University of Twente, Ko van der Weele and his colleagues put six hundred glass beads in a clear tube. A low wall split the tube in two. The whole apparatus sat on a shaker. Beads could jump from either side to the other. With vigorous shaking, the populations hovered around equality. Reduce the shaking and a difference between the two sides could grow until nearly all the beads were on one side. Either side would do.[4]

It sounds like a trick you could catch by watching the first bead cross. Watch the beads it joins instead. They collide with one another, giving up a little of the energy of motion in every collision. In a sizable crowd these encounters come more often. Beads on the crowded side can lose so much speed that few clear the wall. The sparse, lively population on the other side can go on sending reinforcements. A small excess has begun to make the conditions for its own increase.[4]

The wall has the same height for every bead. Suppose there are three hundred and one on the left. With strong shaking the extra bead tends to increase departures from the left, undoing the advantage. Under weaker shaking the same addition can *reduce* the traffic out. Eventually the average traffic in each direction can balance again, with one compartment nearly full and the other nearly empty. A person counting crossings would report balance. A person counting beads would report a striking inequality. Both could watch for a long time and both could be right.[4]

The distinction gives symmetry breaking much of its power. Someone who knows the laws of this apparatus can have an excellent reason to anticipate a crowded side and no reason whatever to write *left* on the prediction. A rule that treats two outcomes alike can produce a settled arrangement that distinguishes them. A fluctuation gets the chance to grow only when the dynamics support its growth. Turn up the shaker and, after enough crossings, the evidence for which side filled first will be gone.[4]

Consider a simple model of a magnet in which each tiny magnetic moment can point up or down. Neighbors favor agreement. At high temperature agitation keeps spoiling the agreement. Cool a large enough system and a collective orientation can hold. Now any given moment tends to line up with a neighborhood whose orientation was produced by just such moments. Here is what *interpenetration of opposites* can mean in practice. The individual helps constitute the whole that constrains the individual. The old up-down symmetry seems to disappear when the magnet points up. It returns in a new role, as the precise relation between two possible ordered states. This recovery of a relation in a changed form is what I understand by *negation of the negation*. As cooling crosses a threshold, a change of quantity makes a new quality of order possible. In an ideal magnet the temperature, the interaction and the order can all be specified. The terms from dialectics earn their keep by drawing one's eye to those changes.[5]

Alan Turing found a still different way a small difference could grow. In a model of chemicals reacting and spreading at different rates, spreading itself could destabilize a uniform mixture and help a pattern emerge. The magnet, the shaker and Turing's chemicals give us good reasons to ask how an apparent uniformity became differentiated. They also give us three different answers. With the universe we have to find out which question the evidence is actually asking.[6]

## What the mirror would show

In 1957 Chien-Shiung Wu and her collaborators reported what happened when they cooled a sample of radioactive cobalt and aligned the spins of its nuclei. The electrons emitted as the nuclei decayed preferred one direction along the axis of spin. An exact spatial reflection of the experiment would reverse that preference relative to the spin. The two versions could be told apart by counting. In some weak interactions, nature distinguishes left from right.[7]

Imagine putting a mirror beside the glass beads. If the left compartment is full, the mirror shows an arrangement the apparatus could equally well have produced. Wu's mirror shows a difference in the behavior of the interaction itself. And there is a third puzzle you can carry into either laboratory in the palm of your hand. Why is the coin made of matter in a universe where the observable stock of ordinary matter so greatly exceeds the corresponding antimatter?

Make a baryon and an antibaryon as a pair and the difference between their numbers stays just as it was. Annihilate a pair and the difference stays put again. Moving them to different compartments can change where to look. It does nothing to this total. In 2025 the LHCb collaboration showed that a particular baryon and its antimatter counterpart have different probabilities for corresponding decays after spatial reflection. Physicists call this violation of the combined symmetry *CP*. It establishes a real preference in a transition. To produce the cosmic surplus from an initially balanced stock takes more. In the standard account of baryogenesis the processes must be capable of changing net baryon number, must distinguish matter from antimatter in specified ways, and must operate away from thermal equilibrium. Here disequilibrium has a definite job. In equilibrium the compensating processes can undo the growth of an excess.[8]

Perhaps the antimatter is simply farther away. That suggestion comes with a trace we can look for. Where large domains of matter and antimatter meet, annihilation would produce radiation. Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow calculated how much diffuse gamma radiation a patchwork of such domains could leave. Under the conditions they examined, keeping that light faint enough required the matter region we inhabit to occupy virtually the observable universe. Looking for the boundaries tells us something a local inventory never could.[9]

There are other inheritances so intimate that we scarcely register them as conditions. We move through three large spatial dimensions. Nothing about the two compartments tells us why there are three. We also remember the water entering the bowl. We have no memory of the moment tomorrow when it will be emptied. To explain a direction in time, compare what happens when gas confined to one half of a vessel is let into the other. There are enormously many ways its molecules can occupy both halves and extraordinarily few in which they all return to the first. This gives a good statistical account of what happens *once somebody has put the gas on one side*. The history of that preparation still has to be accounted for. At the scale of the universe the low-entropy past is precisely the preparation no one can stand outside and perform.[10]

I suspect that these features have histories and that disequilibrium runs deeper than any particular engine or star. The stronger proposal is that as gradients form, routes capable of drawing down more of them can endure and multiply, in some circumstances driving the rate of entropy production toward a maximum. A magnet can sit in an asymmetric state at equilibrium for a very long time. In the bead experiment the shaker kept doing work on the beads. You could follow its power cord to the wall. The cost of applying the book's proposal to the whole universe is that we must account for the equivalent of the plug. We need the source of the difference, the routes actually available and a reason one route persists. If the proposal reaches as far back as the origin of time, it must even say what establishes the order of events. An imagined clock ticking in an empty room is another bowl we have forgotten to take away.[10][11]

## What the vacuum can keep

There is a place in modern physics where the history of a background could matter to nearly everything that follows. In the electroweak theory the low-energy state of the Higgs field affects how other fields behave. The W and Z bosons, which mediate weak interactions, have mass. The photon remains massless. The discovery of a Higgs boson in 2012 gave physicists a way to investigate the field by exciting it. Even after the excitation is gone the background matters. A property that one might have put on a list of attributes of individual particles turns out to depend on their relation to something that pervades space.[12]

Could a particular background have *stuck*? Picture a marble in a hollow with a ridge between it and a deeper hollow. It has a perfectly good place to rest, though there is a lower place it could rest. Physicists call the corresponding kind of state *metastable*. For a field, the two hollows stand for different values the field can take, and the ridge describes the energy cost of changing between them. In quantum theory there can be a chance of tunneling through such a barrier. Now it matters both how the field got into the shallower hollow and what subsequently kept it there.[13]

This is where quantum decoherence enters the story. When a system interacts with its surroundings, those surroundings can carry away information that distinguishes alternatives. Interference between the alternatives becomes hard to observe in the system alone. For a field in the early universe, other fields can play the part of the surroundings. No observer has to be waiting beside the apparatus. Nor need the universe have an outside.[14]

In a model investigated by Robson Christie and colleagues, an expanding universe contains a field with two possible resting places. The speed with which the barrier becomes important affects the chance that the field occupies the shallower one. The researchers found little effect of decoherence on those *initial proportions*. Later, under the conditions they modeled, interactions with other fields could strongly suppress further tunneling. In this quantum Zeno effect the surroundings continually acquire information about which alternative is occupied, inhibiting a transition. Thomas Bachlechner had found a related possibility for a different model of vacuum decay. A history can have one physical explanation for where it goes and another for why it stays there.[13][15]

That is the most concrete form I know how to give my conjecture that decoherence helped retain an assembled universe. Something that happened to a field could govern what is possible for its later inhabitants. Whether our own vacuum was retained in this way is open. Its stability depends on properties of particles and interactions, including some whose values have yet to be determined with the requisite precision.[16]

A field held in a hollow can preserve a condition for other assemblies. A cell renewing its membrane does something more. The cell puts part of what crosses the membrane to work making the means of another crossing. A claim of *autopoiesis* on the scale of physical origins would need a comparable account of what produces what, drawing on which difference. A background might last through the entire history of life without once having done that. What makes the vacuum example so promising is also what makes it an incomplete answer. Its authors give the field two hollows to begin with. What shaped the hollows in the first place?

## Fine-tuning and the two dials

Look at the coin again. Light travels more slowly in water than in air. For the ordinary crossing in the bowl, the bend at the surface gives the quickest route between the coin and your eye. A small displacement of the crossing point trades time in one medium for time in the other. At the ray's actual crossing point those small gains and losses cancel to first order. This is Fermat's principle in a form you can see from your chair. Physics has a wider family of such principles. In classical mechanics a trajectory makes a quantity called the *action* stationary under small variations with fixed endpoints. Stationarity can also occur somewhere other than a minimum. None of this requires a ray to try routes until one works. The formal relation and the material behavior give us two ways into the same calculation.[1][17]

The shape of the water surface constrains which route can reach you. Its depth has a history. So does the water as a material with a particular response to light. Much of physics consists of finding the conditions under which a property reliably appears at one scale and explaining it through relations among constituents at another. A change of scale in a calculation need entail no change over time in a law of nature. It does show how much physical work can lie behind what looks, from the appropriate distance, like a single constant.[18]

Consider two dials on a machine. It works only when the two needles agree to a hair's breadth. Draw all possible pairs of readings on graph paper and the machine works on a thin diagonal strip. What extraordinary luck that it runs at all. Now take the cover off. Both needles turn on the same shaft. They still have to agree to a hair's breadth. You can still spoil the machine by pulling one needle off its mount. What is gone is the reason to suppose its maker had two independent settings to get right.

Fine-tuning arguments do real work when they show what would happen to stars or atoms if a parameter had another value. To read the thinness of the strip as the probability of a universe like ours, though, we would need to know which combinations the universe could reach and how often it would reach them. Even finding a shaft behind the dials would leave a good question. Why did the needles come to rest *here*, at this point along the strip?

My answer to fine-tuning is that the fundamental constants are the measurable features of an autopoietic assembly. In a world whose relations were still becoming definite, a particular arrangement of fields could have opened a route for extracting work from an energy gradient. Suppose the strength of an interaction depended on that arrangement. If some of the work done through the interaction restored the arrangement whenever it was disturbed, the interaction would help maintain its own strength. A physicist arriving much later could measure that strength to exquisite precision and call it a constant. Part of what the measurement recorded would be a history of work.

Why these values in particular? An arrangement that can draw enough work to rebuild after a disturbance may survive a shock that would erase another. I propose that, among the combinations of values physically reachable under the same conditions, the ones that endure together are those that maximize the sustainable rate of energy extraction. A route that releases its energy in a single burst can win the first instant and leave nothing to sustain a second. The claim concerns how much work a route can keep drawing from a gradient while renewing the conditions that make the work possible. Whether the most productive route actually prevails is a physical question. The word *autopoiesis* tells us where to look for the return flow. It cannot tell us which way the needles will point.[11][19]

The cosmological constant sets a hard problem. In the late 1990s two teams used distant exploding stars to infer that cosmic expansion had accelerated. A positive cosmological constant can produce such acceleration. In gravitational equations it acts like a uniform energy density of empty space. Several contributions can enter the physical accounting for that density. Why should their total yield such a small value on particle-physics scales, and what would keep it small as the contributions change? Steven Weinberg showed how a sufficiently large positive value could prevent the formation of the gravitationally bound structures an observer like us needs. That gives one reason we could measure a value in a certain range. To explain our value by assembly, I would have to identify a gradient, show how work drawn from it held the relevant relation in place, and derive the small value we infer. I know of no such calculation.[20]

There are specific proposals for how a setting could come about through history. In 2015 Peter Graham, David Kaplan and Surjeet Rajendran described one for a different quantity, the electroweak scale. In their proposal a slowly evolving field changes a parameter of the Higgs field. Once the Higgs field takes on a value, barriers to the first field's motion grow and can bring it to rest. The physical quantity being set helps bring the setting process to a stop. This proposed mechanism, called the *relaxion*, needs new physics and particular conditions in the early universe. Its virtue here is that you can point to the linkage, ask what it would take to move it and see whether the mechanism has had to borrow all the precision it was supposed to explain.[21]

I think the emergence of a universe can be a natural process for much the same reason that the emergence of life at a vent could be. The next step occurs in a world altered by the last one. A mineral wall narrows the routes a molecule can take while making new chemistry possible. An ordered field can supply a background that later particles encounter everywhere. An organism can acquire the means to repair its own wall. A list of the finished parts can make the settings look more independent than they ever were. What is inherited includes relations a person taking inventory might never see. Symmetry breaking can settle a choice, stationary principles can describe the resulting paths, and self-maintaining activity can keep a route open that a one-time arrangement would lose. The inference to assembled laws is a further one. It would earn its place in physics if one could let a quantity previously supplied to a model develop, recover its measured value across a range of starting conditions, and predict another trace of how it got there.[4][12][17][19][21]

Let someone take water out of the bowl a spoonful at a time. At a certain depth the coin slips out of sight again. You can do this all afternoon. You can see a condition for the light's passage being made and unmade. With the vacuum we came late, and we have no such freedom. The way back is to find something in the fields we can measure today that would have turned out differently if their history had been different.

The next inheritance is close enough to watch being made. Picture a child in a group that has just brought home food. Long before the child was born, people here had a way of deciding who gets a share. By tomorrow, what the adults do tonight will have become part of what the child expects the world to do.
