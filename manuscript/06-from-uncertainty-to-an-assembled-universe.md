# Chapter 6 — From Uncertainty to an Assembled Universe

In 1850 Léon Foucault used a revolving mirror to settle a question about water. Light returning through a tube of water came back later than light traveling the corresponding distance through air. The mirror had time to turn a little farther. Foucault could read the difference from the displacement of an image. It was a measurement of two journeys too brief for anyone in the room to time by hand.[1]

Two theories of light made opposite predictions. One required light to travel faster in water than in air. The other required it to slow down. Both explained why light bends when it enters water. Foucault’s measurement distinguished them.[1]

Foucault measured a property of water. Physicists can now explain much of that property from the behavior of its molecules. Could some of the numbers we call fundamental also have physical origins? I think the universe and its laws probably formed through successive stages. The first question is what it means for a physical property to become definite.

## What an experiment can settle

In Delft in 2015, Bas Hensen and colleagues measured electron spins in two diamonds 1.3 kilometres apart. Each station chose its measurement too late for a light signal carrying that choice to reach the other in time. In 245 trials the two stations obtained results more strongly correlated than a broad class of theories allowed. Those theories treated each answer as determined locally in advance, under the assumptions of the test.[1]

A quantum vacuum already has fields and laws that describe how those fields can be excited. To speak of absolute nothingness is to make a stronger claim. Even the possibility of anything coming into existence would have to be ruled out. My philosophical premise is that such certainty is impossible. I take indeterminacy to be basic to reality. On that premise, physical structure can arise naturally.[2][3]

I use *ontological uncertainty* for indeterminacy in reality itself. A missing observation can leave us uncertain about a value that was definite all along. My claim is that a property may become definite only as a physical system forms. The Delft result restricts which explanations can assign answers in advance. Deterministic theories with nonlocal relations remain possible. The broader view of creation I propose is a philosophical interpretation.[3]

If time also emerged, a theory would have to explain how earlier conditions led to later ones without assuming a clock already existed. For the same reason, a claim about a *rate* of energy extraction applies only where elapsed time has a physical meaning.

## Counting after the shaking stops

At the University of Twente, Ko van der Weele and colleagues put six hundred glass beads in a clear tube. A low wall divided it into two equal compartments. A shaker made the beads bounce over the wall. At high frequency, about half the beads stayed on each side. The researchers lowered the frequency while keeping the amplitude fixed. Nearly all the beads could now collect in one compartment. Either side could fill.[4]

A bead loses energy when it collides with another bead. In the crowded compartment it is more likely to collide several times before reaching the wall, leaving it too little speed to get over. With vigorous shaking, an accidental surplus on one side usually sends more beads back. With weaker shaking, an initial surplus can reduce the number crossing out. More beads enter than leave, and a small imbalance grows.[4]

The team had to stop the shaker to count the beads. A few from the less crowded side could still bounce over before coming to rest. The final count could therefore exaggerate the imbalance while the shaker was running. The researchers reported that limitation with their measurements.[4]

The apparatus treated both sides equally, but an individual run could finish with almost all the beads on one side. Physicists call a change of this kind *symmetry breaking*. It was possible to predict the imbalance without predicting which side would fill. Increasing the drive could disperse the beads again. The experiment also required a continuing supply of energy from the shaker. An explanation for the universe would have to account for its own energy differences.[4]

A magnet gives a second example. In a simple model, each magnetic moment points up or down, and neighboring moments tend to align. At high temperature, thermal motion disrupts the alignment. Below a critical temperature, a large sample can retain one orientation. The orientation of the whole depends on the individual moments, and each moment is influenced by its neighbors. I call that mutual dependence the *interpenetration of opposites*. The up–down symmetry of the rules survives as two possible orientations of a magnet that has settled into one. That change is my example of the *negation of the negation*. The onset of lasting order as temperature falls through a threshold illustrates *quantity into quality*.[5]

I think dialectics works because distinctions such as up and down can acquire different physical meanings as a system forms. The actual mechanism matters. A magnet can remain ordered at equilibrium. The beads require a shaker. Alan Turing showed mathematically how chemicals that react together and diffuse at different rates can form a spatial pattern. Each system can develop order, for reasons particular to its parts and their interactions.[4][5][6]

## Which side of the mirror?

On December 27, 1956, Ernest Ambler drew a box around a measurement in his laboratory notebook in Washington. He and Chien-Shiung Wu’s team were counting electrons emitted by cobalt-60 nuclei as they decayed. They cooled the sample and applied a magnetic field to align many of the nuclear spins. With the detector at one end they could test whether electrons were more likely to come out in one direction.[7]

As the sample warmed, the spins lost their alignment. The initially high count fell. When the researchers reversed the field, the count started low and rose as the sample warmed. Reversing the field had changed the direction of the spins relative to the same detector. The nuclei preferentially emitted electrons opposite to the direction of their spin. A mirror reflection reverses the electron’s direction of travel while preserving the spin orientation. The reflected process would have the opposite preference. The team had shown that the weak interaction distinguishes the two. Ralph Hudson later added the conclusion in capital letters at the top of Ambler’s notebook page.[7]

The Twente apparatus produced either a left-heavy or a right-heavy population. Wu and her colleagues found a preference that survived reversing the nuclear orientation and checking their detector. In the bead experiment the observed state lost a symmetry of the apparatus. In cobalt a fundamental interaction itself distinguished the mirror alternatives. The excess of matter over antimatter in the observable universe raises a third question.

In 2025 the LHCb collaboration reported a difference between the probabilities of corresponding decays of a baryon and its antimatter counterpart with space reflected. This violation of the symmetry called CP matters to the cosmic question. It also shows what remains unexplained. Creating or annihilating baryons and antibaryons in pairs leaves their numerical excess unchanged. Starting from equal numbers, the usual account of a net excess requires processes that can change baryon number, distinguish matter from antimatter in the requisite ways, and act outside thermal equilibrium. A difference in decay probabilities alone supplies only part of the explanation.[8]

Andrew Cohen, Álvaro De Rújula and Sheldon Glashow considered whether there might be large separate regions of matter and antimatter. Along their boundaries, particles and antiparticles would meet and annihilate, producing gamma rays. The researchers calculated the expected radiation and compared it with what astronomers observe. Under their assumptions, the region of matter containing our galaxy would have to occupy virtually the entire observable universe.[9]

There remain two asymmetries for which these experiments supply no origin story. Space has three large dimensions. Macroscopic processes have a direction in time. Statistical physics explains why a gas released from confinement is overwhelmingly likely to spread, given the initially prepared state. Its account draws on a difference between a special past and the much larger set of configurations accessible afterward. The low entropy of the early universe is precisely the preparation that a cosmic history must explain. I regard the number of large dimensions and the arrow of time as possible historical outcomes.[10]

My further claim is that disequilibrium is fundamental to creation. Once a difference can sustain a flow, an arrangement that draws on it may persist or multiply if some of the gain goes back into producing that arrangement again. I suspect such competition can favor the greatest sustained rate of entropy production that the circumstances allow. An unequal stock on two sides of a partition gives us no measure of this rate. In the shaker we could trace the electric supply, the energy lost in collisions and the flow across the wall. A cosmic explanation has to identify its own source of usable differences and the histories across which a comparison would make sense.[4][10][11]

## How long a vacuum state can last

The state of a field can affect the properties of other particles. The Higgs field contributes to the masses of the W and Z particles that mediate the weak interaction. The photon remains massless. In 2012 physicists discovered the Higgs boson, a short-lived excitation of that field. The field remains present after a boson decays. Its state helps determine properties that might otherwise seem to belong to each particle alone.[12]

A field can occupy a state stable against small disturbances even when a lower-energy state exists. A barrier separates the two. Physicists call the first state *metastable* and can calculate under specified conditions how a quantum field can tunnel out of it. The lifetime of that state and its energy are distinct quantities. Both enter an account of which physical history other structures will inherit.[13]

Interactions with other fields can carry information about a field’s state into its surroundings. Interference between alternatives then becomes harder to observe by measuring the original field alone. Physicists call this *decoherence*. The interactions can occur entirely within the universe.[14]

Robson Christie and colleagues modeled a field with two possible states during cosmic expansion. The rate at which a barrier developed affected the probability of occupying the higher-energy state. In their model decoherence had little effect on the initial proportions. Later it could substantially reduce the chance of escape from that state. Under certain conditions, repeated interactions that distinguish the alternatives suppress transitions between them. This is the quantum Zeno effect. Thomas Bachlechner also found conditions under which an environment could delay vacuum decay.[13][15]

I propose that decoherence may have helped hold our universe in a metastable state. Measurements still leave uncertainty about the stability of our electroweak vacuum. And in these models the possible states and the barrier between them are specified at the outset. To explain how the physical laws arose would require an account of those features too.[13][15][16]

## Fine-tuning and the cosmological constant

Foucault’s measurement bears on an older result. At a flat boundary between air and water, a light ray takes a path for which the travel time is *stationary*. A small change in where the ray crosses the boundary leaves the time unchanged to first order. This is Fermat’s principle. It relates the bending of light to its speed in each material. The more general stationary-action principle similarly restricts the motion possible between endpoints. Both require established physical relations.[1][17]

The refractive index of water measures how much light of a particular frequency slows as it enters. The response of water’s constituents helps explain the measured number. Here a quantity we can use as a fixed input at one scale can be explained at another. The question is whether we can do something comparable for a fundamental constant.[18]

An observed sensitivity and an estimated probability answer different questions. Changing one number while holding the others fixed can destroy a stellar structure in a calculation. If several of the numbers owe their values to the same physical history, only some of the variations entered into that calculation may ever have been accessible. Estimating a probability would require their joint distribution. A catalogue of settings at which stars fail supplies no such distribution.

This is my objection to the argument that fine-tuning makes our universe improbable. Earlier physical changes can restrict the values available later. Treating each constant as independently adjustable can produce lists of universes that no physical process could ever create. On the premise of ontological uncertainty, even the relations among constants could have formed over time.

My positive hypothesis is that measured constants are the enduring features of *autopoietic* physical assemblies. For that to be true, an arrangement of fields must open a route for drawing work from a gradient, the strength of an interaction must depend on the arrangement, and part of the work must restore the arrangement after disturbance. Its own activity would thereby help maintain the value that a physicist measures. At a vent the corresponding return is visible in a cell making its membrane. A field can remain metastable for a long time through the height of a barrier alone. There is a genuine question which of those processes, if either, accounts for a particular constant.[13][19]

I propose that, among arrangements physically accessible to the early universe, those that extract energy at the greatest sustainable rate are most likely to endure and recur. Some of the energy would have to go into reproducing the relations that set the constants. Establishing a maximum rate of entropy production would also require tracing how the work dissipated and comparing the alternatives over the same interval. On this view the formation of a universe is a natural process. We do not yet know its possible histories well enough to calculate the probability of ours.[11][19]

In the autumn of 1997 Adam Riess used measurements of distant exploding stars to estimate how much the gravity of matter had slowed the expansion of the universe. He recorded a negative density of matter in his notebook. The calculation assumed matter alone governed the expansion. Ordinary matter could not have that negative density. With a cosmological constant in the equations, the observations admitted another explanation. The expansion was accelerating. Two groups found evidence for acceleration. A positive cosmological constant can account for it as a uniform vacuum energy density. The inferred value is small compared with characteristic particle-physics scales, and physicists still lack an explanation for its size.[20]

Steven Weinberg showed that sufficiently large positive vacuum energy would prevent galaxies from forming. The fact that we observe the universe from a galaxy therefore restricts the values we can measure. My proposal would have to go further. It must identify a physical process that produces the observed value across a range of starting conditions. It must show how work drawn from a gradient sustains that process. If the calculation still predicted the same value with that return removed, the proposed mechanism would be unnecessary. I have no derivation of the cosmological constant.[20]

Peter Graham, David Kaplan and Surjeet Rajendran proposed a mechanism for a different quantity. In their model a slowly moving field changes a parameter of the Higgs field in the early universe. Once the Higgs field acquires a value, barriers grow and can stop the other field. The proposal, called the *relaxion*, requires new physics and particular early conditions. It does specify the interaction that would stop the change. Removing that interaction gives a precise way to test the explanation within the model.[21]

Riess’s notebook shows what a theory of assembled laws needs to explain. An observed number ruled out a prediction under a stated assumption. For my hypothesis to become a physical theory, there must be some measurement that can distinguish a relation assembled over time from one that has always held.
