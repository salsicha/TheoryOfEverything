# Chapter 4 — The Discovery That Became Possible

**Part II · The Physical Argument**

Albert Einstein began his June 1905 paper with a magnet and a wire. Move the magnet toward a conducting loop and a current flows. Move the loop toward the magnet at the same relative speed and the current is the same. The textbooks offered a different explanation for each observation.[1]

When the magnet moved, an electric field arose around it. When the wire moved, its charges experienced a force as they passed through the magnetic field. Einstein wanted one description that would work for either observer. He proposed that observers in uniform motion relative to each other would find the same laws of physics and measure the same speed of light in a vacuum. To apply both principles he had to explain how observers compared their clocks.[1]

The editors of *Annalen der Physik* received Einstein’s paper on June 30. Twenty-five days earlier Henri Poincaré had presented a note to the French Academy of Sciences. He started with the motion of the Earth and considered, among other things, the shape of a moving electron. Yet both men arrived at equations with the same peculiar feature. To calculate when a moving observer would say an event took place, they had to specify *where* it happened too.[1][2]

The coincidence would be extraordinary if the two men had been choosing at random among all the things one could say about clocks. They were in a position to reject most of them without trying them. My claim is that such convergence becomes likely when physical work leaves behind a result that different lines of reasoning must respect. On the first page of his note, Poincaré named one such result. It came from a laboratory in Cleveland.[2]

## A result that could travel by post

In 1887, in Cleveland, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley floated a slab of stone on mercury. Physicists generally took light to be a wave in a medium called the ether. If Earth moved through that medium, light might take longer to travel along one wall of the laboratory than another. The trough of mercury let the experimenters turn the apparatus while they watched what happened to the light.[3]

On the slab, a partly reflecting glass divided a beam of light. Some went straight ahead and some off at a right angle. Mirrors sent both beams back to meet. Crests arriving together made a bright stripe in the eyepiece. Where a crest met a trough there was a dark one. A quarter turn of the slab exchanged the directions of the two paths. If Earth’s motion delayed one beam, a stripe should slide past the crosswire by about four tenths of the distance to the next stripe.[3]

They marked sixteen positions around the trough. At first they stopped the stone at each mark. The strain of stopping it kept the stripe moving for half a minute or more. Wait for it to settle and the temperature changed enough to move it again. They began taking readings on the move. At each mark an observer read the stripe and the stone received another gentle push. One turn took about six minutes.[3]

The stripe scarcely shifted. The report thanked the Bache Fund for the money to obtain the result. In Paris eighteen years later, Poincaré could cite it in a few words. Any theory that predicted a large displacement under those conditions now had to account for the one the men in Cleveland had measured.[2][3]

Poincaré named Michelson and then Hendrik Lorentz. Lorentz had already calculated how moving bodies could change dimensions by just enough to account for the optical measurements. He had also written equations relating electrical and optical measurements made in different states of motion. Poincaré began with those equations.[2]

One number in Lorentz’s equations was still undetermined. It allowed the overall scale of measurements to change between frames. Poincaré required a direct conversion between two frames to give the same result as going by way of a third. Converting back had to restore the original measurements. Taken together with rotations, these transformations had to form what mathematicians call a *group*. The requirement fixed the scale factor at one and ruled out some proposed shapes for a moving electron.[2]

## Whose seven o’clock?

Einstein asked what it meant to say a train arrived at seven. The train pulls alongside the station clock as its hands point to seven. Give the next station a clock and the railway has another problem. How did anyone set the two clocks to the same time? The light by which someone at the first station sees the second clock has already taken time to reach him.[1]

In Einstein’s procedure a light signal leaves clock A, reaches B and is reflected straight back. The person at A can time the round trip with one clock. At the instant of reflection, B should read halfway between the departure and return times at A. Two people can synchronize their clocks by that rule.[1]

Now consider those same two clocks from a frame in which they are moving along the line joining them, with B ahead. While the light is traveling out, B moves away from the point where the flash was sent. On the return leg A moves toward the light. At the same speed of light, these are unequal journeys. The instant of reflection that the people traveling with the clocks call halfway through the trip is no longer halfway through it for the observer watching them pass. This is how the location of a clock entered an equation for the time. Both observers could account for every flash. Each could show how the other arrived at a different judgment about simultaneity.[1]

Poincaré had discussed clocks too. In a 1904 lecture in St. Louis he described moving observers setting their watches by exchanging light signals. He called the result their local time and distinguished it from the time in a frame at rest in the ether. Einstein took the observers’ own clocks as sufficient. They agreed on how to convert many measurements and still differed on what time meant.[1][4]

Poincaré acknowledged the optical measurements and Lorentz’s equations. Einstein thanked Michele Besso for his suggestions. There is no need to supply Einstein with Poincaré’s reading list to explain the agreement. The two arguments each show where the freedom to choose ran out.[1][2][3][5]

Poincaré could keep an ether at rest and still had to make the conversion back from a moving frame undo the conversion out. Einstein let observers set their own clocks and still had to account for the two legs of a light signal’s journey. Either line of reasoning put a term for place into the reckoning of time. Here is a claim about what was ripe for discovery in 1905. Another physicist who accepted these requirements had good prospects of reaching the same relation. Whether that physicist would have abandoned the ether was much harder to predict. The disagreement between the June papers helps locate the constraint more precisely than agreement alone could.[1][2][5]

## A plate struck by light

In 1909 Einstein considered a different problem. A small reflecting plate is free to move in a cavity filled with gas and radiation. Light exerts pressure on the plate. Einstein calculated how the impulses from the light would fluctuate. His answer contained two terms. One had the form expected from interfering waves. The other had the form expected from separate packets of energy. Both were necessary to describe the same plate.[6]

Akira Tonomura and his colleagues filmed the arrival of electrons sent through a microscope one at a time. Inside, a fine charged wire deflected the paths on either side so that the two parts of the electron wave could overlap. One dot appeared on the detector, then another some distance away. After about twenty minutes, crowded rows of dots stood beside nearly empty rows. The wire and detector had stayed in place throughout.[7]

An electron struck the detector at one place. Any account in which part of it struck here and the rest landed down the row had to explain why the detector always registered one whole event. But an account that sent little independent pellets to either side of a wire also had a problem. Adding the probabilities for those two routes would miss the nearly empty rows. In quantum mechanics it is the wave amplitudes for the two routes that are added. Where they reinforce, a detection is more likely. Where they cancel, it is rare. The answer is a prediction about where the next whole dot might turn up. It becomes visible as a pattern only because the team kept the apparatus running.[7]

The puzzle gives the three dialectical principles quite specific meanings. Every mark in the interference pattern was an individual detection. That is the *interpenetration of opposites*. Physicists gave up the claim that a particle simply followed one classical path. They recovered the localized event, with a rule for its probability that required both paths. That is the *negation of the negation*. The change from *quantity into quality* took place in the record. Twenty minutes of arrivals made the interference visible.[7][8]

Relativity and the quantum account of an electron answer different questions. What joins them is the work a successor theory had to do. It had to preserve the current when the magnet and wire exchanged roles, or the whole dot at the detector when both paths contributed to its probability. Simply choosing one side of the old distinction lost a result people could obtain again. Keeping both results forced a change in what the alternatives meant. That pressure can lead two people toward the same relation and can lead one person to revise the categories of a problem. In this sense convergence and the negation of the negation are consequences of the same constraint.[1][2][7][8]

## The first fifteen days

What does energy add to an explanation in terms of shared mathematics? In Cleveland the work went into making a measurement precise enough that a predicted shift would have been plainly visible. The length of the paths, the mercury bearing and the observers’ decision to read the moving stone made a particular prediction vulnerable. Once published, the result could constrain a calculation in Paris without anyone there buying a slab of stone. The physical cost of getting that result was incurred once. Each investigator who used it could spend effort on the questions it left open. This is a mechanism by which work can concentrate a search. My further claim is that inquiry tends to follow such paths when what people learn lets them put energy to new uses and sustain further inquiry. The grant and the papers establish the cost and the reuse of a measurement. They supply no comparison of entropy production across the problems the grant might have supported. What we *can* follow is the relation between place and time into a machine whose builders had to act on it.[2][3][5]

On June 23, 1977, a tracking station in Panama picked up the first signal from a navigation satellite called NTS-2. The spacecraft carried two cesium frequency standards, but for fifteen days the engineers ran its clock on quartz to save power. After the solar panels were deployed they could switch on a cesium standard at full power. Six days of measurements showed the orbiting clock gaining about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day relative to a clock on the ground. The higher orbit made the clock run faster. Its speed made it run slower. The net gain was close to the prediction from general and special relativity. The engineers adjusted its frequency.[9]

By 1977 a relation over which physicists had disagreed had become a constraint on what an engineer could do with a clock. Both the reuse and its direction in time matter. Poincaré could reject an equation inconsistent with the optical measurement. The satellite team could use relativity to recognize why an orbiting clock gained on a ground clock and how much of that gain to allow for. The same relation that narrowed an investigator’s choices enlarged an engineer’s.[1][2][9]
