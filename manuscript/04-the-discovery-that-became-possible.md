# Chapter 4 — The Discovery That Became Possible

**Part II · The Physical Argument**

Albert Einstein began his June 1905 paper with a magnet and a wire. Move the magnet toward a conducting loop and a current flows. Move the loop toward the magnet at the same relative speed and the current is the same. The textbooks offered a different explanation for each observation.[1]

When the magnet moved, an electric field arose around it. When the wire moved, its charges experienced a force as they passed through the magnetic field. Einstein wanted one description that would work for either observer. He proposed that observers in uniform motion relative to each other would find the same laws of physics and measure the same speed of light in a vacuum. To apply both principles he had to explain how observers compared their clocks.[1]

The editors of *Annalen der Physik* received Einstein’s paper on June 30. Twenty-five days earlier Henri Poincaré had presented a note to the French Academy of Sciences. He started with the motion of the Earth and considered, among other things, the shape of a moving electron. Yet both men arrived at equations with the same peculiar feature. To calculate when a moving observer would say an event took place, they had to specify *where* it happened too.[1][2]

Why did two physicists reach that result in the same month? Poincaré named one of his reasons on the first page. Eighteen years earlier two experimenters in Cleveland had tried to measure the motion of the Earth.

## A result that could travel by post

In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley reported an attempt to measure the Earth’s motion through the supposed medium that carried light waves, the ether. A laboratory on the Earth traveled with everything its occupants could ordinarily use as a reference. Light appeared to offer a way round the difficulty. If the Earth were moving through the ether, a beam sent along the direction of travel should take a different time to go out and back from one sent at right angles to it. The difference was small enough that the investigators had to find a way to measure it in fractions of a wavelength.[3]

They split a light beam in two and sent its parts down arms set at right angles. Mirrors reflected the beams back. Where the returning waves reinforced each other they made a bright stripe. Where they canceled there was a dark one. Turning the apparatus by a quarter turn exchanged the directions of the arms. The expected difference in travel time would shift the pattern by about four tenths of the distance between stripes.[3]

Michelson and Morley mounted the mirrors on a slab of stone carried by a wooden float in a trough of mercury. They marked sixteen positions around the trough. At first they tried stopping at each mark to read the stripes. The strain of stopping the slab affected the readings for half a minute or more. If they waited longer, the temperature changed. They kept the slab moving. As each mark passed, they read a stripe and gave the stone a gentle push. One revolution took about six minutes.[3]

The stripes moved far less than the predicted four tenths. Using mirrors to send the beams back and forth had lengthened each path, so a shift of the predicted size should have been visible. When the researchers published their measurements, they thanked the Bache Fund for helping pay for the work. A physicist in Paris could now use the result without building another instrument.[2][3]

Poincaré named Michelson and then Hendrik Lorentz. Lorentz had already calculated how moving bodies could change dimensions by just enough to account for the optical measurements. He had also written equations relating electrical and optical measurements made in different states of motion. Poincaré began with those equations.[2]

One number in Lorentz’s equations was still undetermined. It allowed the overall scale of measurements to change between frames. Poincaré required a direct conversion between two frames to give the same result as going by way of a third. Converting back had to restore the original measurements. Taken together with rotations, these transformations had to form what mathematicians call a *group*. The requirement fixed the scale factor at one and ruled out some proposed shapes for a moving electron.[2]

## Whose seven o’clock?

Einstein gave the example of a train arriving at seven. A passenger standing beside a station clock can see the arrival and the clock reading together. Recording the time of an event at a second station requires another clock. How would the two stations agree on the time when a signal between them takes time to travel?[1]

His published procedure sent a light signal from one clock to another and back. The first clock supplied the times of departure and return. The second was synchronized when its reading at reflection fell halfway between them. An observer moving uniformly relative to the first system could carry out the same operation with clocks at rest in her own frame.[1]

The consequential step came when he translated between the two systems. The time assigned to a remote event depended on its position as well as its time in the first system. Events simultaneous by the first set of clocks could occur at different times by the second. Each set had been synchronized by the same rule. Einstein had traced a disagreement about simultaneity to operations on which both observers could agree.[1]

Poincaré had discussed clocks too. In a 1904 lecture in St. Louis he described moving observers setting their watches by exchanging light signals. He called the result their local time and distinguished it from the time in a frame at rest in the ether. Einstein took the observers’ own clocks as sufficient. They agreed on how to convert many measurements and still differed on what time meant.[1][4]

The published papers let us trace some of what the two men knew. Poincaré acknowledged the American optical measurements and Lorentz’s equations. Einstein thanked Michele Besso for his suggestions. A measurement or a mathematical result could reach someone in another country who had never worked with its author.[1][2][3][5]

Poincaré’s note reached the French Academy twenty-five days before Einstein’s paper reached the German journal. That sequence is evidence about how much of the result had become accessible along another route. Lorentz had supplied a transformation. The optical results had eliminated a class of predictions. Poincaré required consistency between successive changes of frame. Einstein made clock synchronization answer to light propagation. Their agreement on the relation between place and time gives me grounds for thinking its discovery had become highly likely. Their continuing disagreement about the ether shows how much room for interpretation remained.[1][2][5]

## A plate struck by light

In 1909 Einstein considered a different problem. A small reflecting plate is free to move in a cavity filled with gas and radiation. Light exerts pressure on the plate. Einstein calculated how the impulses from the light would fluctuate. His answer contained two terms. One had the form expected from interfering waves. The other had the form expected from separate packets of energy. Both were necessary to describe the same plate.[6]

Many years later, Akira Tonomura and colleagues recorded what happened when they sent electrons through an instrument one at a time. A fine charged wire allowed two parts of each electron wave to overlap. The first arrivals appeared on the detector as scattered spots. As thousands more accumulated, bright and dark bands appeared. Every electron had arrived at one location. Together their locations formed an interference pattern.[7]

The instrument sets two requirements for a theory of the electron. It must explain why the detector records a whole electron at one spot. It must also predict how changing the paths changes the distribution of those spots. Quantum theory assigns probabilities to individual detections using wave amplitudes from both paths. Their interference explains the bands.[7]

I use three terms from dialectics to describe the result. The wave pattern consists entirely of individual detections, an example of the *interpenetration of opposites*. In the *negation of the negation*, a theory recovers something an earlier explanation appeared to rule out. The classical particle picture gave way to a wave account, and quantum theory recovered the localized event while retaining interference. Finally, enough detections reveal a pattern that a handful cannot resolve. This is the change from *quantity into quality* in the observation.[7][8]

The two June papers and the electron film make the same demand of a successor. A theory of the electron has to recover both the individual detections and their distribution. A theory of motion had to recover the laboratory results and explain why one observer’s equations worked for another. An established opposition ceased to organize the evidence, and the particular evidence that defeated it constrained the replacement. That connection between dialectical change and a narrowing field of discovery is the book’s argument. Its proposed foundation, that definiteness can itself have a history, goes beyond what these experiments establish.[2][3][7][8]

## The first fifteen days

Michelson and Morley needed stone, mercury, glass and money. They also needed the mirrors arranged so the light traveled far enough for a measurable shift. The same slab could carry mirrors set for a shorter path and give a less sensitive measurement. This is the distinction I call material and *Platonic*. The material properties of the instrument and the relations among its parts jointly determined what the observers could measure.[3]

On June 23, 1977, a tracking station in Panama picked up the first signal from a navigation satellite called NTS-2. The spacecraft carried two cesium frequency standards, but for fifteen days the engineers ran its clock on quartz to save power. After the solar panels were deployed they could switch on a cesium standard at full power. Six days of measurements showed the orbiting clock gaining about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day relative to a clock on the ground. The higher orbit made the clock run faster. Its speed made it run slower. The net gain was close to the prediction from general and special relativity. The engineers adjusted its frequency.[9]

I propose that disequilibrium influences discovery through the work a society can support. People used some of their available energy to make an instrument, run an experiment and publish a result. Eighteen years later that result helped Poincaré rule out possible answers. Later still, engineers used relativity to correct satellite clocks. These are documented steps. To establish my stronger claim, that such discoveries favor the greatest sustainable rate of entropy production, we would have to trace their physical costs and returns and compare them with the other uses of energy available at the time.[3][5][9]

In 1834 William West faced a problem that could be decided in a day. Other engineers doubted how much water his engine in Cornwall could raise on a bushel of coal. Witnesses came to the mine to measure it.
