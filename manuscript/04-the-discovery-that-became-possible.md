# Chapter 4 — The Discovery That Became Possible

**Part II · The Physical Argument**

In one of the papers of June 1905, a train arrives at seven. Albert Einstein asks us to look at his watch. The small hand points to seven just as the train comes in, and for the length of one sentence there seems to be very little wrong with our understanding of time. Then he asks how to tell the time of something that happened somewhere else.[1]

On June 5 a short note by Henri Poincaré went before the French Academy of Sciences. *Annalen der Physik* would receive Einstein’s paper on June 30. Poincaré was considering, among other things, what shape to give a moving electron. He had more than one model that agreed with the observations he cited. To choose among them, he asked what would happen if you changed your point of view twice.[2]

A watch beside a railway track and an argument about an electron’s shape are curious places to set out from if you intend to meet. Yet put their equations side by side and the resemblance survives even the change of notation. Each man had a way of translating positions and times between observers in uniform relative motion. Each found that to calculate the time one observer would assign to a distant event, you needed to know where the event occurred. Where had that extraordinary requirement come from? And how had the world contrived to put it in front of two people in the same month?

## The stone that had to keep turning

Poincaré leaves a name near the top of his note. Albert Michelson had tried to detect the motion of the Earth through the ether, the medium in which light waves were supposed to travel. A breeze tells you which way you are cycling on a still day. If the Earth was moving through a stationary ether, perhaps light traveling across a laboratory could tell the occupants which way the entire laboratory was going. In 1887 Michelson and Edward Morley published an account of what it had taken to ask the question.[2][3]

They divided a beam of light and sent its two parts on journeys at right angles. Mirrors brought them together again. Where the waves reinforced one another there was a bright band, where they canceled a dark one. If the ether made one journey take a little longer than the other, turning the whole instrument ought to make the bands shift. The effect expected from the Earth’s orbital motion amounted to less than half the distance from one band to the next.[3]

Try to build a machine on which the failure of a stripe to move that far would mean anything. An earlier apparatus had been sensitive to vibration. Michelson and Morley put the optics on a slab of stone that floated, with its wooden support, on mercury. They folded the light paths back and forth, buying distance with mirrors. They tried stopping the slab to read the instrument. The strain from stopping could linger for half a minute, long enough for changes of temperature to begin introducing an error of their own. They got more consistent readings when they let the great stone keep turning.[3]

The bands shifted a little, far less than the calculation predicted. At the foot of the first page there is a small acknowledgment of support from the Bache Fund. Put the acknowledgment beside the result. Somebody had to pay for an arrangement delicate enough to tell one kind of silence from another. Afterward a physicist could open a journal and inherit a difficulty that had taken two men, a rotating stone and a good deal of patience to produce. The difficulty turns up on Poincaré’s first page. He wondered whether the stubborn failure to establish absolute motion might itself express a law of nature.[2][3]

This is one place to put a finger on the book’s distinction between material and Platonic. You could weigh the slab. You could also double the distance traveled by the light without doubling the length of the slab, if you arranged for another reflection. The longer journey gave the supposed ether wind more time to make one beam lag behind the other. A bigger lag would show up as a larger fraction of a stripe. Once the apparatus could distinguish the expected displacement from its own disturbances, it had become capable of putting an idea in jeopardy. That new power belonged to stone, light and glass arranged in a particular relation.

## How far apart are two clocks?

Poincaré’s next move can be tried without a slab of stone. Suppose you have a rule for translating measurements made at a station into the measurements someone aboard a passing train would make. The person on the train can apply the same kind of rule to translate into the frame of a second train. A physicist on the platform should be able to get to the second train directly and give the same account. A round trip back to the original platform had better leave its clock and ruler just as you found them. Poincaré required the transformations, together with rotations, to form what mathematicians call a *group*. Starting from Hendrik Lorentz’s work, he used that demand to fix a scale factor that might otherwise have taken different values. A choice about the moving electron’s shape that had survived the available measurements failed this test.[2]

Einstein gives us a job further along the track. Set a second clock there. If we simply look back toward the first clock and set ours to the time we see, the first one will have run ahead while its light was on the way. Let a pulse leave the first clock, reflect at the second, and return to the first. We can mark both its departure and its return on a single dial. Einstein defines the clocks to be synchronized when the second clock reads the midpoint of those two times at the instant of reflection. A concept that seemed settled enough to run a railway now has an instruction attached. It tells someone else how to do the job.[1]

That instruction has a remarkable consequence. Set a row of clocks along the track and another aboard a long train. Let each crew do the job properly while the train moves at constant speed. As each of two distant trackside clocks strikes seven, let it punch a hole in a slip of paper. The track crew enters the two punches together in its log. The train crew, using its own synchronized clocks and allowing for the travel time of the light, assigns them different times. The crews can agree on what the dial beside Einstein read when the train arrived. They can even calculate exactly how their other readings will differ. What they give up is a single present extending from that dial to every place along the line.[1]

There is a snag in the tidy story where Poincaré had the equations and Einstein had the clocks. Poincaré had clocks too. In a lecture the year before, he had described two observers exchanging light signals to set their watches. He interpreted their readings as the local time available to people who shared a motion they could not detect. Einstein built his theory on the equal standing of inertial frames and the constancy of light speed in vacuum. He could let each crew’s clocks have their say without putting a master clock behind the scenes.[1][4]

In the freezer in the last chapter, each sample belonged to a particular line of descent. A change that helped the descendants of one population live on citrate could stay in that population. There were eleven others for which the food might as well have been behind glass. Now try to label Poincaré’s note in the same way. Whose line of descent would you put on the label? In his opening paragraphs a French mathematician considers an observation made with an American instrument and the explanation a Dutch physicist had proposed for it. The next reader could pick up all three lines of inquiry in the morning post.[2][3][5]

That difference gives us a way to ask what was likely to be discovered. Take Einstein’s paper off the shelf. Poincaré’s still gives us a rule for converting the space and time measurements of one observer into those of another. Now take away Lorentz’s earlier work. Look at the place in Poincaré’s argument where he fixes the scale factor. He can do that in a few lines because someone has already brought him to the point where a single factor remains to be fixed. Once those steps could travel, two people could have a much better chance of meeting at the same equation than a count of all imaginable theories would suggest. The two men could have agreed on every figure a traveler would read on the moving clocks. Ask which crew had the real time and there was still plenty left to argue about.[1][2][4]

Carquillat’s punched cards let another weaver recover the curtain in the silk portrait without choosing afresh every thread the light would seem to pass through. Drawings of the mirrors, set down in print, saved the next physicist work of the same kind. They put something else in the hands of a critic, too. Someone who doubted Michelson and Morley could use the very instructions by which the result had been obtained to see whether it was wrong.[3] A whole school can follow a bad idea with magnificent unanimity. Convergence acquires its force when we can point to what the investigators had to answer to beyond one another. Poincaré could name the missing shift. Einstein could take us to the tracks and ask us to set the clocks.

## A dot at a time

There is another puzzle about Einstein that first drew me to this one. How can the same world require us to think of light as a wave and as something delivered in packets? The question belongs to quantum physics. Four years after the relativity paper Einstein set a small, perfectly reflecting plate loose in an imagined cavity filled with gas and thermal radiation. He asked how much the radiation pressure on the plate would fluctuate. His calculation gave him two terms. One had the form expected from interfering waves. The other behaved as if separate packets of energy were striking the plate. He wanted a theory that could account for both contributions in the same calculation.[6]

To feel the difficulty with matter, too, look at a sequence of pictures published by Akira Tonomura and his colleagues in 1989. They sent electrons through an instrument in which two parts of an electron wave could overlap. The beam was so faint that electrons passed through one at a time. Each arrival registered at one place on the detector. At first you could have taken the scattered spots for so many raindrops on a paving stone. As the arrivals accumulated, bands appeared. Go back to the early picture. Which of those little dots looked as if it knew where a band would be?[7]

The bands consist entirely of dots. Any story about particles that merely land wherever their separate classical paths lead must now account for the bands. Any story about an ordinary wave spread over the whole detector must account for the fact that each arrival is registered locally. Saying that matter is both particle and wave is a shorthand for a much more exacting demand. The next theory has to give the distribution of the spots, down to the places where hardly any appear, while letting each arrival make its own single mark.

That is what I mean here by the *negation of the negation*. Rejecting a classical wave as the whole story might send us back to a little flying pellet. The bands refuse to let us go back. A successor has to keep what made each earlier picture persuasive while changing what we mean by both. The dialectical reading is mine. Einstein’s reflecting plate came from a problem in radiation physics, and the later experiment with electrons belonged to the people who built it.[6][7][8]

The other two dialectical principles earn their names in the same pictures. The supposed opposites interpenetrate in a precise sense. The wave-like pattern is a relation among the local arrivals that at first seemed to belong exclusively to the particle picture. And quantity changes into quality for the observer at the screen. Run the instrument long enough and the accumulated counts let someone distinguish a band of high probability from a chance cluster of spots. The electron has undergone no change on account of how long you have watched. What has changed is what the apparatus and its record together make knowable.[7][8]

My wager is that dialectics works because the world can outrun the oppositions we bring to it. Chapter 6 takes this down to the question of *ontological uncertainty*, whether reality itself can acquire a definiteness that was once absent. The pictures on the screen give us a reason to entertain that idea and a reason for care. A thousand detections can expose the poverty of our original choice. They cannot tell us, by counting alone, whether nature had already settled every individual outcome.

Seen that way, the two puzzles about discovery belong together. The very observation that makes an old choice untenable gives people working separately something exact to agree about. They may discard different assumptions and still have to recover those same bands. They may disagree about an ether and still face the same rules for converting measurements. Our freedom to redraw the categories is accompanied by an extraordinary lack of freedom about what the drawing has to fit.

## The clock that had to wait

On June 23, 1977, a navigation satellite carrying cesium clocks went into orbit. For its first fifteen days the spacecraft used a less demanding quartz mode. The cesium standard could be brought into full operation once the solar panels had deployed. There are seventy-two years between the imagined clocks on Einstein’s railway and this one. Through all that time the equations had been available to anyone with a copy of the paper. Out in orbit, the better clock still had to wait for electricity.[9]

When it was running, a station in Panama compared the signal with a clock on the ground. The orbiting clock ran fast by about thirty-eight millionths of a second per day. At that height gravity speeds an orbiting clock relative to a clock on Earth’s surface, while the satellite’s motion slows it. The predicted net gain, which draws on general as well as special relativity, was close to what the station saw. The team then applied a frequency adjustment on the spacecraft. Something that had once been a question about the meaning of readings on separate clocks had become a setting on a piece of equipment.[9]

I think the ultimate force directing this history is the tendency of systems out of equilibrium to build pathways for drawing more work from the differences available to them. Institutions that put some of the gain into keeping people, skills and instruments at work can afford to investigate things whose uses lie well beyond the next payday. This is the chapter’s stake in the conjecture about maximum entropy production. Shared equations tell us a great deal about why the two June papers meet. The harder claim concerns how a world sustained by energy flows comes to build the means for making and keeping such equations at all.

We can trace some of the journey. A grant helped turn the failure of a fringe to move into a result another physicist could use. Much later an organization could put relativity to work keeping time between an orbiting clock and Panama. Its engineers had a new means of comparing clocks on Earth, available in turn to the people who would build the next system. What the Panama station measured was a frequency. Whether the new capability gave back enough usable energy to help sustain the institutions that built it is a question about what people did with it afterward. A society with energy to spare can maintain an observatory or close one. To account for June 1905 we have to follow the decisions and the resources through years when nobody knew there would be a satellite.

A much shorter interval separates the effort of an invention from its return at the mouth of a coal mine. Someone has built a machine that burns coal to lift water. The seam from which it might draw its fuel lies under that water. Stand at the furnace before the first firing and try to hand the engineer a shovelful of the coal the engine will make available. Where will you get it?
