# Chapter 4 — The Discovery That Became Possible

**Part II · The Physical Argument**

Albert Einstein’s 1905 paper opens with an embarrassment for electrodynamics. A magnet and a conducting circuit move toward one another. For the same relative motion they give the same current. Yet the usual account assigned the effect to one cause when the magnet moved and another when the conductor moved. The observable current contained no record of which object the physicist had moved.[1]

In one account an electric field arose around the moving magnet. In the other the charges in a moving wire felt a force from passing through the magnetic field. Einstein took the sameness of the current seriously. He proposed that the laws have the same standing for observers in uniform motion relative to one another, and that each measures the same speed for light in empty space. He had scarcely begun when he had to stop and explain what he meant by the time on a clock.[1]

The editors of *Annalen der Physik* received the paper on June 30. Twenty-five days earlier the French Academy of Sciences had heard a note from Henri Poincaré. His opening problem concerned the motion of the Earth. Among the questions he went on to consider was what shape a moving electron could have. Lay the two papers side by side and an extraordinary agreement emerges. Both give a rule by which observers moving with respect to one another can translate their measurements. To find the time one observer assigns to an event, each rule requires a piece of information that a clock could never supply on its own. You must also say *where* the event took place.[1][2]

It is customary to ask which man deserves credit. I would like to ask how the world came to be in a position to produce even one of them. Had you set out to build a machine for discovering relativity, would you have thought to order a trough of mercury and a large square of stone?

## A result that could travel by post

In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley reported an attempt to measure the Earth’s motion through the supposed medium that carried light waves, the ether. A laboratory on the Earth traveled with everything its occupants could ordinarily use as a reference. Light appeared to offer a way round the difficulty. If the Earth were moving through the ether, a beam sent along the direction of travel should take a different time to go out and back from one sent at right angles to it. The difference was small enough that the investigators had to find a way to measure it in fractions of a wavelength.[3]

In their instrument a beam of light split in two. One part traveled along one arm of the apparatus and back, the other along an arm at right angles to it. When the beams reunited they made stripes, bright where the waves reinforced one another and dark where they canceled. In the ether picture a light beam making the round trip along the direction of the Earth’s motion should take a slightly different time from one crossing it. Turn the apparatus through a quarter circle and the arms exchange jobs. The difference in travel time ought to show up in a shift of the stripes. The expected shift amounted to about four tenths of the space from one stripe to the next.[3]

How do you turn a measurement that delicate? Michelson and Morley set their mirrors on a slab of stone carried on a wooden float in a trough of mercury. They marked sixteen positions around the trough. You might think the sensible procedure would be to turn the stone to a mark, stop, take a reading, and proceed to the next. They tried it. Stopping introduced strains that could still be seen in the readings half a minute later. Waiting for the strain to die away gave the temperature time to change. So they kept the stone going, about one revolution in six minutes. As each mark passed, they read the position of a stripe and gave the stone a gentle push.[3]

The stripes moved a little, far less than the predicted four tenths. A person who merely announced a failure to see the Earth move might have failed to build a good instrument. These observers had folded their light paths back and forth with mirrors until the shift they expected lay well within reach. They could tell a reader what a successful observation would have looked like and how far short they had fallen. A footnote thanked the Bache Fund for help with the cost. What had the fund bought? For its money, a physicist in another country could know how far a stripe ought to have moved on a stone in Cleveland. Nearly eighteen years later Poincaré opened his note with the trouble this had caused.[2][3]

On the first page of his June note Poincaré named Michelson and then Hendrik Lorentz. The Dutch physicist had worked out how the dimensions of moving bodies might change in just such a way that even an instrument made of the best stone and glass would fail to disclose its motion through the ether. He had also given equations relating electrical and optical measurements in different states of motion. Anyone who had read Lorentz could begin the day’s work with those equations on the desk. Much of what we now call the discovery of relativity was already there, with somebody else’s name on it.[2]

Poincaré found a freedom left over in Lorentz’s equations. A change of frame could apparently alter the overall scale by an extra factor. He required successive changes of frame to be consistent. Going from the first frame to a third directly had to give the same measurements as going by way of a second. A transformation followed by its inverse had to restore the original measurements. The requirement that the transformations, including rotations, form what mathematicians call a *group* fixed the extra factor at one. Some otherwise plausible accounts of how a moving electron deforms had just lost their claim on Poincaré’s time.[2]

## Whose seven o’clock?

Einstein introduced a train arriving at seven to pin down what his argument required of clocks. A train beside a station clock presents an ordinary coincidence of events. Establishing what time an event at a second station took place is already a physical operation. Light takes time to carry the news. Putting a reading on a distant event therefore requires a procedure for comparing clocks.[1]

His published procedure sent a light signal from one clock to another and back. The first clock supplied the times of departure and return. The second was synchronized when its reading at reflection fell halfway between them. An observer moving uniformly relative to the first system could carry out the same operation with clocks at rest in her own frame.[1]

The consequential step came when he translated between the two systems. The time assigned to a remote event depended on its position as well as its time in the first system. Events simultaneous by the first set of clocks could occur at different times by the second. Each set had been synchronized by the same rule. Einstein had traced a disagreement about simultaneity to operations on which both observers could agree.[1]

It would make a pleasing division of labor to leave the mathematics to Poincaré and give the clocks to Einstein. Unfortunately for that arrangement, Poincaré had already given a lecture in St. Louis in 1904 about two moving observers who set their watches by exchanging light signals. He understood how they could arrive at what he called their local time. There remained in his account a distinction between that time and the time of a frame at rest in the ether. Einstein could do the whole job with clocks belonging to the observers themselves. The physicists had converged on rules for reading instruments and had some distance left between them on the question of what the readings meant.[1][4]

In the laboratory freezer in the last chapter, a label could identify a bacterial population and tell you from which earlier inhabitants it had descended. What would a label say on Poincaré’s note? Some of its ancestry was French, some Dutch, some American. The men who turned the stone could transmit an achievement to someone who had never visited their laboratory. The same copy of a journal could bring the reader an observation, an explanation of it, and the means of working out where the explanation might fail. Even Einstein, in a paper remarkably sparing in its references, thanked his friend Michele Besso for his suggestions. The encounters that leave enough of a record for us to trace must be a small sample of all those on which a discovery can draw.[1][2][3][5]

Poincaré’s note reached the French Academy twenty-five days before Einstein’s paper reached the German journal. That sequence is evidence about how much of the result had become accessible along another route. Lorentz had supplied a transformation. The optical results had eliminated a class of predictions. Poincaré required consistency between successive changes of frame. Einstein made clock synchronization answer to light propagation. Their agreement on the relation between place and time gives me grounds for thinking its discovery had become highly likely. Their continuing disagreement about the ether shows how much room for interpretation remained.[1][2][5]

## A plate struck by light

In 1909 Einstein found himself with a problem on which he would have liked two respectable explanations to disagree a little less. He imagined a small reflecting plate free to move in a cavity containing gas and radiation. Light exerts pressure. How much should the impulses from the radiation fluctuate? Working from the law for thermal radiation, he found two contributions. One made sense if waves interfered. The other had just the form one would expect if energy arrived in separate packets. Both appeared in the arithmetic for the *same plate*. This was a problem about radiation. His June paper on relative motion had raised a different one.[6]

For an idea of how uncomfortable the choice between a wave and a particle could become, look at a film made much later. Akira Tonomura and his colleagues used a fine charged wire to bring two parts of an electron wave into overlap. Electrons passed through their instrument singly. A detector registered an arrival as a spot. Pause the film early and ask where the next electron will land. A spot is a reassuringly local affair. Now let the film run and try drawing the bright and dark stripes that emerge as thousands of spots accumulate. Every stripe on the screen is made of the very arrivals that appeared, one by one, to give no suggestion of stripes.[7]

Part of what a classical particle had promised survives. The detector at one place registers a whole arrival. Something of the wave survives too. Change how the two paths come together and you change where the arrivals are likely to register. A little pellet following an ordinary classical path would require an explanation for the dark bands. A classical wave spread over the detector would require an explanation for the solitary spot. A successor theory has to deliver a probability for a *local* event by taking account of alternatives whose amplitudes can interfere. That is a much smaller freedom than permission to declare matter anything we please.[7]

I think this is where dialectics earns its place in a book about discovery. What Engels called the *interpenetration of opposites* is visible in the fact that the wave pattern consists entirely of particle detections. The *negation of the negation* describes a curious return. The wave undermined the sufficiency of a classical particle. The detector gives us back a local event whose probabilities have acquired a wave’s dependence on both paths. And there is a modest, observable passage of *quantity into quality*. An observer who has waited for enough counts can distinguish a band from a lucky cluster. The time spent waiting changes what the accumulated record can tell us about the electron.[7][8]

The two June papers and the electron film make the same demand of a successor. A theory of the electron has to recover both the individual detections and their distribution. A theory of motion had to recover the laboratory results and explain why one observer’s equations worked for another. An established opposition ceased to organize the evidence, and the particular evidence that defeated it constrained the replacement. That connection between dialectical change and a narrowing field of discovery is the book’s argument. Its proposed foundation, that definiteness can itself have a history, goes beyond what these experiments establish.[2][3][7][8]

## The first fifteen days

A copy of an intricate design is cheap compared with the trouble of first producing it. In Lyon, a chain of punched cards could carry the shadow on Jacquard’s sleeve to another loom. In physics, a printed diagram could put the light paths and mirrors from Cleveland on a bench a continent away. Behind either arrangement stood people with good reasons to be paid while they worked. What I mean by a *material and Platonic* system is quite tangible here. You can change the mass of the slab without doubling the path of the light. Rearrange the mirrors and you can double the path on the same piece of stone. The constraint belongs to the relation between things, and it changes what the people who have bought the things can find out.[3]

On June 23, 1977, a tracking station in Panama picked up the first signal from a navigation satellite called NTS-2. It carried two cesium frequency standards. For fifteen days the engineers who had gone to the trouble of launching atomic clocks kept time in a quartz-only mode. It used less electricity. Once the solar panels were out they could run a cesium standard at full power. Now the station in Panama could compare its own clock with one speeding overhead. Over six days the measurements disclosed a rate that would put the orbiting clock ahead by about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day. The higher orbit made a clock run faster relative to one on the ground, while its motion slowed it. The net effect, which requires general as well as special relativity, was close to the prediction. The engineers adjusted the frequency on the spacecraft.[9]

Here, for once, we can say just when a physical relation began doing a new job. A clock on the ground could be compared with one in orbit, and the discrepancy that might otherwise have spoiled their agreement became a correction people could use. In June 1905 that return was a good deal harder to see. Nobody deciding whether to support a physicist could enter a navigation satellite on the credit side of the ledger.

My proposal is that disequilibrium acts through this longer history. A society drawing work from available energy can use some of the gain to keep an instrument in repair, a colleague in employment and an inconvenient result in circulation. Those activities can widen what the next generation is able to measure and build. When that generation uses its new powers to sustain more such work, an institution can reproduce its capacity for inquiry. I suspect that routes which let a system draw down a gradient at a greater sustained rate are favored, up to the proposed maximum rate of entropy production among the routes actually open to it. The journal shows us how a difficult condition reached Poincaré. The Panama record shows what a clock gained. To establish that stronger claim we would have to follow the physical return through the intervening years and compare it with what else the same society could have sustained.[3][5][9]

The Bache Fund’s contribution to an optical measurement took decades to yield a practical correction to a flying clock. In Cornwall William West was asked for an account of the work his engine could return on a bushel of coal in one day. His answer depended on an accident that neither of the instruments hired to settle the matter could register.
