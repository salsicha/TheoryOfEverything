# Epilogue — The World That Builds Its Next Question

Give the racks their electricity at last. The engineer in our imagined data center sends her question about cooling the next hall. She includes the records from the older building. Both temperature sensors now have their proper names. The answer comes with a drawing and a graph of the hottest afternoon in the older hall. She lays them on a desk where the people who worked there can see them.

The operator who worked that afternoon puts a finger on the quietest part of the graph.

For nearly an hour the outside temperature had climbed while the temperature at the racks barely moved. The proposal treats that hour as evidence of what the automatic controls could manage. On those terms a smaller cooling plant looks like a good buy. The operator remembers taking the controls back and bringing another fan into service. The meters recorded the electricity it drew. The fact that somebody had to make a decision is in the shift log, in her handwriting. She goes and gets the book. The engineer has spent the morning checking every spike on the graph. Next time she will know to look at the level stretches too.

It makes a difference which document you inherit. Give the next engineer the graph alone and she might design away the margin that got the last hall through the heat. Give her the graph and the log, and she can ask a better question. What could they save on the days the air outside was cold, while keeping the extra fan and the authority to start it on an afternoon like this one?

I would give the machine some credit for that question. Its proposal put a year's worth of readings into a form in which someone who remembered a single afternoon could see a mistake. I would give the operator some credit for the curve. Her work is there in the hour when nothing much appeared to happen. One virtue of a test record like the Cornish engine reports is that a stranger can challenge a fine figure. Another is that the person with reason to challenge it needn't be a stranger. She might be in the room.[1]

In Lyon, J.-L. Moulin faced a small difficulty in making a pattern for the woven portrait of Jacquard. Where the silk was to be black, he needed a dark wash on squared paper. Black paint would hide the lines. He used brown so the next person could count. His name was left off the silk. We know enough to see his hand in it because a partial copy of the pattern survived, along with the account he gave the museum many years later. Look at the curtain Carquillat wove behind Jacquard's chair. You can see daylight through it. It takes a little more effort to see someone bent over a sheet of paper, counting the lines Moulin took care to leave visible.[2]

That person has interested me more and more as this book has gone on. I began with a Sudoku program and a question about where it ought to look first. In the little world I had given it, one answer could make the next square cheap to fill. At the flooded mine, someone had to buy the first cart of dry coal before there was any answer to pass on. When the water fell below the passage, a crew could walk in. A few inches made the difference between a seam they could point to and a seam that could pay for tomorrow's pumping. Part of the first load out could go straight back to the fire. Someone who had watched the joint on a pump rod being fitted could show a second crew how to keep another mine dry. There had been no square for that second crew on my board.[3]

This is the sense in which I think creation has a direction. A gradient supplies the work. The height of the passage entrance determines how much water has to come out before anybody gains a shift underground. Together a crew and an engine can reach fuel that neither could reach alone. When enough of the gain goes back into renewing the arrangement, there can be more of it in the world. The clearest case of *autopoiesis* is a living cell using part of the work available across its membrane to make more membrane. At a mine the return runs through boilers, pay packets and people who know how to fit a joint. The dry coal, the engine and the people are material. The relation between how fast the water comes in and how fast the pump takes it out is what I have called Platonic. The shift depends on both. Bring the machinery and leave out that relation and you may have bought yourself an expensive way to stir water.

At the mine the engine begins as a claimant on the coal and becomes one of the means of getting it. In the hall the computers begin as a burden on the cooling plant and acquire a hand in its design. That turn is what I find persuasive in dialectics. The thing that imposes a limit can acquire the means to change it. The new arrangement carries forward an old ability, here the operator's ability to intervene, and gives it a different place in the work. If enough costly decisions can be made reliably in advance, a hall that was beyond a crew's capacity to run becomes one it can take on. Artificial intelligence and the buildings that house it seem to me natural developments in a world with that history. Powering the machines was the first half of the story. What they can help power is the interesting half.

It cost money to produce a reassuringly quiet line in the older hall. It also cost money, in another century, to put a stone slab afloat on mercury and watch for a shift in a band of light that scarcely moved. Years later Poincaré could inherit that difficulty by opening a journal. He asked what had to hold true when one change of viewpoint was followed by another. Einstein began with the time on a watch beside a railway track. In June 1905, by different routes, both reached a relation in which the time assigned to a distant event depended on where it took place. What looks like a vast space for solitary invention grew narrow enough for two people to meet. I suspect that a history of extracting work and using it to sustain instruments, teachers and inquiry helped bring those routes into being. There is still a debt to pay on that claim. The power it took to keep a laboratory going cannot tell you which equation was right.[4]

At the shaft I could always send the carter to a working mine in the next valley. Where could I send him for the first usable difference in the universe? If reality is ontologically uncertain, even the relations we call fundamental may have acquired their definiteness. My conjecture about fine-tuning is that the constants we measure are traces of autopoietic physical assemblies. An arrangement made work available from a gradient. Some of that work restored the very arrangement that gave its interactions their strength. Among the combinations physically within reach, I suspect the ones we inherited are those that sustained the greatest rate of energy extraction. To earn that explanation for a single constant, someone would have to find the gradient, follow the work back and come out with the measured value. I cannot do that for the cosmological constant. It matters that the book can name a number it owes the reader.[5]

There is a smaller debt we can settle before we leave the building. Suppose the crew tries the revised cooling plan through a winter and a summer. They enter the hours spent on ordinary controls on the same sheet as the hours the new settings worked. They keep track of the computing they delivered and the cost of getting the design into service. In this telling there is a saving left over. It could pay for more computing. It could also pay for two names to appear together on a shift schedule for a while. Until now the experienced operator has been teaching her replacement, when she can, in the few minutes between one job and the next.

The crew asks for the overlap and gets it. A maximum rate of entropy production, even if one could establish it for this facility, would never have settled that choice for them. It matters who has a vote on the use of a saving. It also matters to the people on the shift that the vote makes its way onto the payroll.

On their first paid afternoon together the operator brings out the old graph and the shift log. They find the quiet hour and the entry about the extra fan. Then the younger woman turns to the previous week's readings. There is another afternoon when the temperature scarcely moved, though the weather changed. Her pencil rests on the date. Who was on duty that day?

The older woman draws her chair around to the same side of the desk. There is time on the shift to find out.
