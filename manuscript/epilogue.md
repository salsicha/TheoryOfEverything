# Epilogue — The World That Builds Its Next Question

On July 18, 1843, Claude Bonnefond signed for five hundred francs. Michel-Marie Carquillat, a silk weaver on the Croix-Rousse in Lyon, had commissioned a painting of the duke’s visit to his workshop. The receipt gives the hour of the visit, half past two on August 24, 1841, and specifies who would pay to have the painting woven. Carquillat would do that part himself, at his own expense.[1]

Bonnefond placed the two men in front of the loom. Carquillat holds out the portrait of Joseph-Marie Jacquard for the duke to see. Several preparatory drawings and the finished composition survive in Lyon.[1][2]

The visit had given Carquillat something his first set of cards could never have supplied. He now had a subject of his own and a reason to engage the painter who had furnished his first one. Plenty of people saw Jacquard’s portrait. The second picture took shape because the weaver himself could commission the work and undertake the cost of weaving it.[1][2]

André Manin made the new pattern from Bonnefond’s drawing. Someone had to decide which threads would show along the edge of the duke’s sleeve at each pass of the shuttle. Carquillat could inherit the means of weaving a likeness. He still had to secure a new set of decisions about the likeness he wanted.[1][2]

Below Whitehaven, storms had left the harbour at Saltom of little use to ships. Carlisle Spedding still had a working pump beside the Irish Sea. In 1734 and 1735 he built two saltpans. One of the colliery’s steam engines supplied them with seawater.[3]

Underground the men had been paid to get rid of water. At the pans they boiled it away and kept the salt. A steady supply of seawater made a business that was still operating in 1760. By the 1780s the saltworks had closed. One panhouse became a foundry casting iron goods for the collieries.[3]

Bellows drove air into the furnace to get it hot enough to melt iron. The archaeologists who studied the site thought the steam engine installed in 1782 might have driven those bellows. They could establish what the foundry supplied to the pits more securely than what powered its furnace.[3]

At a flooded colliery the fastest way for coal to burn could be through a steam engine. A fire on the surface could release only the energy of the coal put into it. A fire working a pump could give miners access to fuel for many more fires, including its own. This required a seam worth draining, a route to buyers and somebody able to keep the engine running. Take away the route to buyers and there is no reason to expect the largest pump to prevail. Keep it open and even the winding horses become more useful as the water falls.[3]

A pump already built for a mine could also supply a saltpan. Nobody had to invent a way to lift seawater from scratch. When the successor gains access to a larger flow and can put some of it into making further successors, we have a reason to expect that kind of arrangement to recur. We can therefore make a prediction without pretending to know the next invention by name. At a working colliery, ask which improvement would release coal that the existing crews could already cut and carry. At a computing plant, ask which change would release hours that the installed machines now spend waiting. Neither a larger fire nor a faster chip can answer that question on its own.[3][4]

There is a difference between reconstructing such a history inside the universe and explaining the universe itself. At Saltom we know coal and a waterline were there before the pump. In a cosmology of assembly the origin of usable differences and of the laws that govern them is precisely what needs explaining. Ontological uncertainty gives me a reason to think those relations could have become definite. A model that derives a measured constant would give a reader a reason to prefer that history. In Lyon we can still read who decided what a workshop would make.[1][3][4]

On the back of the Louvre’s copy of the duke’s visit is a printed label. Hoeth, a dealer in pictures and engravings, gives his address at the corner of the montée de la Glacière and the rue Saint-Polycarpe in Lyon. His name and street told a customer where to find a picture. The names of the people who could make one are on the front.[1]

On the first silk Petit kept a corner for the firm. On the second, Bonnefond’s name is on the left and Manin’s on the right. The man who had once woven another employer’s order had decided what all three of them would make. Between their names are the words *Tissé par Carquillat*. Woven by Carquillat.[1][2]
