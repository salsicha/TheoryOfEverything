# Epilogue — The World That Builds Its Next Question

On July 18, 1843, Claude Bonnefond, director of Lyon’s school of fine arts, signed a receipt for five hundred francs. Michel-Marie Carquillat, a weaver, had hired him to paint an afternoon in his workshop. Two years earlier the duc d’Aumale had visited to see the woven portrait of Jacquard. Carquillat wanted a picture of the visit. The receipt specified that he would have it woven at his own expense.[1]

Both silks survive. In the first, Jacquard holds a drafting compass above a chain of cards. In the second, Carquillat shows the first picture to the duke. In Charles Babbage’s drawing room, David Wilkie had looked closely and still couldn’t tell how the portrait was made. In Lyon, the people who came to see it became the subject of the weaver’s next picture.[1][2]

Running the old cards through the loom would have given Carquillat another Jacquard. There was no duke on them. He needed a painting from Bonnefond and a new pattern from André Manin. The first silk had given him a subject for the second and a chance to show what he could do. We don’t know how much he earned on it. The receipt does tell us who chose and paid for the next picture.[1]

In 1702 Thomas Savery had asked mine owners to consider what would happen if they burned some of their coal to get at the rest. At Saltom, Carlisle Spedding later sank a shaft with room on one side to pump water and on the other to wind up coal. The remains of the horse walk are still there. The horses could lift a load once miners had cut and filled it. To reach the coal in the first place, somebody had to keep the seam dry.[3]

At a flooded mine the fastest way for coal to burn can be through a steam engine. Count only the coal under the boiler and the pump looks expensive. Include the coal still in the seam and the account changes. By burning some fuel at the pit, the owners could get far more out to people who would burn it elsewhere. In time, Saltom’s pumps drained other workings as well.[3]

This is what I mean by creation. A source of energy is available, but only certain arrangements of the materials and skills at hand can use it. At Saltom the pump had to lower the water below the seam and keep it there long enough for men to cut coal. In Lyon the pattern maker had to specify which threads the loom would lift on each pass. These relations are what I call Platonic. Their consequences are material. A miner can enter the seam. A weaver can make a curtain in which a painter thinks he sees light.[2][3]

Once such an assembly exists, why should it appear again? Coal from a drained mine can pay for another day of pumping. A cell can use some of the energy it obtains to make the parts its daughters will inherit. Biologists call this kind of self-production *autopoiesis*. I think it explains why some new assemblies become common. Those able to draw more usable energy and spend part of it on producing more of their kind can leave more successors. The question of how they first formed comes earlier.[3][4]

The mine also makes the three dialectical changes concrete. Fuel was both what the owners wanted and what they had to spend. Pumping a little longer could change the work altogether once the water fell below the seam. And overcoming the limit of horse power preserved a use for horses. Once the miners could work, the horses had coal to raise.[3]

On June 5, 1905, Henri Poincaré presented a result about motion to the French Academy of Sciences. On June 30 a German physics journal received Albert Einstein’s treatment of much the same transformation. Poincaré asked what happens when changes of reference frame are combined. Einstein asked how to synchronize two clocks in different places. Agreement on the transformation still left room for Einstein’s new account of what the clocks measured.[5]

How could two people find so nearly the same answer in the same month? Long before either paper, Michelson and Morley had spent money, obtained mercury and built a rotating apparatus to look for motion through the ether. They published how little shift they saw. Poincaré could use their finding without paying to repeat their work. This is the connection I propose between energy and discovery. Societies spend some of the work available to them on finding out which answers nature allows. The result can narrow the problem for people working in different places. The dates alone cannot tell us how much that explains about relativity.[5]

At Lost City, Deborah Kelley and her colleagues measured differences between the vent fluid and the surrounding seawater. Eric Smith and Harold Morowitz proposed that early metabolism enabled the planet to use chemical energy that otherwise remained largely unavailable. Minerals and flowing water already limited which reactions could take place. The same question arises a step earlier. Where did the physical differences that life could use come from?[4]

My starting premise is *ontological uncertainty*. Some distinctions become definite as the universe develops. Physicists can describe symmetry breaking once they know the rules and the states allowed. I have asked whether some rules and constants might themselves have formed. A value that looks finely tuned when varied alone might belong to a set of relations that could only have developed together. My conjecture is that some arrangements persisted because they drew the most work per unit time from an available gradient and used part of it to reproduce the relations that fixed the value. To establish that, we need an account that produces an observed number. The cosmological constant is one we still have to explain.[6]

In the case of computing we know rather more about what happened. Anna Goldie could point to a manufactured processor whose layout a learning system had helped design. The processor was built for machine learning. This is why I think artificial intelligence and data centers arise naturally in societies that can supply them. Enough past work becomes available in a form machines can learn from, and some of the work they do improves the equipment on which their successors will run. Someone still has to decide which machine is worth building.[7]

On the 1839 silk, Moulin had wanted a corner for his name. The manufacturer wanted the space for the firm. Carquillat’s name never appeared. On the silk he commissioned, the duke and his attendants are identified below the picture. So are Bonnefond, who painted the scene, and Manin, who drew the pattern. The person who commissioned the picture had a credit of his own. In the middle of the lower margin are three words. *Tissé par Carquillat*. Woven by Carquillat.[1][2]
