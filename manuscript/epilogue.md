# Epilogue — The World That Builds Its Next Question

On July 18, 1843, Claude Bonnefond signed for five hundred francs. He was director of Lyon’s school of fine arts. The man paying him was Michel-Marie Carquillat, a weaver. The subject of the commission was an afternoon in Carquillat’s workshop, two years before, when the duc d’Aumale came to see the portrait of Jacquard. The receipt specifies who would turn the painter’s composition into silk and at whose expense. Carquillat intended to do both.[1]

The two silks survive. In the first, Jacquard holds his drafting compass above a chain of cards. In the second, Carquillat shows that first picture to the duke. We began with one of these objects behind glass in Charles Babbage’s drawing room. Even the painter David Wilkie had trouble seeing what it was made of. By the time Carquillat took on the second picture, he could give his audience something Wilkie never saw. The people who had gathered to look at the work could themselves be woven into it.[1][2]

The old cards would have given Carquillat another Jacquard. There was no duke on them. For the visit he needed a fresh composition from Bonnefond and a new pattern from André Manin. The first achievement changed the field in which the second was made. It supplied a subject, an occasion to paint it and public evidence that this weaver could render it in silk. The receipt goes one step further. It puts Carquillat in a position to choose what the next work would be. What portion of his five hundred francs he earned on the first portrait has escaped the record.[1]

In 1702 Thomas Savery asked English mine owners to take a similar change of circumstances seriously. A pump that consumed fuel could enlarge the trade in fuel. On the Cumberland coast, Carlisle Spedding later sank a shaft with room on one side to pump out water and on the other to wind up coal. To this day the remains of the horse walk sit beside the pumping shaft at Saltom. A horse could raise a load from the bottom. No number of turns round the horse walk could put a miner to work in a seam under water. That distinction gave the engine its job.[3]

At a flooded mine the fastest way for coal to burn can be through a steam engine. The proposition sounds odd as long as the account begins with a pile of coal already at the surface and ends when that pile is ash. Include the seam. The use of part of the fuel to lift water gives people access to far more fuel than the engine itself consumes. Saltom’s pumps eventually drained other workings too. What the engine changed was the fate of coal that could otherwise have stayed in the ground.[3]

This is what I mean by creation. A difference from which work can be drawn is present, and there are only certain ways the available materials and skills can put it to use. When those relations come together, something becomes possible that no part had the capacity to do before. Sinking and draining the Cumberland shaft had a physical cost. The machinery also embodied a relation, as exact in its way as the correspondence between a square of Moulin’s grid and a warp thread in Lyon. The pumps had to lower the water below the seam and keep it there long enough for a miner to go in and cut coal. That relation is what I call Platonic. Steam, water, silk and human work are how such a relation acquires consequences in the world.[2][3]

Once an assembly exists, there is another question to answer. Why should anyone ever encounter its like again? Fuel brought up by pumping can buy another day of pumping. A cell can spend part of the work it draws from its surroundings on producing the components its daughters will inherit. *Autopoiesis* gives a name to that return in a living organization. I think it explains a preference in the history of creation. Assemblies that open generous sources of work, and can use some of the gain to produce more of their kind, will tend to occur more often. Their successors start with means that the first instance had to bring into existence.[3][4]

This also gives an old dialectical idea a tangible meaning. At the pit, fuel was at once what people sought and what they had to spend to get it. A little more pumping, sustained long enough, made the difference between water over a seam and men working it. The engine that overcame a limit on the use of horses gave a horse at the top of the next shaft a new job. Such reversals matter because they make different futures accessible. The sharpest question about a successful invention is often what some other person can now do for the first time.[3]

On June 5, 1905, Henri Poincaré presented a result about motion to the French Academy of Sciences. On June 30 the editors of a German physics journal received Albert Einstein’s treatment of much the same transformation. Poincaré had asked what happens when changes of frame are combined. Einstein had asked how two separated clocks could be set to tell the same time. Two routes had narrowed toward a shared requirement. Meanwhile Einstein’s account of what a clock measures went further than agreement on the equation. There was room for a real act of invention on a path a second person could find.[5]

Their figures did not come from a furnace as directly as the water rose at Saltom. Michelson and Morley had the money, the mercury and the skills to look for a motion through the ether, then the means to publish how small a shift they actually saw. Poincaré could put that finding to work without financing a second stone slab. This is the route I propose from usable energy to discovery. Some of the work a society can command goes into producing and circulating constraints on what can count as an answer. Once that has happened, two people with quite different questions can come upon a result that would have been astonishing as a guess. How much this process explains about the timing of relativity remains an open question.[5]

There is one place where it becomes particularly difficult to identify what the first investigator inherited. At Lost City, Deborah Kelley and her colleagues could measure contrasts between vent fluid and the surrounding ocean. Eric Smith and Harold Morowitz proposed that metabolism gave an early planet a new route for drawing work from such persistent chemical differences. In that account the rocks have already supplied a great deal, including some of the constraints that make particular reactions accessible. Where did a universe get its first differences? An account that begins by lending it a mature planet and an ocean has begun rather late.[4]

My starting premise is *ontological uncertainty*. Some physical distinctions can become definite in the course of the world’s history. Symmetry breaking gives physicists concrete instances of differentiation once rules and possible states have been specified. I have asked whether the rules, and the constants by which we express them, might also be products of assembly. Perhaps a value that looks exquisitely tuned when varied alone belongs to a set of relations that could only develop together. Perhaps the configurations that emerged and endured were those that could draw the most work per unit time from a gradient and use some of it to reproduce the very relations fixing the value. That is a conjecture about a natural history for the universe. It owes us at least one observed number. The small value of the cosmological constant would be a fine place to start.[6]

At the other end of this history, Anna Goldie could identify a manufactured processor whose layout had been shaped in part by a learning system. The processor was itself intended for machine learning. Learning had become one of the means by which a society could decide where to put things on the equipment it would use to learn next time. That is my reason for calling artificial intelligence and the buildings that supply its power natural developments. A society able to store the results of past work and pay to learn from them acquires a new use for both its knowledge and its electricity. Which machine it builds still depends on whose problem is worth the cost of solving.[7]

Carquillat left us an unusually good answer to that last question. When Moulin sought a little space on the 1839 silk for his name, the manufacturer wanted the corner for his firm. On the 1844 silk there was space for Bonnefond, Manin and Carquillat. The duke is still a duke in the finished picture, attended by officials whose names and titles are written out below. But the decision to put him there belonged to the man showing him a piece of weaving. Five years earlier, Carquillat had helped make a portrait whose inscription gave him no place at all. Now he had woven the duke, the spectators and the picture they had gathered to see. In the middle of the lower margin are three words. *Tissé par Carquillat*. Woven by Carquillat.[1][2]
