# Chapter 7 — The Species That Changed Its Own Conditions

**Part III · Human Society and Collective Intelligence**

By the time Richard Lee went looking for a second cow, the first had cost him twenty pounds and could easily have fed everybody. Ben!a, a mother of five, had asked what he proposed they eat. The horns? Even a cattle owner Lee trusted thought the animal was painfully thin. What if the portions ran short and a fight broke out?[1]

It was an unusually well-chosen subject on which to make an anthropologist doubt himself. In the 1960s Lee was living among the Ju/’hoansi in the Kalahari to find out how much food people obtained and how they shared it. He kept his own food apart so that his presence would disturb his observations as little as possible. From the other side of the tins this looked rather different. A man with two months’ food put by had come to study people who would share what they had today. At Christmas, when neighboring pastoralists customarily provided an ox, he had a chance to thank his hosts. He bought the biggest animal he could find from a Herero friend.[1]

On Christmas morning the first cuts exposed about two inches of fat. Even then, as Lee tells it, his friend /gaugo maintained for a moment that the poor creature was painfully thin. Everybody started laughing. They ate for two days. Later Lee asked Tomazo, a hunter, what all that disparagement was for. A man who supplied other people with meat, Tomazo explained, might begin to think himself their chief.[1]

The answer turns the usual puzzle of human cooperation on its head. We ask what would induce somebody to provide dinner for the whole company. Tomazo was interested in what might induce the company to keep accepting dinner from him. If every successful hunt added a little to one man’s authority, a run of good luck could get expensive for everyone else. In this respect a gift of meat has a curious property. Long after the last of it has been eaten, somebody may turn up to collect.

## Who owns the animal?

In some Ju/’hoansi communities a visitor wanting to know who would divide a large animal had a deceptively simple question to ask. Who owned the arrow? Hunters borrowed arrows. The person entitled to make the first distribution could have spent the whole day in camp. A particularly good hunter could supply dinner several times running while someone else had the pleasure, and the inconvenience, of settling who got which portion.[2]

There is a political invention in that loan. A good hunter can bring in a great deal of food without thereby acquiring the right to tell everyone what to do with it. Knowing whose hand held the arrow would give an archaeologist the wrong answer about authority in camp. The field accounts record who owned the arrows and what rights went with them. Those details are how we know that the right to give the meat away could travel separately from the skill needed to bring it in. A record of the animals killed would have left out the part of the arrangement that made an animal worth lending an arrow for.[2]

A change of equipment could give that same distinction a different political life. Lee described Ju/’hoansi men in western Ngamiland who hunted with guns borrowed from Herero or Tswana owners. Much of the meat went to the owner. Robert Hitchcock and his colleagues later recorded a variety of bargains between owners and hunters, some satisfactory to both parties, some leaving remarkably little for the person who had done the work. A rule that spread the privilege of distribution when equipment circulated among neighbors could concentrate that privilege when a few people owned the equipment others needed. A more productive hunt could mean less meat in the hunter’s own household.[2]

Human beings have been one another’s environment for a very long time. We generally picture the consequences in one direction. A child grows up under a particular set of rules and learns to live by them. But consider an adult who has always got his way by threatening people. As long as he can deal with each person separately, the arrangement may suit him very well. Give his neighbors the ability to confer and to rely on one another afterward. His muscles are as strong as they were yesterday. There are now considerably fewer things he can get with them.

That change can happen within a lifetime. A second change is possible on a much longer clock. If people differ in their propensity for impulsive violence, if some of that difference is inherited, and if it regularly affects who survives or raises children, then people who choose their associates and defend one another help shape the inheritance of their descendants. This is the proposition called *human self-domestication*. Animals in this account take part in making the social conditions under which their own evolution proceeds.[3]

Richard Wrangham gives the proposition a disturbing twist. He distinguishes the violence of an angry outburst, which he calls *reactive*, from violence that people plan and carry out deliberately. He suggests that coalitions among our ancestors sometimes killed habitually aggressive men. If they did so often enough and over enough generations, planned aggression could have selected against a disposition to lash out at a slight. On this view the ability to make a place relatively safe for children owes something to a capacity those children would have excellent reason to fear.[3]

What would we have to know to tell whether it happened? A person can learn in a few humiliating days to speak differently about his generosity. A person who often loses his temper can lose partners or allies without anyone laying a hand on him. The history Wrangham proposes requires a sustained difference in who left descendants. No one has a tally of such deaths for the Pleistocene. And a face, however well preserved, is a poor witness to how its owner behaved when contradicted.[3][4]

There was a moment when the career of the silver fox seemed to promise a shorter route to the answer. In animals bred for tameness, changes in the face, ears and coat have often been taken to travel together. One proposal traces some of them to changes in a population of embryonic cells, the neural crest, whose descendants contribute to several different tissues. Then Kathryn Lord and her colleagues went back into the history of the fur trade. Some traits famously attributed to the fox breeding experiment had already appeared in the farm stock from which the animals came. A good account of what a breeder changed turns out to require a good account of what a previous generation of breeders had already done. Dor Shilton and her colleagues make a different case for human beings. People learn when anger is appropriate, when it is dangerous, and when somebody else needs them to express it. Think how odd a childhood would be if the only thing anyone ever learned about anger was to have less of it.[4]

## A pair of empty hands

Kristen Hawkes, James O’Connell and Nicholas Blurton Jones accompanied Hadza women and children on trips to dig tubers in northern Tanzania. At the digging places an older child would sometimes take the baby. A mother could dig, nurse, and go back to digging. Toward midday the women gathered at a fire with what they had found. Some of the food they carried there owed its arrival to a child who had dug up none of it.[5]

The difficulty found its way into the researchers’ tables. A woman grinding baobab while holding a child could be entered under food processing. There was still a child on her lap. What disappeared from the column was the work another person would have had to take over if she put the child down. We have a tendency to see cooperation at the moment of division, when one person hands another a share. By that reckoning the child at the digging site has to wait for lunch before she can enter the story. She has already changed the size of the lunch.[5]

Older women put in long hours at the digging too. In a small follow-up comparison of families with a nursing infant, the youngest weaned children tended to gain more weight when a grandmother spent more time foraging, after allowance for their mothers’ foraging time. The researchers sometimes counted as a grandmother an older woman who helped support children beyond her own direct descendants. Kinship on a chart and the people doing the daily work could give a researcher different lists. The finding bears on a much larger question about why human beings can live so long after they have had their last child. What one can see at a digging place is how readily a change in one person’s available hours can reach the stomach of somebody two generations away.[5]

Other primates offer a way of looking at the question without assuming in advance what makes people special. In a comparison of fifteen species, Judith Burkart and her colleagues found that the extent to which others helped mothers care for young was the best predictor they examined of spontaneous help for another. Here the repeated demand for company comes from the least formidable member of the group. An infant can oblige several people to reorganize their day without winning a single contest for dominance.[6]

Tubers store chemical energy human bodies can use. Hawkes and her colleagues documented a difference in access to that energy while the food itself stayed underground. Care changed whose hands were available for digging. That relation, which other people can take up at another site, is the Platonic element of a material system. Measuring only the weight brought back by each named digger would assign part of a joint achievement to one person. The relation matters because it changes the food a group can obtain, and thus its ability to support children and caregivers.[5]

Teaching carries the dependency across a longer interval. At Whitehaven a colliery could hire a pumping engine in a single transaction. Its agent was still counting the people able to repair it years later. Children likewise have to be fed through years in which older people can do much of the work faster. Whether they gain access to the work in the meantime is a condition of the group’s next generation of expertise.

## How old is an expert?

Gul Deniz Salali and her colleagues describe a mushroom outing among BaYaka children in the Republic of the Congo. A younger girl went along with two experienced gatherers and watched which mushrooms they picked. Both experts were six. They were twins. No adult went along to deliver the lesson.[7]

The outing suggests a small correction to the picture of culture descending from a row of elders. If children can safely go along with slightly older children, every advance in competence adds somebody to the number one can learn from. The youngest girl gets to see a series of selections close up. Later, when she is by herself and comes across an unfamiliar specimen, she has acquired a further useful piece of information. She knows who might be able to help. It is a fair achievement for a morning out to have enlarged both what you know and your acquaintance with people who know more.

The same research team saw younger children watching an adolescent distribute meat under an adult’s guidance. They waited for their own portions while he worked. Here an attentive learner faces a different problem. A mushroom may have some feature that helps a person recognize it in another part of the forest. No feature of a piece of meat will tell you whether your neighbor’s child should have it. You have to know who else is there, what has gone before and whose correction the people involved will accept. In adulthood both kinds of judgment can determine how well a child eats.[7]

A community that keeps doing this acquires a capacity no single gifted member could leave it in a will. It can make a new gatherer. The term *autopoiesis* arose in biology for an organization whose activity produces the components that keep the organization going. In extending it to a human practice, I want to be able to find the beginners and the people who give them a place. The morning’s harvest and the novices who learned where to find the next one were two distinct returns on the same outing. It matters which part of today’s return is going into another person’s ability to go out next season.[8]

It is tempting to give that achievement a birthday. We have a face in Morocco, an upper jaw in Israel, ancient camps scattered across Africa, and a floor in the Jordan Valley. Arranged in the right order, they might seem to show us a species getting ready to think. They become more interesting when we ask what sort of event each of them can actually date.

At Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, flints heated long ago helped researchers date the associated human remains to roughly 315,000 years ago. The face and teeth have features familiar in our species. The braincase combines them with an older shape. At Misliya Cave in Israel an upper jaw attributed to *Homo sapiens* has been assigned an age of 177,000 to 194,000 years, a dating that has drawn objections. Other Levantine finds also place members of our species outside Africa well before the dispersal, around 50,000 years ago, from which most living Eurasians derive most of their ancestry. A population can cross a boundary many generations before descendants of a later crossing come to occupy large parts of the world.[9][10]

Emily Hallett, Michela Leonardi and their colleagues looked back from that later expansion and found something going on inside Africa. Beginning around 70,000 years ago, people appear to have made a living in a widening range of habitats, including forests and dry regions. Rainfall affected which routes out of Africa were passable. Being equipped to make a living in a new place, finding the way open and leaving descendants there are all accomplishments with histories of their own. If our only question about the people in Africa is why they took so long to leave, the education that might have made a later crossing successful will register as time spent waiting.[10]

Around twelve thousand years ago some communities began the changes we now group together as the transition to agriculture. At Dhraʿ in the Jordan Valley people were storing grain above the ground on specially built floors before there is clear local evidence for domesticated crops. The space beneath the floor protected what was above it. A store put part of one season’s harvest within reach of people in another. That delay created a new decision, when to release the food. The excavated floors tell us that people had acquired the means to make it. They leave open who had the right to decide.[11]

## Back at the feast

Across the border from Lee’s Christmas gathering, the Nyae Nyae Farmers Cooperative in Namibia later debated whether to permit hunting with guns and dogs. After considerable discussion it turned the request down. One member feared that too little game would survive. Another favored permission with limits on where and how much could be taken. We have the disagreement because people thought it worth listening to both opinions. They were choosing what kind of place their children would have to make a living in.[2]

That decision gives a sharper meaning to the thought that new forms of organization tend to improve access to energy. A community may profit from a way of securing more food once. To keep profiting, it has to carry some advantage into the next round. Where the owners take much of the return, or the animals disappear, someone can be getting better at extraction while the people doing it grow poorer. Whether an arrangement increases the lasting rate at which a community and its surroundings dissipate energy requires following that wider history. The people who turned down the proposal had a reason to care about the length of the count.

Lee’s hosts used concerted ridicule to put a limit on the authority that could come with giving away meat. The Nyae Nyae members later exercised collective authority over which hunting methods an individual might use. Each case makes a different part of the dialectic visible. People who associate to secure food acquire the power to regulate the people and tools that secure it. Exercising that power changes what the next generation can learn and what its country will yield.[1][2]

Lee had set out to thank people who had taught him a great deal. He could have gone home knowing that the meat had lasted and that he had judged a fat ox correctly. He got a better return because, after everybody had eaten, he was willing to go back to Tomazo and ask what on earth had just happened. Perhaps that is a reasonable place to look for the beginnings of human intelligence. It takes considerable ingenuity to make such a joke. It takes a particular kind of company to let the butt of it in on the explanation.

The BaYaka researchers observed a child learning from other children who already knew where to go. In Lyon a silk weaver left physical evidence of a skill similarly spread among people who did different parts of the work. Michel-Marie Carquillat wove a curtain through which the viewer could see daylight. The man who calculated which threads would make it transparent had to fight even to have his name included.[11]
