# Chapter 7 — The Species That Changed Its Own Conditions

**Part III · Human Society and Collective Intelligence**

Richard Lee thought he had bought four pounds of meat for every man, woman and child who would come to Christmas dinner. Ben!a wanted to know whether he expected his guests to eat the horns.

Lee was an anthropologist working with the Ju/’hoansi in the Kalahari. For the last Christmas of that field stay he had bought an enormous black ox from a Herero friend as a thank-you to the people who had helped him. Estimating how much food people obtained was his professional business. Yet after Ben!a, other guests came by with the same objection. Even a cattle owner whose opinion Lee trusted thought the animal was pitifully thin. By the time Christmas arrived, Lee had spent several days wondering how he had managed to buy so much ox and so little dinner.[1]

Then the animal was opened up. Under the black hide lay a breadth of white fat that Lee estimated at two inches. His guests found his relief extremely funny. They ate and danced for two days and two nights. When he asked Tomazo, one of the hunters, what purpose all that discouragement served, Tomazo gave him a one-word answer. “Arrogance.” People took care to belittle a big kill, he explained, because a man who regularly brought home meat might develop some unfortunate ideas about who was in charge.[1]

Here are two things a person might hope to take away from a feast. One is the strength to get up and do some work tomorrow. The other is a claim on the people who ate. The people around Lee had an interest in what happened to both. They could eat the ox he provided and have a say in what his generosity entitled him to do afterward. What interested them in the animal was something a measurement across its back would never settle.

The feast had a perfectly particular history. The holiday had come through missionaries and neighboring pastoralists. The ox had been bought by a visitor with money and his own store of tinned food.[1] We can ask a question about human evolution here precisely because we know which part of the event we are trying to explain. How did we become creatures for whom the opinions of the people waiting to eat could change what it was worth doing to get the food in the first place?

## Who can afford to lose their temper?

Suppose a man regularly takes the best portion and strikes the person who objects. Other people have appetites just as good as his. What they can do about it depends partly on whether each has to deal with him alone. If several can count on one another, his physical strength stays much the same while his prospects change considerably. The next time he reaches across the food he has an unfamiliar calculation to make. His neighbors have helped change the environment in which his temper has to serve him.

An individual can learn this in an afternoon. Now consider an altogether slower possibility. Suppose people differ in how readily they lash out under provocation and some of that difference is heritable. Suppose, over many generations, people inclined to fly into a rage are less likely to find partners, keep allies or survive to raise children. Under those conditions the people in a community could become part of a selective pressure on their own descendants. That is the evolutionary proposition in *human self-domestication*. The very people who inherit a disposition also inherit a social world partly shaped by earlier bearers of it.[2]

Richard Wrangham gives the idea an unsettling form. He distinguishes *reactive aggression*, the outburst in response to a threat or slight, from *proactive aggression*, deliberate harm undertaken for a purpose. He proposes that some early human communities put persistently violent bullies to death. If such sanctions altered reproductive success consistently enough, a capacity for planned violence could have helped select for a reduced inclination to strike someone in the heat of an argument. In this account two people can sit down together in relative safety partly because of a human capacity that neither would care to see turned against them.[2]

The Christmas story lets us see one end of that long argument. Other people really can bring an overconfident adult up short. Lee could describe the day on which it happened to him. To get from a learned change of conduct to a change in inherited disposition we would need a history extending across generations. We have no count of how often Pleistocene communities imposed lethal sanctions, on whom, or with what consequences for their descendants. Someone who keeps losing partners because of his temper and someone who learns to steady herself because a younger child depends on her belong to possible histories too. The biological consequences could be quite different.[2][3]

Some researchers have looked for signs of selection in the body. A proposal by Adam Wilkins, Wrangham and Tecumseh Fitch concerns the neural crest, a population of embryonic cells whose descendants help build parts of the face, pigment cells and other tissues. A developmental change associated with selection for tameness might affect several traits together. The familiar case of the silver fox shows why that can be difficult to establish. Kathryn Lord and colleagues traced the ancestry of the foxes in a celebrated breeding study back to the fur trade. Some traits often credited to the experiment were already present in farm foxes. A photograph at the end of a breeding program can hide a good deal about the animals at the beginning. Dor Shilton and her colleagues place more weight on the ways people learn emotional control in a social world. Learning to be angry on someone else's behalf belongs to that world as surely as learning to hold one's tongue.[3]

There is a further difficulty with calling a more tractable animal a more intelligent one. If nobody hits me for fumbling a job, I can try it twice. Where does that second chance come from? Someone has to bear the cost of my first mistake. And if I am ever going to acquire anything worth passing on, there must be someone nearby who knows something I have yet to learn.

## What the child brought home

In northern Tanzania, Kristen Hawkes, James O’Connell and Nicholas Blurton Jones followed Hadza women and children going out to dig wild tubers. Some women carried nursing infants. At the digging sites an older child would sometimes sit with a baby while its mother dug. The mother could stop to nurse and go back to digging. Around midday the women brought the tubers to a common fire.[4]

Try to enter that older child’s contribution in a list of who brought home how much food. The hands that held the baby could have returned empty. The researchers faced a version of this difficulty when they tabulated the day’s activities. A person grinding baobab while holding a child went into the table under food processing. The baby was still there. Send the older child away in our mental reconstruction of the digging party and somebody has to put down a digging stick. A transfer has taken place before any tuber changes hands. One person has given another the use of part of her day.[4]

The older women did some of the hardest digging. In a later study, the researchers followed how women's work related to the weight changes of children already weaned. In families with a nursing infant, the youngest weaned child tended to gain more weight when a grandmother spent more time foraging, after accounting for the mother’s own foraging time. This comparison was small. In the researchers’ records *grandmother* could include an older woman helping to support children beyond her own direct descendants. You had to know who was working with whom before you could even put the right names in the table. One can see here how care might make a difference to a family's food supply and who gets fed. Whether this kind of help shaped the evolution of the human lifespan is a question on another scale.[4]

Shared care has also been studied across species. In a comparison of fifteen primate species, Judith Burkart and her colleagues found that the extent to which others helped mothers care for young was the strongest predictor they examined of helping that benefited another without being solicited. That opens a route into the human question from the other end of a life. Long before someone is old enough to menace the company at dinner, their arrival has already given other people a substantial problem to solve together.[5]

Think of the opening the book has been looking for. Food holds chemical energy. A deposit of tubers can be there for the taking and still lie beyond what a person can obtain with a baby in her arms. Sharing care can make the very same patch more productive for the community. What changes is the relation among the people who can get to it. That is the material and Platonic character of the arrangement on a scale small enough to watch. A baby has weight. A dependable turn at carrying the baby can alter the work several pairs of hands can do. The arrangement has to be made again tomorrow.

When some of the food goes to a young person, the delay before it pays for itself can be long. We met a much shorter delay at the mine. The first engine had already consumed coal by the time it lifted its first useful load. A child can eat for years while learning things an expert could accomplish before noon. It is possible to improve the way something is taught so the child needs fewer attempts. It is also possible to keep feeding the child through more attempts at a difficult task. A community that does both has changed which skills it can afford to acquire.

For a glimpse of what those attempts look like, go to a BaYaka mushroom-gathering trip in the Republic of the Congo. Gul Deniz Salali and colleagues observed a pair of six-year-old twins picking mushrooms. A younger girl went with them and watched which ones they chose. The girls were foraging without adult help. On this trip the experienced mushroom gatherers were six.[6]

On another occasion in the same study, children gathered around an adolescent boy as he divided meat. An adult guided the distribution. The younger children had two reasons to attend to what happened. They could see how the portions were assigned, and they were waiting for their own pieces. The forest gives a learner information about where a mushroom grows. A piece of meat on the ground carries no comparable mark telling the boy whose it ought to be. To learn that he needs somebody who knows the people around him.[6]

Here is a modest ambition for a theory of intelligence. Account for what that younger girl can eventually do with what she has seen. She has to notice a choice among quite similar things. Later she has to make a choice in a place where the twins may be absent. She may encounter something ambiguous and seek out somebody else's judgment. If another child comes along on her trip, that child gets to start with the benefit of her experience. No single head in this sequence gets a copy of another head. Yet the next generation can begin further along.

The book borrows *autopoiesis* from the biology of systems that help produce the means of their own continued existence. At the scale of a community, this gives us a question we can actually answer in part. Who feeds the beginner? Who gives her access to a task? When she becomes competent, does any of her new capacity go into giving somebody else a turn? One lucky gathering can fill several stomachs. The stronger achievement is a group that can keep making gatherers.[7]

I suspect that some forms of cooperation have spread because they open access to energy and return part of it to the people who can secure that access again. Improving the return from digging is one route. Spending the same return on a longer apprenticeship is another. Either might allow more work to be done later. To claim a maximum rate of entropy production across the whole community and its surroundings, as the book proposes may happen under particular conditions, would take more than counting tubers. Suppose the women use the time saved to come home earlier. The children have still helped them achieve something of value.

## The dates on a human face

How deep does this history go? In Morocco, at Jebel Irhoud, researchers recovered human remains alongside flint that had once been heated. Dating the flint helped put the finds at roughly 315,000 years old. Parts of the face and teeth looked familiar to the researchers who described them as early *Homo sapiens*. The shape of the braincase retained more archaic features. This gives us an occasion when people with a particular combination of anatomical features lived. We could recover a hundred faces and still wish we knew what their owners thought funny.[8]

There is a tempting story in the long interval between those people and the much later expansion of human populations across the world. In that story a brain essentially like ours waits in Africa until cooperation, a mutation, or a turn in the climate suddenly releases it. We have several quite different histories to account for. At Misliya Cave in Israel a partial upper jaw attributed to *Homo sapiens* was reported at about 177,000 to 194,000 years old. The dating has been disputed. Fossil finds in the Levant nevertheless put people of our species outside Africa long before the dispersal to which most living Eurasians trace most of their ancestry, around 50,000 years ago.[9]

Emily Hallett, Michela Leonardi and their colleagues asked a question that puts some life back into the intervening years. Where in Africa had people already learned to make a living? The researchers assembled dated sites and compared them with reconstructions of earlier environments. By their account the range of habitats people used began to widen substantially around 70,000 years ago. Forests and arid regions both figured in the expansion. Climate models also suggest that routes out of Africa became more or less passable as rainfall changed. An opening along a route, a population taking it, and descendants persisting on the far side are three events. People could be gaining experience of unfamiliar environments while generations passed without all three coinciding.[9]

Then, in the last twelve thousand years, farming began to transform the way some people got their food. The granaries at Dhraʿ that we met in the engine chapter had raised floors to protect their contents. They precede clear evidence there of domesticated crops. A store carries a harvest from one season to the next. It also changes what someone can do by refusing to open the door. One can uncover the remains of a floor. The agreement about who might draw on the grain is a more elusive find.[10]

Put a face, a trail and a floor beside the children waiting for meat. Each gives us a different piece of the question. The face tells us something about inheritance through bodies. The occupied sites tell us where people managed to live. The raised floor tells us that some people could now keep food past the season that produced it. At any of these places, keeping a learner in food long enough to acquire an adult skill would have mattered. Trying to read the whole history of intelligence off any one of those dates asks a jawbone or a piece of burnt flint to do a remarkable amount of work.

## Who gets to correct the teacher?

Go back to the Christmas ox. Lee admitted that he had sometimes withheld tobacco from people who declined to cooperate with him. His guests already knew what it could cost to displease the man with the supplies. This time it was Lee who had to come to them for an explanation.[1] Now picture the same gift for collective ridicule in the hands of people determined to silence a dissenter. A child can learn when to speak by seeing what happens to the last person who spoke. The capacity to hold a bully in check and the capacity to make a disagreement intolerable can reside in exactly the same company.

This is the dialectical turn in the history of restraint. People acting together can overcome a kind of domination none could escape acting alone. The agreement that gives them that freedom also creates an authority whose decisions they may one day need to challenge. Make every lesson a test of obedience and the community can train children to repeat its practices with great fidelity. Then let one of those practices fail. What happens to the first person who says so? A community able to hear the answer can keep what it has learned and change what its learners are asked to do. At a certain point a difference in the amount of protected practice or tolerated disagreement becomes a difference in the kind of work a group can undertake.

It is worth remembering who taught whom about the ox. The man trained to measure a food supply had got the quantity more or less right. What he missed was what his hosts knew about the dangers of being the person who hands it out. Human intelligence has room for both achievements, including the faculty that let him go back and ask Tomazo to explain the laughter. We become people who can learn from one another in places other people have made safe enough for an admission of ignorance. Some of what we learn there can help us remake those places.

In a Lyon workshop a weaver once made a piece of silk in which a stranger can recognize a man's face. A learner could hold that finished cloth for years without discovering how to make another. At some point somebody had to be able to sit down beside someone who knew more. And while a beginner spoiled perfectly good thread, both of them had to eat.[10]
