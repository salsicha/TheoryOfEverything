# Chapter 7 — The Species That Changed Its Own Conditions

**Part III · Human Society and Collective Intelligence**

Richard Lee had paid twenty pounds for the biggest ox he could find. Ben!a, a mother of five, wanted to know what he expected people to eat. The horns? Even a cattle owner Lee trusted said the animal was painfully thin. Lee went looking for a second one. He was afraid there would be a fight if the meat ran short.[1]

Lee was an anthropologist living among the Ju/’hoansi in the Kalahari in the 1960s. He studied how much food people obtained and how they shared it. To avoid disturbing his observations he kept his own supplies apart. His hosts shared what they had with one another. They had seen him keep two months’ food for himself. At Christmas, when neighboring pastoralists customarily provided an ox, he bought one from a Herero friend to thank them.[1]

On Christmas morning the first cuts exposed about two inches of fat. Even then, as Lee tells it, his friend /gaugo maintained for a moment that the poor creature was painfully thin. Everybody started laughing. They ate for two days. Later Lee asked Tomazo, a hunter, what all that disparagement was for. A man who supplied other people with meat, Tomazo explained, might begin to think himself their chief.[1]

Tomazo had explained something Lee’s figures on food sharing could never have told him. The people at the feast wanted the meat. They also wanted Lee to understand that providing it gave him no authority over those who ate it. Making fun of a generous hunter was one way to keep him from making that mistake.[1]

## Who owns the animal?

In some Ju/’hoansi communities, the person who owned the arrow used in a successful hunt had the right to divide the meat. Hunters borrowed arrows. The person making the first distribution could therefore have spent the whole day in camp.[2]

Lending an arrow let the community draw on a skilled hunter without giving him control over every animal he killed. The field accounts record whose arrows the hunters carried and who decided where the meat went. The kill by itself would tell us little about who had that authority.[2]

Lee also described Ju/’hoansi men in western Ngamiland who used guns borrowed from Herero or Tswana owners. Much of the meat went to the owner. Robert Hitchcock and colleagues later recorded agreements that left some hunters with very little of what they had brought down. When arrows circulated among neighbors, ownership could distribute authority. Where a few people owned the guns, it could concentrate it.[2]

Cooperation changes the conditions under which people grow up. Children learn whom to ask for food, who is entitled to divide it and what happens to someone who claims too much. Adults can also act together to stop one person from intimidating the others. That can change the advantage an aggressive individual gains from being physically strong.

Learning can change behavior within one lifetime. The hypothesis of *human self-domestication* concerns a change across generations. If differences in impulsive aggression are partly inherited, and if a community consistently affects which people with those dispositions survive and have children, then the community helps shape the evolution of its members.[3]

Richard Wrangham distinguishes reactive aggression, such as lashing out in anger, from violence people plan together. He proposes that groups among our ancestors sometimes killed habitually aggressive men. If this altered who left descendants over many generations, planned violence could have selected against impulsive violence. It is a specific, disputed claim about human evolution.[3]

Lee’s account shows how quickly a community could correct someone’s behavior. A person could also lose allies without being killed. To distinguish either of those effects from genetic selection requires evidence of differences in reproduction over many generations. There is no record of how commonly prehistoric communities imposed lethal sanctions, and the shape of a fossil face cannot establish its owner’s temperament.[3][4]

One influential comparison concerns silver foxes bred for tameness. Researchers proposed that changes in the face, ears and coat might be linked through embryonic cells that contribute to several tissues. Kathryn Lord and colleagues examined the history of the fur trade. Some traits credited to the experiment were already present in the farm stock. For humans there is another possible explanation for some of the differences. Dor Shilton and colleagues emphasize that children learn when to express anger, when to restrain it and when to act on another person’s behalf.[4]

## A pair of empty hands

Kristen Hawkes, James O’Connell and Nicholas Blurton Jones accompanied Hadza women and children on trips to dig tubers in northern Tanzania. At the digging places an older child would sometimes take the baby. A mother could dig, nurse, and go back to digging. Toward midday the women gathered at a fire with what they had found. Some of the food they carried there owed its arrival to a child who had dug up none of it.[5]

The researchers’ categories could hide some of the cooperation. A woman grinding baobab with a child on her lap could be recorded as processing food. She was also caring for a child. At the digging site, an older child holding the baby freed the mother to dig. Sharing the work had increased the amount of food available before anyone divided it.[5]

Older women also spent long hours digging. In a small follow-up study of families with a nursing infant, the youngest weaned children tended to gain more weight when a grandmother spent longer foraging, after accounting for their mothers’ foraging time. The researchers included some older women who supported children beyond their direct descendants. The comparison suggests one way women can contribute to a child’s growth long after they have stopped bearing children themselves.[5]

Judith Burkart and colleagues compared fifteen primate species. Of the factors they examined, the best predictor of spontaneous help for another individual was the extent to which others helped mothers care for young. In both the field observations and the cross-species comparison, the demands of raising young help explain why cooperation might develop.[6]

The Hadza case gives a precise meaning to the claim about energy. Tubers of the same kind were available in the same ground. Another person’s care gave a mother more time to dig them. The tubers and the labor are material. The division of work, which another group can also adopt, is the relation I call Platonic. Counting only what each digger carried home would leave the caregiver’s contribution out of the explanation.[5]

Care also gives children time to learn work at which adults are already faster. Among the BaYaka, observers could see what that opportunity meant on a gathering trip.

## How old is an expert?

Gul Deniz Salali and colleagues observed a younger girl on a mushroom outing in the Republic of the Congo. She watched two experienced BaYaka gatherers choose what to pick. The two were twins, both six years old. No adult had accompanied them.[7]

The girl could see the mushrooms being selected and who knew how to select them. The twins already had enough experience to help a younger child learn. A community gains teachers in this way well before its children reach adulthood.[7]

The researchers also saw younger children waiting for their portions while an adolescent divided meat under an adult’s guidance. Here the learner had to understand who was present and what each person was entitled to. Recognizing a mushroom and dividing a kill required different judgments. Children could watch both being made.[7]

The outing supplied food and gave a novice a chance to become a gatherer. I extend *autopoiesis* to that second process when a community’s practice helps produce the next people capable of carrying it out. The observation that matters is who actually got to go, learn and try the work.[7][8]

How far back do these capacities go? Archaeological finds can date human remains, a migration route or the construction of a store. Each records a different change.

At Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, flints heated long ago helped researchers date the associated human remains to roughly 315,000 years ago. The face and teeth have features familiar in our species. The braincase combines them with an older shape. At Misliya Cave in Israel an upper jaw attributed to *Homo sapiens* has been assigned an age of 177,000 to 194,000 years, a dating that has drawn objections. Other Levantine finds also place members of our species outside Africa well before the dispersal, around 50,000 years ago, from which most living Eurasians derive most of their ancestry. A population can cross a boundary many generations before descendants of a later crossing come to occupy large parts of the world.[9][10]

Emily Hallett, Michela Leonardi and colleagues found that, beginning around 70,000 years ago, people in Africa lived in a wider range of habitats, including forests and dry regions. Rainfall also affected which routes out of Africa were passable. During the long interval between the earliest remains of our species and its major dispersal, people were acquiring ways to live in new environments within Africa.[10]

Around twelve thousand years ago some communities began the changes we now group together as the transition to agriculture. At Dhraʿ in the Jordan Valley people were storing grain above the ground on specially built floors before there is clear local evidence for domesticated crops. The space beneath the floor protected what was above it. A store put part of one season’s harvest within reach of people in another. That delay created a new decision, when to release the food. The excavated floors tell us that people had acquired the means to make it. They leave open who had the right to decide.[11]

## Back at the feast

Across the border from Lee’s Christmas gathering, the Nyae Nyae Farmers Cooperative in Namibia later debated whether to permit hunting with guns and dogs. After considerable discussion it turned the request down. One member feared that too little game would survive. Another favored permission with limits on where and how much could be taken. We have the disagreement because people thought it worth listening to both opinions. They were choosing what kind of place their children would have to make a living in.[2]

A more effective way to kill game can increase the food available this season and reduce it for the next. The Nyae Nyae members considered what would happen to the animals and who could use them. A comparison of energy extraction must also take account of the time over which food remains available and who receives it. One successful hunt cannot establish a long-term increase.

Lee’s hosts used concerted ridicule to put a limit on the authority that could come with giving away meat. The Nyae Nyae members later exercised collective authority over which hunting methods an individual might use. Each case makes a different part of the dialectic visible. People who associate to secure food acquire the power to regulate the people and tools that secure it. Exercising that power changes what the next generation can learn and what its country will yield.[1][2]

Lee had bought a fat ox and knew enough about food to estimate how many people it would feed. What he had missed was the risk his generosity posed to relations among his hosts. He learned that only after the feast, when he asked Tomazo why everyone had laughed.[1]

In Lyon, the people who designed and wove Jacquard’s portrait also divided work that one expert could scarcely do alone. From the finished silk it was easy to identify the man in the chair. Finding the man who worked out how to weave the curtain took a letter to a museum.[11]
