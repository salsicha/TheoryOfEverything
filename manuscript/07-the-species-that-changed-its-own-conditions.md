# Chapter 7 — The Species That Changed Its Own Conditions

**Part III · Human Society and Collective Intelligence**

When Ben!a heard which ox Richard Lee had bought for the Christmas feast at /Xai/xai in the Kalahari, she came to see him. “What did you expect us to eat off it, the horns?” Lee had paid his Herero friend Yehave twenty pounds for the big black animal. By his reckoning there were four pounds of meat apiece for a hundred and fifty guests.[1]

Lee had been measuring what his hosts ate. He kept his own food supply so that his presence would disturb the count as little as possible. At Christmas he meant to thank them. Tomazo, a hunter he trusted, took him to see a smaller, fatter cow. Its owner declined to sell. Lee had seen meat divided and redivided for two hours while the recipients watched. By Christmas morning he still had only the black ox.[1]

Lee asked /gaugo to make the cut at the breastbone. On a thin beast the knife would soon reach bone. On the first cut /gaugo found white fat. On the second and third, more white fat. Lee reckoned it was two inches thick. When he pointed it out, /gaugo insisted the beast was a wreck. Then /gaugo laughed, and so did everyone around him. They ate for two days.[1]

Afterward /gaugo told Lee what happened when a hunter brought down a large animal. He came home quietly and waited for somebody to ask. The party that went out for the carcass disparaged it even as they cut it up to carry home. Tomazo explained what was at stake. A man who fed a camp might come to think of the others as his servants. Lee had received much the treatment a successful young hunter could expect.[1]

In physics we have to reconstruct how an enduring relation might have arisen. Here the people who made a rule binding could explain its effect to the man on whom they had just enforced it. Lee’s twenty pounds bought beef enough to feed a camp. The rule kept that purchase from buying him the right to give orders. That distinction matters to an argument about energy and human society. An ability to obtain more food can sustain the group that made it possible only if people can count on receiving some of it. A rule that constrains the successful hunter may enlarge what the people around him can do together.[1]

## Who owns the animal?

Among the Ju/’hoansi groups described by Lorna Marshall and others, the right to divide a carcass could belong to whoever owned the arrow that had brought the animal down. Hunters borrowed arrows. To find out who would make the first division of the meat, it sometimes took two questions. Who killed it? Whose arrow was it?[2]

Lending an arrow could give a person a share of the duty to feed the camp. An exceptional hunter could bring in much of its meat without acquiring the right to distribute it all.[2]

Lee also described Ju/’hoansi men in western Ngamiland who used guns borrowed from Herero or Tswana owners. Much of the meat went to the owner. Robert Hitchcock and colleagues later recorded agreements that left some hunters with very little of what they had brought down. When arrows circulated among neighbors, ownership could distribute authority. Where a few people owned the guns, it could concentrate it.[2]

The difference lies in who can count on a share after the hunt. A rule about ownership can spread the risk of coming home empty or leave the successful hunter short of meat. Both arrangements have implements, skill and a carcass. To explain what the hunt makes possible tomorrow we also need the terms on which people get fed today.[2]

Cooperation changes the conditions under which people grow up. Children learn whom to ask for food, who is entitled to divide it and what happens to someone who claims too much. Adults can also act together to stop one person from intimidating the others. That can change the advantage an aggressive individual gains from being physically strong.

Learning can change behavior within one lifetime. The hypothesis of *human self-domestication* concerns a change across generations. If differences in impulsive aggression are partly inherited, and if a community consistently affects which people with those dispositions survive and have children, then the community helps shape the evolution of its members.[3]

Richard Wrangham distinguishes reactive aggression, such as lashing out in anger, from violence people plan together. He proposes that groups among our ancestors sometimes killed habitually aggressive men. If this altered who left descendants over many generations, planned violence could have selected against impulsive violence. It is a specific, disputed claim about human evolution.[3]

There are two quite different routes from these practices to the people who will occupy a camp a generation later. A child can learn to check an impulse because acting on it costs friends. A persistent difference in who has children could also alter the frequency of inherited dispositions. The feast gives direct evidence for learning a rule, in Lee’s case within days. The stronger claim about self-domestication requires the second route. We have no record of how commonly prehistoric communities imposed lethal sanctions, and the shape of a fossil face cannot establish its owner’s temperament.[1][3][4]

One influential comparison concerns silver foxes bred for tameness. Researchers proposed that changes in the face, ears and coat might be linked through embryonic cells that contribute to several tissues. Kathryn Lord and colleagues examined the history of the fur trade. Some traits credited to the experiment were already present in the farm stock. For humans there is another possible explanation for some of the differences. Dor Shilton and colleagues emphasize that children learn when to express anger, when to restrain it and when to act on another person’s behalf.[4]

## A pair of empty hands

Near Lake Eyasi in northern Tanzania, Kristen Hawkes, James O’Connell and Nicholas Blurton Jones followed Hadza women digging for tubers. The women tapped the rocky ground around a woody stalk to judge what lay beneath. Some holes ran more than a metre deep. A nursing mother sometimes asked an older child to hold her infant while she dug. Just past midday the women gathered to roast the tubers. The child who had freed a digger’s hands could come to the fire carrying none.[5]

When a woman ground baobab with a child on her lap, how should the observers count that time? Food processing and childcare could each quite properly claim the whole interval. Weighing the tubers at the fire could tell the researchers what each digger had brought back. To learn what another pair of hands had contributed, they had to follow the women out to the digging ground.[5]

Older women also spent long hours digging. In a small follow-up study of families with a nursing infant, the youngest weaned children tended to gain more weight when a grandmother spent longer foraging, after accounting for their mothers’ foraging time. The researchers included some older women who supported children beyond their direct descendants. The comparison suggests one way women can contribute to a child’s growth long after they have stopped bearing children themselves.[5]

Judith Burkart and colleagues compared fifteen primate species. Of the factors they examined, the best predictor of spontaneous help for another individual was the extent to which others helped mothers care for young. In both the field observations and the cross-species comparison, the demands of raising young help explain why cooperation might develop.[6]

A tuber a metre underground is an opportunity a person can have without being free to take it. The older child holding the infant changes that fact as surely as a better digging tool could. This gives cooperation a causal place in an account of energetic gain. Time spent caring can make food accessible to the party even when the caregiver brings no food back. To learn whether the arrangement can recur, follow the food to the people doing the caring as well as the people doing the digging. The tubers and the labor are material. The division of labor, which another group can adopt, is the relation I call Platonic.[5]

## How old is an expert?

Gul Deniz Salali and colleagues observed a younger girl on a mushroom outing in the Republic of the Congo. She watched two experienced BaYaka gatherers choose what to pick. The two were twins, both six years old. No adult had accompanied them.[7]

From where the younger girl walked she could see which mushrooms her companions picked and which they passed. A portion delivered to her in camp would have fed her just as well that day. On the outing she could start learning to find her own.[7]

The researchers also saw younger children waiting for their portions while an adolescent divided meat under an adult’s guidance. Here the learner had to understand who was present and what each person was entitled to. Recognizing a mushroom and dividing a kill required different judgments. Children could watch both being made.[7]

The outing supplied food and gave a novice a chance to become a gatherer. I extend *autopoiesis* to that second process when a community’s practice helps produce the next people capable of carrying it out. The observation that matters is who actually got to go, learn and try the work.[7][8]

How far back do these capacities go? Archaeological finds can date human remains, a migration route or the construction of a store. Each records a different change.

At Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, the remains include faces and teeth recognizably like ours and braincases whose shape retains older features. In the same deposits researchers found flints that had been heated in antiquity. Heating can empty stores of energy trapped in a mineral. Radiation from the surroundings then builds up a new store over time. By measuring the energy released on reheating a flint, the investigators could estimate when it last saw a fire. The associated human remains are roughly 315,000 years old. It is a date for people with particular features. It tells us very little about who held a baby while its mother worked.[9]

At Misliya Cave in Israel an upper jaw attributed to *Homo sapiens* has been assigned an age of 177,000 to 194,000 years, although the dating is disputed. Other Levantine finds also place our species outside Africa long before the dispersal around 50,000 years ago from which most living Eurasians derive most of their ancestry. Someone crossing a boundary, and a population growing numerous enough to become ancestral to millions of people on the other side, can be events many generations apart.[9][10]

Emily Hallett, Michela Leonardi and colleagues found that, beginning around 70,000 years ago, people in Africa lived in a wider range of habitats, including forests and dry regions. Rainfall also affected which routes out of Africa were passable. During the long interval between the earliest remains of our species and its major dispersal, people were acquiring ways to live in new environments within Africa.[10]

About twelve thousand years ago some communities began the transition to agriculture. At Dhraʿ in the Jordan Valley people kept grain on raised floors before there is clear local evidence of domesticated crops. The store put one season’s harvest within reach in another. Someone could now decide when to release the grain. The floors survive. Who made that decision remains open.[11]

Dates so widely separated are difficult to reconcile with the idea that a single anatomical change conferred an adult’s whole way of life. A child with the capacity to learn still needs somebody who can show her which mushrooms to take. Storing grain changes when she can eat without teaching her anything about a mushroom. These are distinct additions to what people can do. Making one helps explain why an animal like us can enter an environment its ancestors could scarcely use, even while the rate of genetic change remains an open question.[7][9][10][11]

## Back at the feast

Across the border in Namibia, the Nyae Nyae Farmers Cooperative later debated whether to permit hunting with guns and dogs. One member feared that too little game would survive. Another favored permission with limits on where and how much could be taken. After discussion the cooperative turned down the request.[2]

A rule favoring the largest kill today would have given a different answer. The vote exposes a choice about the interval over which a gain matters and the people to whom it will accrue. A community can create a capacity to secure food and also decide to limit its use so others can hunt later. Such decisions change what an individual hunter stands to gain from his skill. They can alter conduct within days. Whether the same process changed inherited human dispositions across millennia remains the separate wager of self-domestication.[1][2][3]

Lee still had a question for Tomazo. Why had his friend never told him how people spoke to a successful hunter? “Because you never asked me.”[1]
