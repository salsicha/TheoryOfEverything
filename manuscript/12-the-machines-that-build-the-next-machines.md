# Chapter 12 — The Machines That Build the Next Machines

A little over half a second into a discharge in Lausanne, a camera looked into the vessel of a fusion experiment and caught two separate bodies of plasma. The physicists called them droplets. You can see them both in the published photograph. The controller that kept them there for the picture had to do its work without that view.[1]

The controller had learned its part on a simulation. On the Swiss machine, called TCV, it received magnetic and electrical readings ten thousand times a second and sent voltage commands to the coils. The researchers had given it a distinctly odd job. Hold the two plasmas in position while raising the current in each. They left it latitude over what shape each could take along the way. In the control interval allotted to it, a fifth of a second, there was time for about two thousand rounds of measurement and response. When that interval was over the two plasmas were still there.[1]

Suppose someone had written every voltage command down in advance. What would happen when the plasma moved a little farther than expected on the first one? On TCV, the movement after the first command could change the second. The experimenters had a further way to check what had happened. Anyone who doubted whether there had really been two droplets could look at the photograph.

The pulse drew power. What it returned was knowledge of how to hold a difficult shape. Someone still had to supply electricity for the next shot. Between holding those droplets and powering the next machine lies most of what we need to understand about machines that might help build their successors.

## Where to put the memory

In 2021 a team of chip designers and machine-learning researchers at Google described a shorter journey around that circle. They had used a learning system in the design of a processor made to run machine learning. Anna Goldie, one of the researchers, reported that some of the layouts had gone into a chip that had already been manufactured. Here, at least, a choice made on a computer had come back from a factory in silicon.[2]

The job concerned a deceptively modest question. Where on the chip should a block of memory go? There are other blocks it has to talk to, smaller components that must fit around it, and wires that must find their way to all of them. Move two close neighbors a little closer and their connection gets shorter. You may also have closed the only convenient passage for a crowd of other wires. A circuit diagram can specify all the right connections while leaving its designer weeks of work to find out where to put things.[2]

The team gave the learning system a representation of what connected to what. It placed the large blocks one at a time. A placement used some of the available area and changed what would fit in the rest. After a round, the system could be scored on estimates of such things as wire length and congestion. Engineers then took layouts through more detailed design tools that placed smaller components and evaluated how the resulting design would perform. The quickest score was cheap enough to teach with. The slower checks asked questions the quick score could miss.[2]

Think of Carquillat bringing out the light in the curtain behind Jacquard. The chain of punched cards for the portrait could pass from hand to hand. In principle another weaver equipped with the cards, loom, silk and skill could make the light come through again. A chip layout is a description with a destination every bit as particular. The position of a memory block in a file acquires its consequences when a factory puts matter in the places the file specifies. In the woven portrait a person can inspect the finished surface for a misplaced thread. In silicon, one consequence of an awkward choice may be a signal that arrives too late. To find it takes another act of engineering.[2][3]

Google later reported using the method across three generations of its machine-learning processors. On other chip designs, Chung-Kuan Cheng and his colleagues compared the public version of the placement tool with alternatives. Two other tools consistently produced shorter routed wires on the modern designs they examined. They were working with different circuits and training conditions. We can believe that a learned placement made its way into manufactured silicon and still want to know who else could have done the job, with how much wire, time, electricity and effort. A company can answer the first question by ordering a chip. Nobody can answer the second by holding the chip up to the light.[2][4]

A machine has now helped make a consequential choice about one of the machines that can run a learning system. There is a temptation to shorten that sentence until the machine has built itself. Consider who would go missing. Someone specified the calculations the new chip had to support. Someone decided how much delay on a connection was tolerable, paid the factory and took the risk of ordering a part that might disappoint. Even a working chip, delivered in its box, is waiting for a great deal more of the world to show up.

## The distance between two chips

Why does so much of that world end up under one roof? A small trained model can do useful work on a device at the point of use. An undertaking that divides a large calculation among thousands of chips has an additional expense. The chips have to tell one another what they have done. If enough of them must wait for the last answer before the next step begins, an improvement in arithmetic can be spent entirely on waiting.

Consider an actual machine. Google's fourth-generation Tensor Processing Unit supercomputer linked 4,096 chips. Its designers included optical switches that could change the paths along which groups of chips communicated. They spent engineering effort on changing who was next to whom in the network, even though the chips had already been installed. The floorplanner's question had turned up again at the scale of a building.[5]

My argument about data centers starts with this cost of cooperation. A site with plentiful electricity can run many chips at once. A network can let them share one piece of work closely enough to finish it. The waste heat from all those useful operations has to be taken somewhere else every hour of the run. Arrange those provisions once, employ people who can keep them working, and another research group may be able to attempt a calculation it could never have equipped a room of its own to do. A building that needed an exceptional project to justify it can, once it exists, make less exceptional projects affordable. Some of those projects will fail. They have to be paid for on much the same terms.[5]

There was a pile of poor coal beside our first colliery engine. The cheap fuel bought the engine time to be wasteful at a place where getting the water out mattered more. A facility with power, cooling and skilled operators can carry an uncertain first design through several failures for the same material reason. Somebody has the means to carry the cost until there is an improvement worth keeping. One of the important distinctions in both stories is who can afford to find out.[6]

This is the sense in which I think artificial cognition and the data center arise naturally. Once a society can put part of the work of making a choice into a machine, the plant that supplies the machine becomes a possible object of its choices. Physical constraints narrow where that work can be done usefully. A placement system inherits the circuit it must fit. A training run inherits the distance it can afford between chips. The case for concentration is strongest where coordination and shared equipment buy something that smaller, dispersed installations cannot. A task that fits on one device and has to respond to events beside it offers a reason to go the other way. A particular address, owner and size of building all have histories in addition to this physical one.

An established operator also possesses advantages that may have little to do with the merits of the next design. The power connection is there. The staff know this equipment. A second user can find other people who have learned the same tools. W. Brian Arthur showed in a model of competing technologies how adoption itself can tip later choices toward the route that got ahead early. An efficient new design can have difficulty getting the first order that would give someone a chance to learn how to run it. Counting the number of halls built by the winner will tell us little about what the runner-up would have cost to cool.[7]

## What the cold was worth

In 2018 a data center operator named Dan Fuenffinger described something that had surprised him. A controller at Google had learned to take advantage of winter weather to produce colder water than the facility usually used. The waste heat had somewhere colder to go. Electricity still came in on the same wires. What changed was how much of it the plant spent carrying heat away. One can look at a data center from a long way away and see a placeless service. You can tell from this particular decision that the building had weather.[8]

An earlier version of the system recommended actions to people. The operators had picked up a practice from it. Sometimes it paid to spread the cooling load over more pieces of equipment. The trouble was that somebody had to keep carrying out recommendations. Staff asked whether the system could take on that work as well. In the later arrangement, every five minutes the system received sensor readings and estimated the effect of different actions. The proposed change had to pass checks in the computing service and again at the site. Operators could take the plant back to its ordinary controls. Besides the route that carried a command into the building, there was a route by which a person in the building could withdraw authority to give it.[8]

Somewhere between the fifth of a second at Lausanne and those five minutes, the question changed. In a plasma the next reading can tell the controller whether a correction is due. In the cooling plant, the next reading might do that too. It takes much longer to discover whether running the plant this way has made it cheaper to cool a year's worth of computing. Google published a graph covering nine months. It compared power drawn per unit of cooling delivered with the facility's historical performance. By the end the reported improvement was around thirty percent. You could believe every point on that graph and still have no idea how much of the next computer it paid for.[8]

William West could have recognized the difficulty. At Fowey Consols, both sealed counters faithfully recorded the strokes of his engine while a broken pump bucket had put one lift out of action. The committee had to deduct work the instrument had registered and the mine had never received. West's extraordinary day's trial also exceeded his engine's ordinary monthly figure. In Cornwall they had learned at some expense to distinguish a stroke, a lift of water and a month of keeping the workings dry.[6]

What is the lift of water here? The company reported electrical power per unit of cooling. To find out what it cost to deliver a piece of computing, we would need to know how much useful work the servers did while the plant cooled them. A winter that makes cold water cheap, a hall only half occupied and a change of workload can all alter the bill. Then there is the equipment, the electricity and the labor it took to obtain the saving. For a fair account, the hours of cooling on ordinary settings, when an operator took the controls back, would have a place on the page too. The old committee would know what to ask about a figure from which inconvenient hours had been excused.

All this could come out well. Let us say the revised controls free enough of a fixed electricity supply to run another rack through the summer. That would open a use of energy the operators previously could not sustain. It is possible to improve efficiency per calculation and use more electricity across the site as further work comes in. It is equally possible for the saving to finish its career as a smaller electricity bill. To discover which occurred you would have to follow the saving out of the engineering department. There is no line on a pump gauge that says who may spend it.

## Who gets the next shift?

Suppose the company commissions the rack. It arrives on a truck. Someone connects the power and the network, tests it under load and takes responsibility for it overnight. Suppose one of the jobs it runs helps to reduce failures in the cooling plant that keeps the rack in service. Now there is something worth calling self-production. A portion of what the organization can do has gone back into producing its ability to do it again. Change the destination of the saving and a different history follows. People may quite reasonably want to spend it on a less punishing shift or a cheaper service. The cooling plant would have saved exactly as much electricity either way.

I use the word *autopoiesis* for this proposed return in the larger organization. Choose the boundary too narrowly and a model appears to manufacture its own chips. Draw it too generously and every purchase and sale counts as evidence that the world keeps itself going. In the example we can name the people who repair the cooling plant, the capacity that pays for them and the work they make possible in return. We can equally well name the electricity, spare parts and new workers that still have to come in. What recurs is an ability to carry out a task, despite the quite ordinary fact that the components that first made it possible wear out.[9]

An arriving train on the South Devon line told a passenger that the pressure difference across the piston had done its work. To decide how to send tomorrow’s train, the directors also needed to know what it cost to seal the pipe and pump the air out again. Our engineer has the same reason to keep a second set of figures. How much electricity did the controller save on a hot afternoon? Over the life of the plant, did the saving cover what it cost to run the controller and keep the sensors trustworthy? If enough came back to equip another hall, we can begin to see why there might be two of them. To learn whether the hall approaches a maximum rate of entropy production, we would still have to measure the energy flows and compare the alternatives that could run on the same supply.[6][9]

There is a dialectical turn in that winter episode. A program could suggest an adjustment because operators had made measurements, set limits and kept records. Its first success created a new claim on those same people's time. Their answer preserved their authority to judge a recommendation while changing who had to execute each one. Once adjustments could pass reliable checks at the site, the operators could use the learned recommendations round the clock without having to carry out each one by hand. In the chip case the relationship goes one turn further. A result of calculation can enter the physical design of the equipment on which later calculations will depend. That is a development worth explaining even if each succeeding chip takes longer to qualify than the last.[2][8]

What is a saved hour worth to the person who used to spend it making adjustments? Chapter 9 opened with a weaver who had done the work and was still forty francs short. A working controller, like a finished piece of silk, settles very little about who will get paid. A worker who has acquired authority to stop a plant may have gained a skill and a voice. Another might have helped train a system and lost the paid hours on which a household depended. Share the gain through wages, shorter hours, a public dividend or cheaper service, and it supports different lives. Each choice requires an institution capable of delivering on it. Even the cleverest placement of a memory block leaves every one of those arrangements to be made by people.[10]

There is a question about origins as well as destinations here. Earlier I asked whether some relations we call constants could themselves be the record of a physical assembly. A manufactured chip supplies no measurement of how those constants arose. It does give an ordinary instance of something that would otherwise be hard to picture. For a program running years from now, the time a signal takes to cross that chip will be a given. For the people and machines who laid it out, the distance the signal has to travel was once a choice. A world that inherits conditions can acquire some purchase on how the next world will inherit them.

Our engineer can bring the records from the older hall to the building we left in the prologue. She knows which row got too hot. She also knows whom to ask about the coldest days of the year. If the machines can use any of that to suggest a better design, she has some idea now how she would judge it. For the moment the racks stand in their places, waiting for the power to come on.
