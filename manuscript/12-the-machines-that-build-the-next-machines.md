# Chapter 12 — The Machines That Build the Next Machines

In Mäntsälä, Finland, Jani Laine and his colleagues came across two accounts of what a data center did for the town. One belonged to the servers, which used more electricity in July than in January 2016. The other belonged to the municipal heating company. Its heat pump delivered 1,042 megawatt-hours of heat in January and 752 in July. The place that paid to run the computers and the place where their warmth acquired a customer kept different books.[1]

Air leaving the server rooms passed through heat exchangers. The municipal company ran a heat pump to raise the temperature high enough for the district heating network. Either side of that transaction had existed elsewhere without the other. In Mäntsälä, bringing them together changed what could be sold. Every calculation still ended by releasing heat. Some of that heat could now go to work a second time.[1]

The site in Mäntsälä was chosen in part because the heat could be used nearby. At an English colliery, low-value fuel at the pithead had been worth burning because the water to be lifted and the coal beneath it were on hand. In Finland a supply of lukewarm air acquired a customer because the town already had a network that could carry heat away. In each place a relation among things already built created an opportunity peculiar to the site.[1][2]

There is a further possibility the town’s meters leave open. Some of the work done *inside* a hall can go into building a machine on which people will do the next round of computing. In one instance we can follow a choice made by a learning system all the way to a manufactured chip. It helps to begin with a much shorter trip from a decision to its consequence, beside a plasma vessel in Lausanne.

## A photograph taken half a second in

In a fusion laboratory in Lausanne, a camera caught two separate bodies of plasma inside the same vessel. They appear one above the other in the photograph. The physicists called them droplets. The exposure was taken a little over half a second into the discharge. The controller responsible for holding them there had to manage without a camera’s view of what it was doing.[3]

The controller had learned in simulation. On the machine, called TCV, it received magnetic and electrical measurements and sent commands to coils. The task was to keep the two plasmas in position while the current in each rose. The researchers allowed their shapes to change along the way. Ten thousand times a second the controller could use what had happened since its last decision. It held the droplets for the full fifth of a second it had been given. That brief interval left room for about two thousand occasions on which reality could decline to go along with the preceding instruction.[3]

Jonas Degrave and his colleagues published the photograph alongside the control record. The camera caught the two droplets at 0.55 seconds. The instrument directing the coils had access to magnetic and electrical readings, and the currents in the coils were what it could change. Training had put a repertoire of responses within reach. Which sequence was actually used came to depend on the plasma itself. A useful configuration that had been available on a computer in simulation could now be produced in the vessel.[3]

The apparatus drew energy to make the discharge. What the laboratory got back was knowledge of how to hold a difficult configuration. The picture may give someone an excellent reason to organize another shot. The next shot will still require an outside supply of electricity. There is a long way to travel from a machine that can guide a physical process to a process whose return can help build another machine. In chip design a portion of that journey has already been made.

## Somewhere to put a memory

Azalia Mirhoseini and Anna Goldie had to find room for two things on the same piece of silicon. Large blocks of circuitry took up space, and the wires connecting them had to get through the spaces left over. Placing related blocks close together shortened one journey while sometimes obstructing another. A diagram naming every connection still left much of a chip’s eventual performance undecided.[4]

Azalia Mirhoseini, Anna Goldie and their colleagues trained a system to place large blocks on a chip one at a time. It was given the connections a circuit required. Each block it set down changed the spaces still available. It received a score based in part on estimates of wire length and congestion. More detailed design software could then place the small components, work out where wires would actually run and evaluate the result. The quick estimate made it affordable to try many arrangements. Once a design might be worth manufacturing, people had an excellent reason to spend more time finding out where the estimate had misled them.[4]

In 2021 Goldie reported that layouts produced with the method had gone into a chip that had already been manufactured. The interesting detail is what the chip was for. It was a processor intended for machine learning. By 2024 Google was reporting that the approach had contributed designs to three successive generations of its processors. Some of the choices that had once been possible moves in a search had gone to a factory, and the factory had returned parts on which later searches could run.[4]

Moulin could mark a square on paper in Lyon and have his decision arrive in another room as the selection of a warp thread. A chip designer could move a block in a file and have the decision return as a physical distance a signal must travel. This is what it means for an assembly to be jointly material and Platonic. The relation can travel from paper to cards or from a file to a factory. Its consequence appears in a specific material, as light showing through a woven curtain or as a signal that arrives in time for the next operation.[4][5]

Chung-Kuan Cheng and his colleagues compared a publicly released version of the placement system with other methods. In an updated assessment they fine-tuned the released model and compared it with a search method called simulated annealing. On seven of nine cases the older method gave shorter total lengths of routed wire. On six of the same nine, the learned method did better on a measure of how badly paths missed their timing requirements. On those chips both teams could have measured perfectly and still given different answers to the question of which layout was better. Length mattered. So did whether a signal arrived on time.[6]

The factory order and the independent comparison answer different historical questions. Goldie’s processor establishes that a useful design had gone into manufacture. Cheng’s results identify distinct constraints under which an alternative could outperform the learning method. Both matter to a theory of creation. It must explain why a previously unavailable way of designing chips acquired a use, and why it need not displace every other way of doing the job.[4][6]

Goldie’s manufactured processor settles one part of the question about a machine building its successor. A machine’s choice of a layout acquired physical consequences for another machine. People specified the operations the chip ought to perform, set the acceptable delays, supplied a factory and authorized production. Locating the work of invention among those participants gives a better account of what became possible. A layout once chosen only by people could now be proposed by a learning system and tested against the demands of a real chip.[4][6]

## Moving a neighbor with a mirror

At the next scale up, chip designers meet a familiar irritation. A chip can finish its arithmetic only to spend the time it saved waiting for another chip. When thousands share a job and the next stage needs all their answers, where the messages travel can matter about as much as how fast the chips add. It is possible to buy a faster calculator and spend the dividend in the queue for its neighbors.[7]

Google’s TPU v4 supercomputer offered an ingenious response. There were 4,096 chips. Optical switches used small movable mirrors to change the paths along which groups of chips communicated. The machines were bolted into place. With a turn of a mirror they could acquire different neighbors for the purposes of the job. One would learn very little about that kind of distance by asking a surveyor to measure the length of the aisle.[7]

That, I think, is one reason data centers arise naturally in this history. Power can be brought to one place, cooling installed, a network arranged, and the costs shared among undertakings that could scarcely afford to provide all four walls for themselves. Once built, a hall can give a second group a vastly cheaper first attempt. It may be doing for that group what a heap of cheap coal once did for an experimental pump. It can buy people the opportunity to be inefficient until they find out how to be efficient.[2][7]

The conditions also give us a reason to expect exceptions. If a job fits on a device beside the thing being measured, sending its readings somewhere else might add a journey to a task whose whole value lies in rapid response. It takes unusually close cooperation among machines, or expensive equipment worth sharing, to make a hall full of them pay. Once a particular site has the connection, the staff and the customers, it acquires another advantage. The next firm can buy services from people already there. W. Brian Arthur showed in a model of competing technologies how an early lead could change the rewards available to later adopters. A city full of one firm’s buildings would tell us something about its history. It would take more to show that its last building was the best anybody could have built on that supply of electricity.[7][8]

## The winter operator

In 2018 Dan Fuenffinger, a data center operator at Google, described watching a cooling system do something he had not expected. It took advantage of winter conditions to make colder water than the facility usually used. On the face of it, asking a cooling plant for colder water sounds like a good way to get a larger bill. The outside air offered a different bargain in winter. Under the conditions Fuenffinger described, the plant used less energy for cooling. Someone reading the instructions in July would have needed to know when they had been tried.[9]

As the team tells it, the original program made recommendations to operators. One thing they learned was that it could pay to spread a cooling load over more pieces of equipment. They also pointed out a defect in the proposal. The better the recommendations, the more hours somebody might spend putting them into effect. They asked to have the system carry out some of its own instructions. In the later arrangement, sensor readings went to the service every five minutes. A proposed adjustment had to clear checks there and again at the site. The operators could return the plant to its ordinary controls. They had gained a new means of intervening as well as a means of having fewer interventions to make.[9]

There is a dialectic here that the people on shift had every reason to take seriously. A system built from their records assumed that an operator would act when it found something worth doing. The more often it succeeded, the more demands it made on the very people it was supposed to assist. They could overcome that limit by handing over some authority, provided they retained the authority to take it back. Once enough decisions could pass the local checks, a different organization of the shift became possible. A system for giving advice had become part of the equipment that actually ran the building. The workers who had taught it about the plant had also altered the conditions under which it could give the next lesson.[9]

What had the alteration bought? Google published nine months of cooling performance against the plant’s historical baseline. The measure was electrical power per unit of cooling. The reported improvement rose from about twelve percent at the start of autonomous operation to about thirty percent. To get from there to the cost of a year’s useful computation, there are some further entries one might like to see. What work were the servers doing? How hot was the weather? What did it cost to run the controller and keep the sensors in order? The graph records one real kind of gain. It is silent on whether that gain paid for another rack.[9]

At Fowey Consols in Cornwall, the witnesses deducted three hours of work for the lift whose bucket broke. Two sealed counters had faithfully gone on recording every stroke. In Devon the directors had to decide whether the expense of resealing a pipe made it worthwhile to keep moving passengers by atmospheric pressure. Fuenffinger’s winter observation belongs in that company. A way to produce colder water at lower cost was a new option for operating a building. The company published a cooling measure. The figure needed to price an additional year of useful computing would be a different one.[2][9]

## Where the saving went

Jani Laine and his colleagues followed a portion of the server heat as far as the municipal network. They found that the emissions attributed to the arrangement depended on what source of heat it displaced and where the electricity to run the heat pump came from. The physical connection had created a use for what the servers formerly rejected. Its effect beyond the town depended on what happened in the other places that supplied the power or had supplied the heat.[1]

The strongest evidence here for further creation comes from the chips. A company sent a design to a factory and received a processor intended for machine learning. It later identified blocks in three generations of processors to which the placement method had contributed. Here is something more specific than a saving that might someday be put to use. Some of the work performed on one generation of equipment had already entered the physical design of another. The independent comparison also shows why the path remained open to rivals. On several circuits an older method still gave the shorter wires.[4][6]

This is why I expect powerful computers and concentrated facilities to emerge naturally in a society able to supply them. Once a society can use computation to improve the machinery that does the computing, the work accessible to a later generation changes. Some of the return can pay for more such attempts. That last step explains why the arrangement might recur. The creative step was finding a layout, a cooling decision or a use for heat that had previously been out of reach. A claim that these arrangements maximize the rate of entropy production would also need physical accounts of the competing ways the same power could have been used.[9][10]

There is no particular justice in an energy gradient. In Lyon the fact that Pommet had created something her employer could sell gave her a claim for forty francs. It could not tell the tribunal where to find the money. In Mäntsälä the municipal company paid to raise the temperature and sold heat its customers could use. The difference matters. A physical return opens possibilities. Ownership and obligations help decide which of them anyone gets to pursue.[1][11]

This leaves us with a relation that runs all the way back to the book’s most speculative question. Someone choosing a chip layout can still move a block. Years later, to a programmer waiting for a signal to cross the chip, the travel time is a fact about the world. We have the unusual privilege in this instance of seeing both sides of the history by which a choice became a constraint. In chapter 6 I asked whether some of the laws and constants on which all our calculations depend could themselves be the durable results of a physical history. We have no factory record for the universe. The experience of building a successor does let us put a sharper question to it. Which relations we now have to take as given might once have been outcomes, and what traces of that earlier freedom could they still carry?[10]

At the bottom of a silk in a French museum there are separate credits for painting, drawing the pattern and weaving. Behind them stands something rarer in a history of invention, the receipt showing who paid to have the next picture made. It bears the name of a man whose part in weaving the first picture had gone uncredited.[5]
