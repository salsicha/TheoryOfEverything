# Chapter 11 — Why the World Builds Artificial Intelligence

Charles Babbage offered to do a piece of mathematics for Ada Lovelace to save her some trouble. She sent it back. He had made what he later called a “grave mistake.” Babbage tells the story on himself in his memoir. He remembers whose idea it was to spare whom the work. He leaves out which bit of the mathematics he got wrong.[1]

The calculation was for his proposed Analytical Engine. In 1843 Lovelace published a table showing how it could produce one of a sequence called the Bernoulli numbers. You can follow the first three moves without knowing what a Bernoulli number is. Take a number, double it, and put the result in three places. Subtract one from the first copy. Add one to the second. Leave the third alone. Three columns that a moment ago held exactly the same number now have different futures. Lovelace had to work out where every quantity would be when the Engine needed it again.[2]

She took some of her language from the loom. One set of cards would tell the Engine which operation to perform. Another would tell it where to find its numbers and where to put what came back. Reading her table is a little like following Moulin across the squares from which Carquillat would weave Jacquard’s face. A person had made a chain of choices legible to a piece of machinery. Much of the Analytical Engine existed only in designs. Yet a mistake could travel in two directions before a single number passed through the complete machine. A wrong step on paper could go forward into the cards. A reader who understood what the calculation was *for* could send it back to its inventor.[1][2]

While the new building waits for its power connection, imagine the engineer from the prologue visiting a hall that has been running for years. Something went wrong with its cooling last summer. She would like to understand it before she signs another set of plans. At her desk she can consult a language model already running on someone else's equipment. For the moment her hands are in her lap. The model has every capacity it will have a second from now. None of it is at work on her question.

## What the first answer already cost

Why would anyone build such an arrangement? Consider what the engineer already has. People have learned to make chips and thermometers, to carry electricity over a distance, to keep records of faults, to describe repairs so somebody else can perform them. A great deal of experience can reach her as writing. She has one afternoon, and the fact she needs might be on any of a thousand pages. A machine that could learn relationships across many examples, then put a relevant distinction in front of one reader at the right time, would give that reader a new way to spend the afternoon. Whether that saves any electricity will depend on what she does next.

There is a considerable difference between making such a machine and punching the answer into cards. Suppose you knew the answer to every question the engineer might ask. You could write each answer down, but a fair part of your undertaking would already be over. What learning offers is the possibility of shaping a capacity on cases you have and using it on a case for which nobody has written the answer. One common training task is to give a model part of a passage and ask it to predict what comes next. The next piece of text supplies a correction. Training adjusts numerical values called *weights*, so that the difference between a prediction and what followed influences later predictions. The choice of passages, the architecture and the kinds of correction its builders reward all narrow the search. A test based on familiar examples can also make a useful distinction and a lucky shortcut look equally good.[3]

The weights have to be stored somewhere. The operations that use them have to happen in matter. Which stored values enter a calculation and in what relation to one another is the Platonic side of the assembly in this book’s sense. Put a set of learned weights into a file and carry it across the street. You have carried an achievement that another team need never repeat. To get so much as a comma out of it on the far side takes a compatible program, working equipment and electricity. The physical cost depends on how the operations are realized.[3][4]

People have to pay for that first learning interval while the file does not yet exist. The situation would have been familiar to the owner of the flooded shaft. A better pump can reduce the coal a mine will eventually consume. A cartload from a dry seam lets someone fire the pump today. For a learning system, finding a better procedure and assembling enough machines to run a costly one are likewise two different achievements. An energy supply and a design do not become artificial cognition simply by occupying the same building. Training has to produce a capability someone can use. The proposed next level of assembly is the engineer working with that capability to do something neither the unaided reader nor the powered racks could manage on their own.

She types. In ordinary use the weights stay fixed while the program generates a response. An initial calculation uses her request to select a first piece of text. That piece becomes available to the next step, and the step after that can use both. A future run can inherit the conversation if the service saves it and puts it back into the input. A later training session could change the weights themselves. You can see why the distinctions matter if you have ever explained a mistake at length to a machine that seemed to grasp every word, then begun a fresh conversation and had to explain the whole thing again.[3]

A program could call the same model every time a temperature changes. It could let a response request a reading and feed the reading back for another round. Researchers have built systems that alternate generating a proposed action, taking it through an available tool, and using the observation to decide what to do next. Once the loop is running, the engineer can take her hands off the keyboard. Someone has supplied a reason to start, a means of continuing and a condition for stopping. What looks like initiative from the other side of the desk is a good deal of organization, all of which somebody can forget to unplug. Nothing about this arrangement settles whether the model has an experience of wanting to go on.[5]

## Wait

Before trusting an expensive question to a machine, try a cheap one. How many times does the letter *r* appear in *raspberry*?

In 2025 Niklas Muennighoff and his colleagues published a language model’s attempt. It found the first *r*. It found another at position seven. Having explained its count, it was ready to stop. The program running the model suppressed the signal that would have ended this part of the response. It inserted one word. *Wait.* The model went through the letters again. This time it picked out positions one, seven and eight. You can settle the count yourself with the tip of a pencil.[6]

It is hard to think of a more economical intervention. The word contained no information about raspberries. It didn’t say there was a mistake, still less where to find it. What it bought was another pass through material the model already had. Nor was it necessary for a person to be watching for the wrong answer. The intervention operated where the model tried to finish. It would have made a correct count wait too.[6]

How much intelligence is there in the word *Wait*? Consider the preparation that made it work. The researchers started with an already trained model. From a much larger collection they chose a thousand problems accompanied by worked reasoning. They then spent twenty-six minutes training on sixteen high-end processors. Those minutes bought the final adjustment. Somebody had already had to produce the starting model and the worked material. A four-letter cue at the end of this history could elicit an ability the same four letters written on a loom card would have done little to encourage.[6]

When an interface offers a “thinking mode,” one entirely reasonable suspicion is that somebody has changed the prompt. It can work. Researchers have improved results on certain tasks by putting worked examples in the prompt, or simply asking for a step-by-step solution. The raspberry case is a particularly handsome piece of prompt hacking, right down to the timing of the interruption. Several different investments can nevertheless lie behind the same button. In the DeepSeek-R1 work, for instance, rewards for answers that could be checked helped train reasoning behavior before a user supplied a particular problem. A service can also spend its allowance on several candidates and a check on which one to keep. A new instruction, a changed set of weights, and extra computation on the day of the request can each change the answer. Only one requires teaching the model again.[7][8]

Each actual run occupies machines for a finite time. A fixed set of weights can be used once or ten thousand times. For a given question it might pay to follow a promising line further, or to start again from an earlier point and look for an independent solution. Charlie Snell and colleagues found that which use of extra computation worked well depended partly on how difficult the problem was for the model. There is no unit marked off along a line of generated text that measures how much thinking has taken place. Grant a poor method twice the time and you may have purchased a more elaborate mistake. In tests by Jie Huang and colleagues, asking models to revisit their reasoning without outside feedback sometimes led them to give up a correct answer.[9]

A second *Wait* would still have found three letters available for counting. To see the limit more sharply, give the machine the engineer’s problem.

## Which end of the hall?

In the hall the engineer is visiting, the racks stand in an east row and a west row. She has found a brief, unwelcome rise in inlet temperature on one of two traces. The trace marked A jumped. The trace marked B stayed level. She would like the designer of the next hall to know which row ran hot. The trace names came across in a spreadsheet. The sheet that says where the sensors were installed is missing.

Let the model explain why an east row might run warmer. Let it draw up an equally good account for the west. It can suggest ways to tell the stories apart. Here is one thing it cannot do with the traces alone. Put sensor A in the east row in one building and the west row in an otherwise mirrored building. Give the model the very same sheet from both. Every number it can read agrees. The correct answers are opposite. A million continuations can still be addressed to either building. In *raspberry* the eighth letter was on the page all along. The east wall isn’t in the spreadsheet.

This is a moment at which an excellent answer might be five words long. *Where was sensor A installed?* A colleague who goes to read its identifier supplies what the next computation needs. If an independent installation record turns up, a program with access to it could do the same job. A correct guess and a traced connection would yield the same name in the report. Only one would give the crew a reason to trust it when they spend money on the next hall. A model trained to deliver a confident location every time might be easier to mark on a scorecard and rather harder to work with at a drafting table.

There is a lesson here about the kind of intelligence the larger system possesses. The model can propose a discriminating observation. The colleague can make it. The engineer can send the answer back, and a revised proposal can reach the person with authority to test it. Where a consequence travels back and changes the next action, cybernetics gives us a precise question to ask. Which mistake could this return detect? A spelling can be checked against a line of print. In a formal proof, a separate checker can reject a step the proposing program rated highly. Even a faultless deduction about the cooling system inherits whatever somebody told it about the pipes. A process that can send a proof back for revision still needs the colleague at the far end of the hall.[10]

This division of work helps explain why a society might come to build artificial cognition. The machine need never learn what it is like to be warm at the east end. If it can help a crew spot a recurring loss soon enough to change a design, it opens a way to use energy that had been going to waste. At a certain scale a difference of minutes can alter the kind of decision available. An assessment that used to come after the drawings were signed might now arrive while a line can still be moved. People have committed electricity and paid for learning in advance to put that possibility within reach. The history is material all the way through, including the moment when a distinction between two columns changes what someone builds.

Will anyone build such a capability again? The model can write a perfectly good paragraph about replacing a fan. The paragraph leaves the old bearing exactly as worn as it was. To keep the service available, people have to maintain the equipment, renew their skills and secure another supply of power. To claim *autopoiesis* for the larger organization is to say that enough of what it produces goes back into making those activities possible again. If the savings from the hall help pay the crew, replace the worn equipment or support the next round of learning, we can trace a piece of that return. The firm could equally well sell the building and take the proceeds elsewhere. Either way the file of weights can survive the sale.[11]

My hypothesis is that assembling artificial cognition is a natural development where constrained search gives people a practicable way to unlock more usable energy and some of the gain supports another round. There are two gradients in this story, and it is easy to send the bill to the wrong one. During training, the direction in which changing a weight will reduce an error is a mathematical relation. It will not turn a pump. In the productive system one can ask how much useful work becomes available under the new arrangement, for how long, and what it cost to train the model, run it and act on its advice. A higher score on problems with known answers is worth having. It is the second accounting that would give the book’s energetic argument a claim on this building.[3][11]

Lovelace could send Babbage’s calculation back because she knew enough mathematics to catch his mistake. The engineer is in a less comfortable position. The two sensor names are now on the drawing in the right places. The model has proposed a change to the cooling, and the designer is prepared to try it. There is no sheet at the back of the book showing what will happen. Someone has to authorize the test. Someone has to be there to read the meter. Until then, the proposal and every objection the machine can raise to it are all on the same side of the glass.
