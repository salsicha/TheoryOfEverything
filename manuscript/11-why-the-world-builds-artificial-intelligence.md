# Chapter 11 — Why the World Builds Artificial Intelligence

Ada Lovelace needed three copies of the same number. On the diagram she published in 1843 for Charles Babbage’s proposed Analytical Engine, the first operation doubled an input and put the result in three columns. The next two operations subtracted one from the first copy and added one to the second. With four as the input, the three eights would become seven, nine and eight. The value was the same at the start. The future of each eight depended on its address.[1]

The diagram was her account of how an engine might calculate a Bernoulli number. There was particular work to be done even after the mathematics had been settled. Each quantity had to be where a later operation expected to find it. The more faithfully a machine carried out its instructions, the further a mistake in the table could travel.[1]

According to Babbage, he had volunteered to do the Bernoulli algebra himself to save Lovelace some trouble. She sent it back for amendment. He had made a mistake serious enough to mention in his memoir, though he left out just where it was. The published table and the story concern an engine that was never completed. Before the Engine had turned a wheel, someone checking what it would be asked to do had caught its inventor in an error.[1][2]

Where had the Engine acquired its cards? Babbage had borrowed the idea from the loom, and Lovelace saw how far the resemblance went. One set could specify an operation and another where to get the numbers on which to perform it. A pattern for a curtain and a calculation about numbers had something in common that was easier to see once you could put both on cards. It was possible to settle the sequence of choices somewhere other than the room where the work would be done. Moulin did it so that another pair of hands could make black silk out of brown marks. Lovelace did it to give arithmetic an address for each of three apparently identical eights.[1]

What could such a machine contribute that hadn’t first been contributed by its makers? Lovelace thought an Engine could execute what people knew how to specify. She also thought that having to express mathematics in a form the machine could use might change what mathematicians themselves came to see. In 1950 Alan Turing took up what he called Lady Lovelace’s objection. Machines, he observed, surprised him quite often. Sometimes he assumed two points in a circuit would be at the same voltage, neglected to work it out properly, and by the time of the trial had even forgotten he had made the assumption. The machine was under no obligation to remember his shortcut.[1][3]

Lovelace had made an operation portable. Turing had described how a machine could surprise the person who specified its every operation. A third possibility opened when researchers found a way to specify *how a machine should learn*. The people who trained it could acquire a machine with a new capacity before anyone could write down an adequate rule for exercising it.

## Before anybody types

In an appendix to a 2020 paper, Tom Brown and his colleagues printed a question from their test of a language model. What is 98 plus 45? The answer they would accept was 143. They could give the model examples of additions in the text of a prompt, then ask for a fresh sum and check it by ordinary arithmetic. Lovelace had specified the operations and where to find the numbers. Brown’s team could show a trained machine what a sum looked like on the page and start finding out how much addition it could already do.[4]

The cheap lesson in this history was the one that left the evidence for the answer in the problem itself. During training, each passage of text supplied many such lessons. A program used the words so far to predict the next piece of text. What the writer had actually written supplied the check. The world’s correspondence and books had been produced for many purposes. Once collected and given this constraint, some of that earlier work could go into a machine’s capacity to continue a sentence it had never encountered.[4]

A discrepancy between prediction and example let the training program adjust numerical values called *weights*. The architecture determined which adjustments could affect later computations. The examples offered repeated chances for distinctions to earn their keep. Predicting the next piece of text rewards a model for recognizing how a correct calculation is usually written. It can also reward a model for reproducing a familiar error with admirable fluency. The arithmetic in Brown’s tests gave the researchers an independent check on what their training had bought.[4]

The learned relations can travel as a file. A numerical weight has the same place in the calculation on a second compatible machine. That is the Platonic character of the assembly in the sense used here. Its material character shows up in every training run. Operations take time, electricity and equipment. The new capability consisted in giving those resources an organization under which the examples could do work on the next problem.[4][5]

At Whitehaven there had been fuel available to run a pump before the pump could release any coal. The people who trained the language model also commanded resources in advance of the capacity they wanted. Yet nobody could have calculated the positions of Lovelace’s three eights from the price of electricity. What picked out a productive path was a relation among the things available for work. The writer had already supplied an answer to each next-token question, in some instances centuries before there was a machine to ask it.[4][5]

A trained model on storage produces no answer until something initiates a computation. An input can come from a person, a timer or another program. Shunyu Yao and his colleagues built a service that could turn a model’s requests into searches of Wikipedia. On question-answering and fact-checking tasks, the result of a search became part of the material available for the next step. The model could now acquire a fact during a run. That capability came into existence when the researchers joined it to a source of observations and gave it a way to ask.[6]

During an ordinary exchange the weights typically stay fixed. Each response can still alter the text supplied on the next step, as did each result Yao’s program retrieved. Retaining that text gives a service a record it can consult. Repeating training with new evidence can alter the weights used even in a fresh conversation. These are two different ways an answer given on Monday can affect one given on Tuesday, with different costs and different histories.[4][6]

## An extra consonant

The word was *raspberry*. On the first pass recorded in a 2025 paper, a model found the r at the start and the one in seventh place. In eighth place, according to its account, was a y. Everything needed to catch the mistake was still there in the word.[7]

At this point the model tried to finish. A piece of software intervened. It suppressed the signal that would end this part of the generation and appended *Wait*. Nobody supplied the missing letter. The model spelled the word out again, this time keeping both r’s near the end. It arrived at three. Had its first answer been correct the program would have interrupted it just the same. The intervention registered an attempt to stop. It had no special faculty for detecting an inadequate acquaintance with raspberries.[7]

How much had it cost to give that one word its effect? Niklas Muennighoff and his colleagues began with a model that had already been trained. They selected a thousand problems with worked reasoning and gave it further training on them. That part took twenty-six minutes on sixteen high-end processors. The twenty-six minutes excluded the making of the original model and of the material on which the new lessons drew. The electricity for attending to *Wait* when it finally appeared was another expenditure again. It was the cheapest-looking item in a supply chain that stretches well beyond four letters.[7]

When a service offers a “thinking mode,” the suspicion that someone has been doing a little prompt hacking is well founded in this particular case. An instruction can elicit a performance the machine was ready to give. Worked examples in the input have improved answers on some tasks. So has an invitation to work through a problem step by step. Other gains are purchased at a different date. The DeepSeek-R1 researchers, for instance, used rewards for checkable answers in training reasoning behavior before a user ever brought a new problem to the machine. A service may also run several candidates and pay for a separate check. All three investments can make the same button look good. Of the three, training is the one that changes the weights that will be used on the next request.[7][8]

A fixed set of weights leaves plenty of room for a variable bill. Every generated step occupies hardware. A longer attempt, several fresh attempts, or a check by a second system can spend quite different amounts of computation on the same question. Work by Charlie Snell and his colleagues found that the value of these choices depended in part on how hard a problem already was for the model. In other studies Jie Huang and colleagues found cases where an invitation to reconsider, without outside feedback, persuaded a model to abandon a correct answer. One ought occasionally to spare a thought for the first answer that was right and had nobody to speak up for it.[9]

The *raspberry* trace shows why a longer computation sometimes helps. The eighth letter remained on the page when the model looked again. Huang’s results show why the same instruction sometimes hurts. More time to generate a case for an answer confers no special power to judge it. And in one particularly expensive calculation on Earth, the decisive fact had never reached the people doing the checking.[7][9]

## The unit that stayed on Earth

On September 23, 1999, NASA lost contact with the Mars Climate Orbiter as it reached Mars. Among the things the investigators examined was a file produced on the ground. Small thruster firings had given the spacecraft a succession of nudges during its journey. To reconstruct the orbit, navigators needed to know how much impulse those firings had supplied. The figures delivered in the file were in pound-force seconds. The navigation calculations expected newton-seconds. To the receiving program each nudge appeared smaller than it was by a factor of about 4.45.[10]

The unit the navigators expected was in the specification. Reading that document a second time would have fortified them in an entirely reasonable belief about the intended contents of the file. What they needed to know was what the program producing the file had actually delivered. The board also found missed opportunities for checking the trajectory and resolving discrepancies. People with access to the equipment, the software and independent observations had ways to find the difference. The spacecraft was lost before that difference reached the people who could have used it.[10]

The orbiter matters to a history of machine reasoning because it puts a price on mistaking internal consistency for an independent check. Multiplying every number in the file by one unit or the other gave two physically different histories from exactly the same digits. Reading those digits more carefully could do nothing to distinguish the histories. Examining an actual transfer between the two programs could. Yao’s Wikipedia search made progress for a related reason. It let a machine bring evidence into a calculation that reasoning over its own previous words could never supply.[6][10]

The investigation board turned its diagnosis into recommendations for the Mars Polar Lander team. It wanted the people who used the ground software to sign off on its products, the transfers between programs to be checked, and independent navigators to review the team’s analysis while there was still time to act. The failure had exposed a defect in the way the organization checked itself. The proposed remedy changed what would count as a check. That movement between levels was what Darwin accomplished with his rule about evidence he was likely to forget.[10][11]

There is a stronger test of artificial self-modeling here than a machine announcing it has reconsidered. Does it recognize a recurring defect in its checks? Can it change what it consults the next time that defect matters? In the Mars record people did the diagnosing. In ReAct the researchers provided the route to outside evidence. The existence of that route is already a creative achievement. Attributing its invention to the trained model would give the wrong party credit for it.[6][10][11]

I propose that artificial cognition arises naturally when a society can direct power through equipment of this kind and has enough past work in a form machines can learn from. A physical gradient supplies usable work. A learning gradient tells a program which change to its weights would reduce an error. The first helps pay for the second. To complete the argument we must show where a new capacity to draw on the physical world comes back out. Dan Fuenffinger gave one answer at a Google data center. A system trained on the plant’s records found a way to use the cold of winter to reduce the electricity spent on cooling.[4][5][12]

The difference between Lovelace’s table and Fuenffinger’s plant is the difference between checking an answer and discovering a use for it. Algebra could settle the disposition of the three eights. In the data center, the machinery had to run and somebody had to measure what the cold water had bought. That takes the story out of the calculation and into the building.[12]
