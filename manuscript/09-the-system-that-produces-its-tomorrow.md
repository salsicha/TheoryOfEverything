# Chapter 9 — The System That Produces Its Tomorrow

Forty francs were due to Mademoiselle Pommet for work she had done in a Lyon weaving shop. She took her claim to the city's labor tribunal. The judges could pass over the question that might have occupied most of a hearing. The woman who owed her the money agreed that she owed it.[1]

In its January 1841 issue, *L’Écho des ouvriers* reported the explanation Madame Vieux gave the council. Her husband had been ill. He had died. The illness had used up her resources. She had already received most of the workshop's piece earnings in advance. The balance had been retained by a loan fund set up to help workshop heads in difficulty. Of all the people whose claims had been recognized, the one still waiting was the woman who had done the weaving. The council ordered the accounts settled and urged Pommet to give the widow more time.[1]

There the report leaves them. It says nothing about when Pommet got her forty francs. In the interval the judges asked her to allow, who would be giving credit to whom?

Carquillat’s woven portrait was two years old when this report appeared. A person looking closely at the silk can see something of the knowledge that went into weaving Jacquard’s face. No such inspection could tell when the people who made it were paid. A finished piece can leave the workshop while the weaver is still waiting, as Pommet was.[1] By the time the cloth can be sold, several people may be counting on spending the same franc. The next piece of silk depends on whose claim gets there first.

## What a workshop makes

A few cases further down the column, two idle looms came before the same council. A workshop head named Burgat had fitted them up to make shawls for a merchant named Chinard. For a while the merchant failed to supply the material. To make up for the delay, he agreed to pay Burgat fifty centimes per thousand weft insertions. Then Burgat could find no workers for the job. With his shawls delayed in turn, Chinard wanted to pay the ordinary rate. The council held that the lost time on the two sides offset each other. It awarded Burgat the fifty-centime rate for both kinds of shawl.[1]

Had you passed those looms on the right two days, you might have seen the same sight twice. On one day an invitation to a weaver would have done nothing to start them. On the other, a delivery of yarn would have been equally useless. The difference would have been plain to the person who had to get them going.

That is what I want the word *autopoiesis* to help us see in an economy. In a cell, some of the work that goes on inside the membrane makes new membrane. Widen the boundary around the loom. A workshop can send out cloth and receive enough in return to replace the silk, support the people and teach a new pair of hands. It takes in food, fuel and materials throughout. What it puts back together is the ability to weave. This is the book's extension of a biological idea to people who can bargain over the conditions for their return. The date of a delivery matters as much to that ability as the weight of the yarn. A cupboard with a week's food in it gives one household room to wait through a delay that would break another.[2]

Marx asked what else a completed round of production had put back into the world. A worker who has been paid can go home, eat, rest, help raise children, and come back capable of work. The payment also leaves the worker needing to earn another. Meanwhile somebody with a claim on the product can again buy materials and engage people to use them. Even if all the cloth is sold and its buyers carry it away, the relation between the people who control the next order and the people who need it can emerge from the transaction ready for use again. Marx called the renewal of production on the same scale *simple reproduction*. Growth is optional. Another working day is quite an achievement.[3]

Niklas Luhmann helps us notice something Marx's question leaves to be worked out. Think of the number forty passing from Pommet's claim to the widow's acknowledgment to the council's ruling. Each act of communication gives somebody else something to respond to. Now imagine that the same number was said by someone in the street recounting yesterday's hearing. Quite a different set of actions would follow. A society has ways of deciding which utterance is a judgment, which a promise, and which an interesting piece of news. Its next dealings depend on people being able to make the distinction.[4]

Cybernetics supplies a way of putting Marx and Luhmann to work on the same forty francs. A worker reports that the expected payment has failed. The report reaches a body that can require an accounting. Its decision can change who is paid and when. If payment follows and the weaver can come back, the circuit passes all the way from a material interruption through a recognized claim to a change in material activity. If she is still waiting, the message has gone round considerably faster than the money. Regulating an operation and supplying the wherewithal to keep doing it can come apart in front of the same three people.[2][4]

This is one system, jointly material and Platonic in the book's sense. The widow and the weaver could not divide a franc that neither had. Equally real in their lives was the rule determining who could ask whom to wait. Reverse the order of payment and you change whose kitchen needs to carry the debt. That relation weighs nothing in a scale with the silk. To give it effect takes witnesses, accounts, an authority willing to act, and eventually someone with the means to pay.

## A thousand crossings, a price

Ten years earlier the silk workers of Lyon had attempted a more ambitious correction. They wanted a figure that a person could consult *before* undertaking the work. Counting the length of a finished piece was a poor way to reckon how long its weaving had taken. Different fabrics called for different densities of warp and different numbers of times the weft had to cross. In October 1831 delegates worked out minimum piece rates across eight divisions of the trade. Among the units they used were a thousand warp threads and a thousand passes of the weft. What a collector might admire as the shadow of a curtain, a weaver could also see as so many movements someone had to make.[5]

The delegates carried their tables into negotiations with the merchants. The first number of the workers' paper *L’Écho de la fabrique* told its readers what had happened. On October 21 the merchants present said they lacked authority to commit their colleagues. The workers replied that they had been given authority to negotiate only with people who had it too. New merchant delegates were chosen. On October 25, in the presence of the prefect, the sides worked through the tables and signed. One can appreciate the achievement without assuming that everyone who signed meant the same thing by a signature.[5]

By November the price schedule met resistance. A stoppage became an insurrection, with deaths in the city. On December 7 the minister of war, Marshal Soult, declared the published rates void. Individual disputes could still go to the competent judges. The difference is worth the distance of ten years back to Pommet and Burgat. It is one thing to win a ruling about work you have already done. It is another to have a rate that everyone can count on when the thread first goes on the loom.[5]

Was the price of weaving what the market would bear? The delegates had to count threads, choose representatives and find people entitled to negotiate for the buyers. It took an order from a marshal to remove the resulting schedule. Once these people and their acts disappear from view, the price can look as though the cloth brought it into the world by itself. Marx used the analysis of commodities to show how a relation among people could take on the appearance of a property of things. This is what is false in an ideology that treats the prevailing price as an instruction issued by nature. The belief itself is perfectly able to do material work. A minister who holds it can put his signature to an order.[3][5]

Who has the authority to give that signature its force? A treasury has a related ability. It can tell a stranger what a piece of paper will buy.

## A promise that comes back

On December 10, 1690, the General Court of Massachusetts Bay ordered a committee to issue printed bills, up to a total of seven thousand pounds. The colony had incurred debts in war and lacked the money to settle them promptly. People to whom it owed payment for services or property could choose to take bills. The order instructed the treasurer and his subordinates to accept them at their stated values in public payments. It even provided for a bill worn out in somebody's hands. Return it and the committee would give you a fresh one with the same number and amount.[6]

Imagine being offered such a bill by a neighbor the colony has paid. You might know little about the service she rendered or the prospects of getting silver out of the treasury. If you owe a tax, there is one use for the paper you can discover in advance. Walk it to the treasurer and it will discharge your obligation. Knowing that somebody else can use it that way gives you a reason to accept it even if your own account is settled. Follow the direction of travel. The authority can declare the obligation first. It has to get the paper into somebody's hands before anybody can bring it back.[6]

*Money comes from tax. Taxes come from war.* There is a material history inside that shorthand. To field a force, someone has to obtain food, transport and people's time. A recurring levy can make that possible. Requiring payment in a specified currency gives people reason to acquire what the issuer spends, and the issuer can mobilize resources with it. Charles Tilly followed the connection between war, extraction and protection in the making of European states. He took seriously the awkward case of a protector who also creates the danger from which protection is offered. An authority can deliver safety to some of the people it taxes and use what they pay to menace others. Massachusetts described its own position in the broadside as one of defense. That tells us whose purpose the proclamation served.[6][7]

The document gives us the birth of a particular issue of money. It also gives us creditors, accounts in pounds and a shortage of money already in existence. Taxes help explain why people would want those bills. They leave plenty of work for a historian asking how pounds came to be counted there in the first place. Today a commercial bank making a loan can create a deposit in its customer's account. An enforceable obligation can give currency a use, a treasury can issue it and a bank can create a spendable balance. Keeping these operations in order tells us who can call upon somebody's labor and what happens when the claim comes home.[7]

## Who protects whom?

When William, duke of Aquitaine, asked Bishop Fulbert of Chartres to set down what fealty required, Fulbert sent him a letter. A person who undertook such an obligation should safeguard his lord's person, secrets, defenses and possessions, and give counsel and aid. Toward the end Fulbert turned the obligation around. The lord owed corresponding good faith to his vassal. Then the bishop explained why the letter was short. His city and church had burned, rebuilding occupied him, and he drew comfort from the hope of help from God and the duke. A few lines after a definition of obligation we meet a man who has a use for one.[8]

It was this reciprocal claim that first made me think, *feudalism was a labor union*. Consider who gets to say *we* in that sentence. A lord and a vassal could owe each other assistance, while the people growing the grain that supported them both might have little say in the undertaking. A union of wage earners puts a right to call for support in the hands of people who sell their work. What crosses the centuries is a question. When a person gives service or a share of earnings today, who can they call on when trouble comes? Fulbert could set down what his duke owed in good faith. His letter leaves us waiting to discover who actually paid for the rebuilding.[8][9]

In England in 1349 the Crown faced a difficulty that a statement of duties could scarcely settle. The pestilence had killed so many people that surviving laborers could ask more for their work. An order addressed to the sheriff of Kent directed that eligible workers must serve at customary wages and provided for imprisonment when they refused. Landlords were to have preference in retaining their own dependent tenants. The order discloses how the situation looked from above. If a reaper has two offers, an employer who formerly had first claim on his time suddenly has a use for the sheriff.[10]

A century later, in October 1453, Alice Chaucer divided the 387 arable acres of her demesne at Walsham in Suffolk into twenty-two parcels. Each went to a tenant owing a fixed annual rent. The demesne was the land held for the lord's own use. Put the old and new accounts next to one another. Where an estate once had to get its fields ploughed and the crop in, the lady of this manor could now look for twenty-two rents. Each tenant now had to reckon with how much grain it would take to meet the rent in a poor year. Several arrangements had been tried at Walsham in the intervening years. A royal order in 1349 and a decision by one landholder in 1453 leave a century of local history between them.[10]

Here is my claim that feudal relations failed when their closure stopped. Land and people could remain where they were while the operations that made a particular form of lordship pay ceased to renew it. A lord might enforce an obligation, reduce it to keep a tenant, or take a rent in exchange for giving up the task of running a farm. Each could preserve a source of income. Only some preserved the old relation. A failed promise of protection matters when it changes what people can do or be compelled to do next season. We should expect to find places where extra enforcement prolonged an arrangement that elsewhere gave way. Walsham gives us a change we can actually identify. It would take more evidence to say which guarantee, if any, its tenants thought had failed.[10]

There is a wonderfully literal account of a later protection pact in a British parliamentary debate. In 1871 a speaker in the House of Lords read out evidence the trade unionist Robert Applegarth had given to a commission. Around the sixth of the month the returns reached him from the branches. He looked for towns where men wanted work and towns where work wanted men. He wrote to the secretaries, and the union paid the railway or steamboat fare when it sent a member to a place where a job was available. If a worker could already buy that ticket, a letter might do. For someone who had spent the last of his pay, it made all the difference whether the envelope contained the means to undertake the journey.[11]

Labor unions are mutual protection pacts in this concrete sense. Members can pool what none knows in advance that they will need. Dues pay for someone to read the returns and for a fare that puts a member in a position to earn and contribute again. The same debate described sick pay, burial benefits and pensions. It also recalled a difficulty unions had faced under British law. An association might have money in the bank and struggle to bring a civil suit to recover it. The 1824 repeal of earlier combination laws had loosened restrictions on organizing over wages and hours. Decades later legislators were still arguing over what an association could do with its own funds. A contribution, a recognized right to the common purse and an actual ticket are three things a traveler needs other people to connect.[9][11]

The weavers in Lyon had faced a related choice. Each could return to an individual bargain whenever another order came in. Together they had made a rate that could go into the room ahead of the next worker. In that change one can see what this book asks of dialectics. The people competing with one another for work are the very people whose agreement might give each of them room to refuse a bad price. A common rate can preserve the ability to sell one's own work while changing the terms of the sale. Saving enough dues to cover a fare is an especially plain case of quantity passing into quality. A little below the fare, the job is still a town away. Reach the fare, and someone can get there.

## Who can afford to wait?

Capitalism has a guarantee of its own. In a growing economy, a job that pays for what a household needs gives people a powerful reason to return to work and an employer a workforce capable of returning. A falling price can mean a warmer room or enough to eat before payday. My hypothesis is that capitalism can remain the dominant system as long as it keeps delivering jobs and affordable goods widely enough to renew that relationship. One has to ask whose wage and which prices. Cheap silk for a customer and forty francs in arrears for its maker can appear on the books of the same prosperous trade.[1][3]

Asking what happens when delivery fails makes the claim more demanding. A household can carry an unpaid worker, a shopkeeper can extend credit, a public fund can carry a firm. The fund that was supposed to help workshop heads had a claim of its own on Madame Vieux's earnings. Similarly, a lender relying on a house as collateral has reason to welcome a price rise that can put the house beyond the reach of the people who need shelter. Keeping a creditor solvent and keeping a weaver at work can make rival demands on the same return. Coercion can also keep people working long after they would choose to leave. An institution's power to survive and the quality of life it delivers then have to be accounted for separately.

The distinction matters to the claim about energy on which the book rests. In a coal mine one can follow the fuel from a newly drained seam back to a boiler that keeps the seam open. In the union example the fare bought access to a journey under steam. A person could reach work that their unaided effort had put too far away. Whether this enlarged the lasting productive capacity of the trade depends on what the work yielded and who got the return. A merchant who sold more silk could pay the people capable of making another order, or send the gain elsewhere. Gross sales tell us little about which happened. Greater energy access can favor a higher assembly only where the return helps regenerate the people and arrangements that make use of it. A lender can have an excellent year while a workshop closes its doors. To learn whether the wider organization has increased its rate of entropy production we would have to follow the physical flows. A set of accounts denominated in francs could agree to the last centime and leave that question quite open.

The Lyon tribunal had a remarkably clear view of one boundary. It could recognize Pommet's forty francs and ask her to wait. The ruling came with no account of who would put food on her table during the delay. The report lets us follow a decision to the point where someone else's reserve of food or cash would have to take over. That is where I would look for the next piece of silk.

There is one more person in that column. A workshop head named Condamine came to complain about his apprentice, young Bron. The boy grew so sleepy that his master could scarcely keep him awake for evening work. Bron's mother said she had warned Condamine. He denied it. The council released them from their agreement and awarded Condamine sixty francs in compensation.[1]

The mother could recognize what was happening to her son. The master could see what it was costing the workshop. The council could attach a price to ending the arrangement. The newspaper gives us no account of what the boy himself could tell about the moment his attention began to go. Yet a great deal depends on just that knowledge in anyone we trust to catch a broken thread. How do you come to notice, while there is still time to act, that the next thing to give way may be your own grip?
