# Chapter 9 — The System That Produces Its Tomorrow

Mademoiselle Pommet went before Lyon’s labor council to collect forty francs. Her employer, Madame Vieux, agreed she owed it. The council ordered the accounts settled. Then it asked Pommet to give the widow more time.[1]

In January 1841 the workers’ paper *L’Écho des ouvriers* reported the widow’s explanation. Her husband’s illness had exhausted her savings. He had since died, and she had already taken advances on most of the earnings from the work. A relief fund for workshop heads had retained what remained. Pommet’s wages came due when the fund already had a claim on the money. The newspaper gives no date for payment.[1]

Pommet had done the work and was now being asked to lend the workshop forty francs until the widow could pay. The report tells us what the shop owed. It gives no account of how Pommet lived while she waited.[1]

Elsewhere in the same column, two shawl looms stood idle for quite different reasons. A workshop head named Burgat had set them up for a merchant, Chinard, who had kept him waiting for materials. Chinard agreed to pay fifty centimes per thousand passes of the weft to make up for the delay. Then it was Burgat’s turn to keep Chinard waiting. He could find no one to work the looms. The merchant wanted to go back to the ordinary rate. The council reckoned that the delays on the two sides offset each other and awarded Burgat the promised rate on both kinds of shawl.[1]

In Burgat’s case, the first delay was for lack of yarn and the second for lack of a weaver. The looms were ready throughout. The industry depended on bringing materials and skilled workers together. The people before the council wanted a decision about who would pay when that failed.[1]

## Who can place the next order?

The sale of a shawl had to cover the yarn, the use of the loom and the labor of weaving it. Part of the payment also had to reach the household that fed and trained the weaver. When Pommet’s pay was delayed, the cost of keeping the workshop in business fell partly on her.[2][3]

Biologists use *autopoiesis* for a system whose activity helps produce the components that constitute it. The workshop offers an economic application. A sale can pay for the yarn and skilled time required for the next commission. That is the *closure* at issue, a return of resources to the activity that produced them. Burgat’s two idle looms showed what one missing delivery could do. A different question faced the city’s weavers. Could people who depended on merchants to set the terms of each new order gain some power to set those terms themselves?[1][2]

Marx called the renewal of production on the same scale *simple reproduction*. A worker spends wages on food and housing and returns to work for more wages. A proprietor sells the goods and can employ a worker again. In Lyon, making and selling silk could reproduce the relationship between employer and weaver long after a particular shawl had been sold.[3]

Niklas Luhmann studied how one communication makes another possible. Pommet stated her claim, Madame Vieux acknowledged it, and the council issued a ruling. A ruling carried authority because people recognized the council’s right to settle debts. Its decision established what Pommet could demand. She could still leave the building without her wages.[4]

Cybernetics connects these two explanations through feedback. The missing payment affected Pommet’s household. Her complaint brought that consequence to people authorized to act. Their ruling could affect production if it resulted in payment. We can follow the complaint through to a decision. The account stops before we know whether any money reached her.[1][4]

This is the material and Platonic character of the same economy. There is a quantity of money someone has to find. There is also an order in which claims on it will be heard and honored. The relief fund’s prior claim had real consequences for Pommet’s forty francs. The order lived in rules and practices that people could learn, enforce and sometimes change. Ten years before Pommet’s case, Lyon’s weavers had tried to change who got to make such a rule.[1]

## What a yard of silk is worth

In 1831 the silk workers of Lyon, the *canuts*, wanted to know their minimum pay before accepting an order. Two pieces of equal length could require very different amounts of work. A fine cloth could take far more crossings of the weft for each yard. Twenty-two commissioners calculated rates across eight divisions of weaving. Their units included a thousand warp threads and a thousand passes of the weft. The schedules allowed weavers and merchants to price the actual work required.[5]

According to the first issue of *L’Écho de la fabrique*, the merchants present at a meeting on October 21 said they had no authority to settle for their colleagues. The workers’ delegates replied that they had been authorized to negotiate only with people who could settle. The merchants went away and appointed representatives. Four days later the two groups met again. After four hours of debate they signed the tables. A crowd gathered in the prefecture courtyard to hear the prefect.[5]

Some merchants resisted. In November a stoppage became an insurrection and people died in the city. On December 7 Marshal Soult, the minister of war, declared the published schedules void. Parties to a dispute could still go before the competent judges. That left open the route by which, ten years later, Burgat would obtain a ruling over the price of work he had undertaken. The workers had sought something rather more valuable. They wanted to know what they could count on before they accepted the order and mounted the looms.[5]

The rates in Lyon had been measured, negotiated, signed and then revoked by a minister. A customer buying silk might see only a length of cloth and a price. Marx used *commodity fetishism* to describe how relations among people can appear as properties of the things they sell. Here we can identify the workers who calculated the rates and the authority that overturned them. Treating the price as a natural property of silk would conceal both.[3][5]

## Can a debt pay a debt?

On December 10, 1690, the General Court of Massachusetts Bay faced unpaid bills from a war. The colony owed people for goods and services and had too little cash to pay them. The court authorized a committee to issue up to seven thousand pounds in printed bills to creditors willing to accept them. Public receivers would take the bills at their stated values. Someone with a worn bill could exchange it for a new one of the same number and amount.[6]

The colony owed its suppliers. Taxpayers owed the colony. The court’s promise to accept the bills in payment of taxes meant a supplier could pass a bill to someone who had a tax debt. That gave each creditor a reason to accept the paper in the first place.[6][7]

One of the early bills survives in the Smithsonian. Its face reads twenty shillings. The museum thinks someone altered it from two shillings and sixpence. The change would have made the paper worth eight times as much at the tax office, provided the receiver accepted it.[6]

*Money comes from tax. Taxes come from war.* In Massachusetts the connection is visible in the order authorizing the bills. The colony needed goods for a war. Its power to levy taxes helped it procure those goods with paper. Charles Tilly traced comparable connections among war, taxation and protection in the formation of European states. An authority offering protection could itself be responsible for some of the danger. The Massachusetts court described its war as defensive. That was the colony’s account of what its spending was for.[6][7]

Massachusetts already had creditors, accounts and a unit called a pound. The 1690 order explains how the colony issued a new currency. Other money has other origins. When a commercial bank makes a loan, it can create a spendable deposit by crediting a customer’s account. In each case the practical questions are who can issue the claim and what someone else can do with it.[6][7]

## The price of protection

In 1020 Bishop Fulbert of Chartres received a request from William, duke of Aquitaine, to explain what an oath of fealty required. Fulbert drew up a list. A vassal had obligations concerning his lord’s safety, secrets, possessions and ability to act. He owed active counsel and aid. The lord, in turn, owed good faith to the vassal on the same points. At the end Fulbert excused the brevity of his letter. His city and church had burned and he was occupied in rebuilding. He drew comfort from the prospect of help from God and the duke. A bishop who needed his duke’s help had found a tactful occasion to remind him what he owed.[8]

This is the comparison I had in mind when I wrote that *feudalism was a labor union*. People rendered services in return for a promise of protection. There were important differences in who made the promise and who could enforce it. Fulbert was describing a lord and a vassal of unequal rank. The people who worked their land might have no right to protection against either. Union members pool resources with other wage earners. Fulbert’s own circumstances give the comparison a practical test. When his city burned, what help could he actually obtain from the duke?[8][9]

In England in 1349 the plague had killed so many workers that survivors could demand higher wages. The Crown sent sheriffs an ordinance requiring eligible workers to accept employment at customary rates. Lords had priority over their dependent tenants. Employers who offered more could also be punished. Individual landlords had reason to bid for scarce workers even as landlords collectively sought to hold wages down.[10]

In October 1453 Alice Chaucer divided the 387 arable acres of her demesne at Walsham in Suffolk into twenty-two parcels at fixed annual rents. The demesne had been the land farmed for the lord’s own use. Under the new arrangement each of twenty-two tenants decided how to farm a parcel and owed rent even after a poor harvest. Alice received the payments in place of directing the farming herself.[10]

At Walsham we can say what it meant for one form of feudal production to lose its *closure*. Under direct management the estate had to obtain labor and organize cultivation year after year. Under fixed rents it could keep its income while giving up those tasks. The grants record the change on this estate. They leave open why particular tenants accepted the arrangement and when other lordships changed.[10]

In an 1871 debate in the House of Lords, members heard evidence from the union secretary Robert Applegarth. Around the sixth of each month he received reports from the branches. Some towns had members looking for work. In others employers needed workers. Applegarth wrote to the branch secretaries. When the society sent a member to a job, it paid his railway or steamboat fare.[11]

The railway, the boat and the job already existed. Branch reports told the society where the workers were needed. Dues made the journey affordable to a member who was out of work. This is a specific service a union could provide as a mutual protection pact.[11]

Members who compete for work can sustain one another’s ability to refuse it on bad terms. The worker remains someone with labor to sell, and acting with others changes the terms on which he can sell it. That is the dialectical possibility the Lyon delegates saw in a table of rates. A common purse can also pay sickness or burial benefits and support people who will never personally return the contribution. In Britain, even after the repeal of the earlier combination laws in 1824, workers’ associations faced restrictions on enforcing claims to their funds. The 1871 debate reached the elementary question of whether a society could recover its own money through the courts. Giving a member a fare took contributors, a person reading the branch returns and a claim on a purse that other people were bound to respect.[9][11]

## When the guarantee comes due

Growing markets can keep people in work and put goods within reach of households that could never afford them before. I expect capitalism to remain dominant while it continues to deliver jobs and affordable goods widely enough. The gains can be distributed unevenly. A merchant can sell the silk, its buyer can afford the price, and the woman who wove it can still be owed forty francs.

In Pommet’s case, the council upheld the wage agreement and asked her to bear the delay. The workshop got time. The worker got an acknowledged claim. Both outcomes matter to an explanation of how the trade continued.[1]

Applegarth’s union shows how a change in organization can make a different use of energy possible. By pooling wages and information, the branches could pay for a steam journey when it would get someone to a job. A record of fares identifies who could travel. A claim that this increased the overall rate of entropy production would need further measurements of fuel, work and heat.[11]

There is one further case in the column that reported Pommet’s claim. A workshop head named Condamine complained that his apprentice, young Bron, grew so sleepy he could hardly keep him awake for evening work. Bron’s mother said she had warned Condamine. He denied it. The council released the parties from their agreement and awarded Condamine sixty francs in compensation.[1]

The reporter preserved what Bron’s mother and employer said about his fatigue. We have no account from Bron. The council could see the effect of lost sleep on his work. Whether he could tell when his own attention was failing is a different question, and one for which we do have firsthand accounts from other people.
