# Chapter 9 — The System That Produces Its Tomorrow

Mademoiselle Pommet went before Lyon’s labor council to collect forty francs. Madame Vieux, her employer, acknowledged the debt. The council ordered the women to settle their accounts and asked Pommet to give the widow more time to pay. The case appeared in the workers’ paper *L’Écho des ouvriers* in January 1841.[1]

Her husband’s illness had consumed Madame Vieux’s savings. She had already borrowed against most of what the workshop would earn. The relief fund for workshop heads had retained the rest. By the time Pommet asked to be paid, other people had claims on every franc.[1]

Each day Pommet agreed to wait would amount to another day’s credit from the weaver to the shop. The paper gives no date on which she was paid. This is where the question of who owned the pattern in Lyon becomes a question about who can afford to weave tomorrow. A trade can produce a valuable thing and still fail to put a living in the hands of the person whose work it needs again. To explain an institution’s endurance, we have to follow the return far enough to see whose participation it buys.[1]

Elsewhere in the same column, two shawl looms stood idle for quite different reasons. A workshop head named Burgat had set them up for a merchant, Chinard, who had kept him waiting for materials. Chinard agreed to pay fifty centimes per thousand passes of the weft to make up for the delay. Then it was Burgat’s turn to keep Chinard waiting. He could find no one to work the looms. The merchant wanted to go back to the ordinary rate. The council reckoned that the delays on the two sides offset each other and awarded Burgat the promised rate on both kinds of shawl.[1]

## Who can place the next order?

The sale of a shawl had to cover the yarn, the use of the loom and the labor of weaving it. Part of the payment also had to reach the household that fed and trained the weaver. When Pommet’s pay was delayed, the cost of keeping the workshop in business fell partly on her.[2][3]

With the proceeds of one sale, a merchant could buy yarn and a weaver could feed herself while she worked on the next piece. This is where I apply *autopoiesis* to a workshop. Some of what its people produce has to replace what it took to do the work. Economically, *closure* means the payment making its way back to the people who must start the next piece. Pommet’s forty francs were still outstanding.[1][2]

Marx called the renewal of production on the same scale *simple reproduction*. A worker spends wages on food and housing and returns to work for more wages. A proprietor sells the goods and can employ a worker again. In Lyon, making and selling silk could reproduce the relationship between employer and weaver long after a particular shawl had been sold.[3]

For Niklas Luhmann, a binding communication changes what can be decided next. After Madame Vieux acknowledged the debt and the council ruled, Pommet could demand payment of an established claim. The price of the work had been settled.[4]

A missed payment brought Pommet to the council. Its ruling could in turn alter who received the proceeds of a sale. Cybernetics calls this return of information *feedback*. Here it joins Marx’s argument to Luhmann’s. Knowing how much silk the workshop sold could never tell us which creditor would be paid first. Knowing that Pommet had won her claim could never tell us whether there was money to pay her. Together the two accounts explain how a rule enforced by people can redirect resources, and why resources can run out while a perfectly valid ruling stands.[1][3][4]

That is the sense in which an economy is jointly material and Platonic. The priority of claims was real enough to keep money from Pommet. It had that power because people administered the fund and acted on the ruling. Treating the order of claims as a natural property of forty francs would turn a human arrangement into something nobody could take responsibility for changing.[1][3][4]

## What a yard of silk is worth

Ten years earlier Lyon weavers had tried to fix a price before mounting their looms. A merchant ordered lengths of silk. On a fine piece, a thousand passes of the shuttle might add much less length than on a coarse one. Twenty-two commissioners divided the trade into eight branches and calculated rates. In some branches they counted warp threads by the thousand, then counted how many thousand times the weft would have to cross.[5]

At the October 21 meeting the merchants present could speak only for themselves. The workers’ delegates had authority to negotiate only with people who could bind the other side. The merchants left to appoint representatives. Four days later the two sides met again. After four hours they signed the tables, and the prefect went out to address the people waiting in the courtyard.[5]

Some merchants resisted. In November a stoppage became an insurrection and people died in the city. On December 7 Marshal Soult, the minister of war, declared the published schedules void. Parties to a dispute could still go before the competent judges. That left open the route by which, ten years later, Burgat would obtain a ruling over the price of work he had undertaken. The workers had sought something rather more valuable. They wanted to know what they could count on before they accepted the order and mounted the looms.[5]

The rates in Lyon had been measured, negotiated, signed and then revoked by a minister. A customer buying silk might see only a length of cloth and a price. Marx used *commodity fetishism* to describe how relations among people can appear as properties of the things they sell. Here we can identify the workers who calculated the rates and the authority that overturned them. Treating the price as a natural property of silk would conceal both.[3][5]

## Can a debt pay a debt?

On December 10, 1690, the General Court of Massachusetts Bay faced unpaid bills from a war. The colony owed people for goods and services and had too little cash to pay them. The court authorized a committee to issue up to seven thousand pounds in printed bills to creditors willing to accept them. Public receivers would take the bills at their stated values. Someone with a worn bill could exchange it for a new one of the same number and amount.[6]

Massachusetts owed its suppliers. Taxpayers owed Massachusetts. A supplier could spend a bill with a taxpayer, who could then take it to a public receiver. The court instructed the receiver to accept the printed amount.[6][7]

Anyone can print a promise. The court could do something a private printer could not. It could impose a liability and accept its own paper in settlement. That gave a taxpayer a reason to take a bill from someone who had supplied the army. The military expenditure explains why the colony issued the bills then. The route back to the tax office explains how they could pass into other hands.[6][7]

One surviving bill in the Smithsonian reads twenty shillings. The museum thinks it originally read two shillings and sixpence, one eighth as much. Someone stood to gain the difference if a receiver honored the altered figure.[6]

*Money comes from tax. Taxes come from war.* In Massachusetts the connection is visible in the order authorizing the bills. The colony needed goods for a war. Its power to levy taxes helped it procure those goods with paper. Charles Tilly traced comparable connections among war, taxation and protection in the formation of European states. An authority offering protection could itself be responsible for some of the danger. The Massachusetts court described its war as defensive. That was the colony’s account of what its spending was for.[6][7]

Massachusetts already had creditors, accounts and a unit called a pound. The 1690 order explains how the colony issued a new currency. Other money has other origins. When a commercial bank makes a loan, it can create a spendable deposit by crediting a customer’s account. In each case the practical questions are who can issue the claim and what someone else can do with it.[6][7]

## The price of protection

In 1020 William, duke of Aquitaine, asked Bishop Fulbert of Chartres what an oath of fealty required. A vassal owed counsel and aid, Fulbert wrote. He had to guard his lord’s safety, secrets and possessions. The lord owed his vassal good faith on the same points. Fulbert closed by explaining why his letter was short. His city and church had burned, rebuilding occupied him, and he looked for help from God and from the duke.[8]

This is the comparison I had in mind when I wrote that *feudalism was a labor union*. People rendered services in return for a promise of protection. There were important differences in who made the promise and who could enforce it. Fulbert was describing a lord and a vassal of unequal rank. The people who worked their land might have no right to protection against either. Union members pool resources with other wage earners. Fulbert’s own circumstances give the comparison a practical test. When his city burned, what help could he actually obtain from the duke?[8][9]

A guarantee of protection acquires material force in much the same way as the promise on a bill. Someone must be able to claim a service when it falls due. An oath may continue to be recited after the parties have found another way to secure their harvest. To recognize a change of system, it is worth looking for the transfer of that work and its risks.

In England in 1349 the plague had killed so many workers that survivors could demand higher wages. The Crown sent sheriffs an ordinance requiring eligible workers to accept employment at customary rates. Lords had priority over their dependent tenants. Employers who offered more could also be punished. Individual landlords had reason to bid for scarce workers even as landlords collectively sought to hold wages down.[10]

In October 1453 Alice Chaucer divided the 387 arable acres of her demesne at Walsham in Suffolk into twenty-two parcels at fixed annual rents. This was land previously farmed for the lord’s own use. Henceforth the tenants arranged the sowing and harvest. Each owed rent even in a poor year.[10]

Alice could draw income from the land after the estate ceased to direct the labor that produced it. That is the sense in which the old form of production lost its *closure* at Walsham. The grants record what changed hands, including the risk of a bad crop. They leave open which tenants welcomed the change.[10]

In 1871 the House of Lords heard how union secretary Robert Applegarth began work when his branch returns arrived around the sixth of each month. He looked for towns short of workers and towns where men were idle. Then he wrote to the secretaries where men were out of work. The union paid the railway or steamboat fare of a member it sent to a vacancy.[11]

A carpenter out of work could easily be too poor to travel. Dues collected in the weeks members earned wages bought the ticket that put a job within reach.[11]

Members who compete for work can sustain one another’s ability to refuse it on bad terms. The worker remains someone with labor to sell, and acting with others changes the terms on which he can sell it. That is the dialectical possibility the Lyon delegates saw in a table of rates. A common purse can also pay sickness or burial benefits and support people who will never personally return the contribution. In Britain, even after the repeal of the earlier combination laws in 1824, workers’ associations faced restrictions on enforcing claims to their funds. The 1871 debate reached the elementary question of whether a society could recover its own money through the courts. Giving a member a fare took contributors, a person reading the branch returns and a claim on a purse that other people were bound to respect.[9][11]

## When the guarantee comes due

My argument about capitalism rests on the same distinction. So long as growing markets deliver wages enough to live on and put goods within reach of households that could never buy them before, people have material reasons to keep taking jobs and buying from firms. That helps explain why I expect capitalism to remain dominant under those conditions. It also explains why a rising total of goods sold can overstate the strength of the arrangement. A merchant can sell more silk while the woman who wove it still waits for forty francs. Whose share is renewed matters as much to the next round of production as the size of the last.[1][3]

Applegarth could show that dues paid by working carpenters had bought other carpenters passage to a job. That is evidence of a return reaching the people whose labor the trade needed. An increase in energy extraction across the whole trade would be a further claim. Food and fuel have to reach workers under any system. The institutions determine whether they reach this worker soon enough for her to accept another order.[1][11]

In the column that reported Pommet’s claim, a workshop head named Condamine complained that his apprentice, Bron, grew so sleepy he could hardly keep him awake for evening work. Bron’s mother said she had warned him. Condamine denied it. The council released the parties from their agreement and awarded the employer sixty francs. The paper records no testimony from Bron.[1]
