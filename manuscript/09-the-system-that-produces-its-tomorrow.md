# Chapter 9 — The System That Produces Its Tomorrow

The trouble with Mademoiselle Pommet’s forty francs was that everyone agreed about them. She had done the work. Madame Vieux, in whose weaving shop she had done it, acknowledged the debt. Lyon’s labor council ordered the accounts settled. It also asked the woman who had earned the money to wait a little longer.[1]

In January 1841 a workers’ paper, *L’Écho des ouvriers*, reported what the widow Vieux had told the hearing. Her husband’s illness had exhausted her resources. He had since died. She had already drawn most of the earnings on the work in advance. What remained had been retained by a fund established to make loans to workshop heads in difficulty. Here was a fund intended for precisely such a household, and here, when Pommet asked for her pay, was the fund with a claim ahead of hers. We lose sight of the two women when the hearing ends. The paper records no payment date.[1]

Throughout the delay the workshop had the benefit of Pommet’s labor and Pommet was being asked to extend it credit. Who met her expenses during that interval is missing from an account exact enough to put a figure on what she was owed. The gap matters. An arrangement can keep a business solvent by shifting costs to a household whose accounts never come before the council.[1]

Elsewhere in the same column, two shawl looms stood idle for quite different reasons. A workshop head named Burgat had set them up for a merchant, Chinard, who had kept him waiting for materials. Chinard agreed to pay fifty centimes per thousand passes of the weft to make up for the delay. Then it was Burgat’s turn to keep Chinard waiting. He could find no one to work the looms. The merchant wanted to go back to the ordinary rate. The council reckoned that the delays on the two sides offset each other and awarded Burgat the promised rate on both kinds of shawl.[1]

Burgat’s looms stood ready through both delays. What was missing changed. First the merchant failed to furnish yarn, then the workshop failed to furnish a weaver. The council had to decide who would bear the cost of each interval. The cases supply a measure of productive organization that the finest surviving silk cannot. An industry persists to the extent that it can repeatedly arrange for materials and people to reach the same loom at the same time.[1]

## Who can place the next order?

A workshop ordinarily receives an order to make a thing that can leave the premises. Its continued existence depends on what comes back. Enough of the payment has to reach somebody who can replace the materials, keep up the looms and feed people between jobs. A skilled worker has spent years becoming someone whose time it is worth buying. A next generation needs some of those years set aside for it too. Widen the boundary to include the households where people eat and sleep and a town can be seen doing another kind of work while it makes shawls. It is producing the people and the arrangements that will let it make shawls again.[2][3]

Biologists use *autopoiesis* for a system whose activity helps produce the components that constitute it. The workshop offers an economic application. A sale can pay for the yarn and skilled time required for the next commission. That is the *closure* at issue, a return of resources to the activity that produced them. Burgat’s two idle looms showed what one missing delivery could do. A different question faced the city’s weavers. Could people who depended on merchants to set the terms of each new order gain some power to set those terms themselves?[1][2]

Marx asked what else comes back with the weaver. If a worker is paid enough to go home, eat and rest, the payment has restored her ability to work. It has ordinarily also left her with a reason to seek another payment from somebody able to hire her. On the other side of the transaction, the sale of the product can leave its proprietor in a position to place another order. The cloth may have gone to the far end of France. A relationship between two people in Lyon can have come through its journey in excellent health. Marx called the renewal of production at the same scale *simple reproduction*. When one considers the number of people it takes to keep it simple, it is hardly surprising that he gave it a chapter.[3]

Niklas Luhmann offers another way into the hearing. Follow the forty francs as far as they traveled that day. An amount entered in a claim was acknowledged by the widow and taken up in a ruling. Each communication made another possible. If a bystander repeated exactly the same words outside the building, the authority to settle the account would stay inside. A society reproduces distinctions of this kind every time people act upon a promise, an acknowledgment or a judgment. A weaver can go home with a perfectly recognizable judgment in her favor while somebody else still has the cash.[4]

Cybernetics gives Marx and Luhmann some common ground on which to meet. Pommet brings word that an expected payment has failed. The council can hear the complaint, decide something is due and direct an account to be settled. If its intervention gets food and time back to the worker, a message about a failure has helped repair the material business of weaving. If the news travels only as far as a newspaper reader, people may be considerably better informed while Pommet is still out forty francs. The sequence of decisions and the sequence of payments can part company at a point one can put a finger on.[1][4]

This is the material and Platonic character of the same economy. There is a quantity of money someone has to find. There is also an order in which claims on it will be heard and honored. The relief fund’s prior claim had real consequences for Pommet’s forty francs. The order lived in rules and practices that people could learn, enforce and sometimes change. Ten years before Pommet’s case, Lyon’s weavers had tried to change who got to make such a rule.[1]

## What a yard of silk is worth

Ten years earlier the silk workers of Lyon, the *canuts*, tried to settle a question before the cloth went on the loom. Every customer could see how long a piece was. Only people familiar with the weaving would know how many times the weft had to cross to produce a length of that particular cloth. Piecework by the yard could be a generous bargain on one fabric and a ruinous one on the next. The workshop heads wanted to be able to consult a schedule of minimum rates before taking an order. Twenty-two commissioners worked out the figures across eight divisions of weaving. They priced the work in units that included a thousand warp threads and a thousand passes of the weft. In a finished curtain one can see which way the light falls. The tables made something else visible. Someone had to do every crossing.[5]

The first issue of *L’Écho de la fabrique* gives the workers’ account of the negotiations. At the meeting of October 21, 1831, the merchants present explained that their colleagues had given them no mandate to agree. The workers’ delegates had a rather good answer. They had a mandate themselves, and its terms allowed them to negotiate only with other people authorized to settle. The merchants went away to choose representatives. On October 25 the two groups met again. After four hours of debate they signed the tables. A crowd was admitted to the prefecture courtyard to hear from the prefect. Someone arriving at the end could easily have supposed the matter settled.[5]

Some merchants resisted. In November a stoppage became an insurrection and people died in the city. On December 7 Marshal Soult, the minister of war, declared the published schedules void. Parties to a dispute could still go before the competent judges. That left open the route by which, ten years later, Burgat would obtain a ruling over the price of work he had undertaken. The workers had sought something rather more valuable. They wanted to know what they could count on before they accepted the order and mounted the looms.[5]

How much human activity does it take to produce a market price? In Lyon the answer included measuring, counting, electing, negotiating and, finally, an intervention by the minister of war. In a draper’s window almost all of this would be invisible. The customer would see the silk and its price side by side, almost as if the second were a property of the first. Marx had this quite ordinary feat of disappearance in mind when he wrote about commodity fetishism. An ideology can be false in a very practical way. It tells the customer that the price arose naturally from the properties of the goods. Take the customer back to the prefecture and the price acquires a history, down to the four hours it took the delegates to agree on it and the name of the man who struck it down.[3][5]

## Can a debt pay a debt?

Madame Vieux was hardly the first debtor to find herself in need of time. On December 10, 1690, the General Court of Massachusetts Bay addressed a similar embarrassment on a much larger scale. War had left the colony owing money for property and services, and the treasury lacked the money to settle promptly. The court empowered a committee to issue printed bills up to a total of seven thousand pounds to creditors willing to take them. Public receivers were to accept them at their stated values in payment. A worn bill could be exchanged for a fresh one of the same number and amount. The colony had thought to provide for the possibility that its promises would last longer than the paper it printed them on.[6]

The resupply of the treasury was written into the terms of the issue. The colony owed its suppliers. Taxpayers owed the colony. Its commitment to take a bill back in payment gave a holder a claim that could settle someone else’s tax debt. The terms explain why a creditor had reason to accept the paper and why it could travel farther than that creditor’s own purchases. Madame Vieux’s promise had none of this public power. The Massachusetts authorities could help create demand for their own debt because they could impose and collect obligations in the other direction.[6][7]

One of the colony’s early bills survives in the Smithsonian. It reads twenty shillings. The museum thinks someone raised the denomination from two shillings and sixpence. Here is a small tribute to the ingenuity of the whole arrangement. Someone evidently thought the paper worth altering. The public promise gave it a use. A cashier had another job to do. Had the colony really undertaken to accept this piece of paper for eight times as much tax?[6]

*Money comes from tax. Taxes come from war.* The proposition has some teeth when we follow an actual obligation. A government waging war must command provisions, transport and people’s time. A levy gives it a means of making the demand recur. If there is a particular currency it will accept in settlement, its subjects have reason to acquire what the government spends. Charles Tilly traced the making of European states through the connections among warfare, extraction and protection. Sometimes the authority offering protection helped create the very danger for which it wanted to be paid. The Massachusetts order describes a war of defense. People on the other side of that war had been given little reason to regard the colony as their protector.[6][7]

The Boston document kindly supplies a limit to the genealogy as well. The colony already had accounts, creditors and a unit called a pound. An order authorizing bills can tell us a great deal about a new money without telling us who first invented counting in pounds. And when a commercial bank now makes a loan and credits a deposit to a customer, a further spendable balance comes into existence without a soldier or a tax collector making an appearance at the counter. The questions are who can issue a claim, what gives somebody else reason to accept it, and what work its holder can command before it comes due. One can answer them differently in different economies.[6][7]

## The price of protection

In 1020 Bishop Fulbert of Chartres received a request from William, duke of Aquitaine, to explain what an oath of fealty required. Fulbert drew up a list. A vassal had obligations concerning his lord’s safety, secrets, possessions and ability to act. He owed active counsel and aid. The lord, in turn, owed good faith to the vassal on the same points. At the end Fulbert excused the brevity of his letter. His city and church had burned and he was occupied in rebuilding. He drew comfort from the prospect of help from God and the duke. A bishop who needed his duke’s help had found a tactful occasion to remind him what he owed.[8]

This was the sense in which it first struck me that *feudalism was a labor union*. Fulbert’s letter exposes the first limit of the comparison. The oath bound the duke and his vassal. The people who worked their lands had made no such mutual agreement. The people supplying the produce that supported a lord and his vassal might be entitled to very little protection against either. A modern union of wage earners pools claims and resources among people who can sell their labor. Feudal obligations joined people of unequal rank, sometimes under compulsion, with very different powers to exact what was due. What is worth carrying across the comparison is the day after the fire. Yesterday a person rendered a service. Whose door are they entitled to knock on today, and what happens if it stays shut?[8][9]

In 1349 the English Crown had reason to take a sudden interest in which doors a laborer could knock on. The pestilence had killed so many workers that survivors could command higher wages. An ordinance sent to the sheriffs required eligible workers to accept employment at customary rates and gave lords priority over their own dependent tenants. It also penalized employers who paid too much. A landlord who wanted the laborer on his neighbor’s estate might be making the very bid that landlords acting together wanted the king to prohibit. People often discover their common interest some distance from the place where each is trying to bring in his own harvest.[10]

A century later we can see one estate under quite different management. At Walsham in Suffolk in October 1453, Alice Chaucer divided the 387 arable acres of her demesne, the land held for the lord’s own use, into twenty-two parcels for twenty-two tenants at fixed annual rents. The estate had tried various arrangements in the intervening years. Now somebody had to see that each parcel was farmed well enough to meet a rent. Alice could collect twenty-two payments in place of organizing the farming of 387 acres. A tenant had acquired more control over production and a different problem if the harvest went badly. The earth that had yielded a crop for the lord could yield a crop for a tenant and a rent for the lady on exactly the same spot.[10]

This is what it would mean to say that a form of feudalism failed when its closure stopped. For a particular lordship to reproduce itself, people had to deliver labor under its terms, find the protection sufficient or the compulsion effective, and keep producing what made that authority worth maintaining. When a laborer could do better elsewhere, the old claim might require an entirely new investment in sheriffs. When land could more readily bring in a rent, the owner had a reason to give up an earlier claim while keeping an income. There was no morning when the whole of Europe woke to find the guarantee had expired. The Walsham grants let us see exactly which arrangement ceased to come back on one estate. They leave open what each tenant thought the former arrangement had promised.[10]

There is a particularly fine answer to the question of whom you could call on in a British parliamentary debate of 1871. The Lords heard evidence, read from an earlier commission, from the union secretary Robert Applegarth. Around the sixth of the month reports reached him from the branches. In some towns members wanted work. In others employers wanted hands. Applegarth wrote to the secretaries concerned. When the society sent a member to a job, it paid the railway or steamboat fare.[11]

The society’s payment of the fare turned Applegarth’s letters into a means of moving a worker. Railway and steamboat services already connected the towns. Reports from the branches identified where work and members were available. Dues purchased access to the transport at the moment those reports made a journey worthwhile. Enough small contributions had changed the kind of assistance one carpenter could offer another. A union was acting as a mutual protection pact with material means to deliver the protection it offered.[11]

Members who compete for work can sustain one another’s ability to refuse it on bad terms. The worker remains someone with labor to sell, and acting with others changes the terms on which he can sell it. That is the dialectical possibility the Lyon delegates saw in a table of rates. A common purse can also pay sickness or burial benefits and support people who will never personally return the contribution. In Britain, even after the repeal of the earlier combination laws in 1824, workers’ associations faced restrictions on enforcing claims to their funds. The 1871 debate reached the elementary question of whether a society could recover its own money through the courts. Giving a member a fare took contributors, a person reading the branch returns and a claim on a purse that other people were bound to respect.[9][11]

## When the guarantee comes due

Capitalism offers a bargain that is easy to underestimate from a comfortable room. A job opens, a wage arrives, and things that had been beyond a household’s reach get cheap enough to buy. Where growth keeps delivering jobs and affordable goods broadly enough, I would expect capitalism to retain a powerful hold. People have material reasons to go on making it work and employers receive a workforce able to return. The more interesting question is whose disappointment the system can absorb. A merchant can sell silk at a splendid price, a buyer can get an affordable dress, and a weaver can be forty francs out of pocket in the same town.

Pommet’s creditors are unknown. Her case establishes something we can say with confidence. A council could treat an agreement as binding while giving a workshop relief through delayed payment to its worker. The relief fund, the workshop and the household of the woman who had earned forty francs did not stand in the same relation to the guarantee. An account of why an institution survives has to include the people from whom its continued operation extracts time and credit.[1]

At Saltom, pumping raised the quantity of coal that could reach port. Applegarth’s society directed part of its members’ wages into dues that made a steam journey possible for the next member. Both relations joined an energy supply to an organization capable of putting it to use again. The union accounts add something essential. It mattered who had a claim on the return and who could direct it. An account in francs or in fares can establish what people could demand. To infer an overall rate of entropy production would also require accounts in fuel, work and heat.[11]

There is one further case in the column that reported Pommet’s claim. A workshop head named Condamine complained that his apprentice, young Bron, grew so sleepy he could hardly keep him awake for evening work. Bron’s mother said she had warned Condamine. He denied it. The council released the parties from their agreement and awarded Condamine sixty francs in compensation.[1]

The report preserves what Bron’s mother and employer said about his fatigue. His own account is missing. The council could dissolve an agreement and fix compensation without settling whether the boy himself could recognize the onset of sleep. This gap marks the boundary of a problem that occupied the first cyberneticists. Observing how an organism behaves and identifying what it knows about its own behavior require different evidence.
